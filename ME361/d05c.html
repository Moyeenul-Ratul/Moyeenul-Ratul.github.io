Sampling and Aliasing

A computer never sees your signal. It sees a list of numbers taken at fixed instants. Take them too rarely and a fast vibration can masquerade as a slow one, and nothing in the numbers will warn you.

Where we are

From a conditioned voltage to numbers in a computer

By now a sensor has turned a physical quantity into a voltage, and signal conditioning has amplified and filtered it. That voltage is analog: it exists at every instant and can take any value. The last step of a modern measurement chain is to hand it to a computer, which can only store a finite list of numbers. Parts A and B of this topic supplied the digital vocabulary for that step: bits and codes, gates, flip-flops and counters.

Two earlier ideas are needed here, so here they are again in one line each.

Fourier series: any periodic signal can be written as a sum of sines and cosines at the fundamental frequency \(f_1\) and its multiples \(2f_1, 3f_1, \dots\), each with its own amplitude.

Discrete Fourier transform (DFT): a calculation that takes a list of \(N\) equally spaced samples and returns the amplitude of each sine component at a set of discrete frequencies. The FFT is a fast algorithm for it, usually needing \(N = 2^M\) points.

Decision 1 · this part

How often?

The time between samples, \(\delta t\). Too long and frequencies are misread.

Decision 2 · this part

For how long?

The record length \(N\delta t\). It sets how finely the DFT can separate frequencies.

Decision 3 · part D

How finely?

The voltage step of the converter. Too coarse and small signals vanish.

Sampling concepts

A sampled record is a list of numbers taken every δt seconds

Analog signals are continuous in time and in amplitude. A discrete-time signal exists only at instants \(t = r\,\delta t\). A digital signal is discrete in both time and amplitude: each sample is also rounded to one of a finite set of levels (part D).

The sample time increment \(\delta t\) is the spacing between samples, and the sample rate is how many samples are taken per second:

Sample rate and the discrete record $$f_s=\frac{1}{\delta t}\qquad \{y(r\,\delta t)\},\quad r=0,1,\dots,N-1$$
\(f_s\)sample rate, Hz (samples per second) \(\delta t\)time between samples, s \(N\)number of samples in the record \(N\delta t\)total record length, s

How well those dots represent the curve depends on three things: the frequency content of the signal, the increment \(\delta t\), and the record length \(N\delta t\).

Sampling concepts

Sample too slowly and a sine changes its identity

(b) 100 Hz: ten samples per cycle. Join the dots and you see the 10 Hz sine clearly.

(c) 27 Hz: 2.7 samples per cycle. You can still count ten cycles in one second, so the frequency is right, but the samples rarely land on a peak and the apparent amplitude wobbles from cycle to cycle.

(d) 12 Hz: 1.2 samples per cycle. The dots trace a smooth wave with only two cycles per second. A 10 Hz signal has taken on the false identity of a 2 Hz signal.

A film camera takes 24 pictures a second. A walking person looks smooth on screen, but a fast-spinning car wheel can appear to crawl, stop or turn backwards. The camera did not malfunction; it simply took too few pictures to follow the motion.

The mechanism

Two different sines can produce exactly the same samples

Take a single sine of frequency \(f\), amplitude \(C\) and phase \(\phi\), and sample it every \(\delta t\). Reveal the argument one line at a time.

  1. Continuous signal: \(y(t) = C\sin(2\pi f t+\phi)\). Samples at \(t=r\,\delta t\): \(\;y_r = C\sin(2\pi f\,r\,\delta t+\phi)\).
  2. A sine does not change if you add whole turns: \(\sin x=\sin(x+2\pi q)\) for any integer \(q\).
  3. Choose \(q = m r\), with \(m\) any integer. Since \(r\) is an integer, so is \(q\): $$y_r = C\sin\!\Big[2\pi\Big(f+\frac{m}{\delta t}\Big)r\,\delta t+\phi\Big]$$
  4. So a sine at \(f + m f_s\) (\(m = \pm1, \pm2, \dots\)) gives the same list of numbers as the sine at \(f\). The samples cannot tell them apart.
  5. Negative \(m\) can give a negative frequency, and \(\sin(-x) = -\sin x\). A component at \(f - f_s < 0\) therefore looks like a sine at \(|f-f_s|\) turned upside down.

Check with the case from the previous slide, \(f = 10\) Hz, \(f_s=12\) Hz, so \(\delta t = 1/12\) s. With \(m = -1\), \(f - f_s = -2\) Hz:

Between two samples the 10 Hz sine turns through 10/12 of a cycle. That is the same as turning back by 2/12 of a cycle. The samples record only where the wave is, not how many whole turns it made in between.

The sampling theorem

The Nyquist frequency is a fold line

Every real frequency has a family of look-alikes \(|f + m f_s|\). Among them exactly one lies between 0 and \(f_s/2\), and that is the one your data will show. This half-rate is called the Nyquist frequency:

Nyquist (folding) frequency $$f_N=\frac{f_s}{2}=\frac{1}{2\,\delta t}$$

If the highest frequency in the signal, \(f_m\), is below \(f_N\), the lowest look-alike is the true one and nothing is lost. That gives the sampling theorem:

Sampling theorem $$f_s>2f_m\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \delta t<\frac{1}{2f_m}$$

Anything above \(f_N\) appears at a false alias frequency \(f_a\) below \(f_N\):

Alias frequency $$f_a=\big|\,f-n f_s\,\big|,\quad n=\text{the integer nearest to } f/f_s$$

A paper ruler folded like a fan. Look down through it and the marks 0.5, 1.5 and 2.5 sit on top of each other. Sampling looks at frequency the same way: from above, through the folds.

Worked example

A 10 Hz sine sampled at 12 Hz

A 10 Hz sine wave is sampled at 12 Hz. What is the highest frequency the sampled record can represent correctly, and at what frequency will the 10 Hz signal appear?

Known \(f=10\) Hz, \(f_s=12\) Hz. Find \(f_N\) and \(f_a\). Assumptions constant sample rate; a pure sine.

  1. Nyquist frequency: \(f_N = f_s/2 = 12/2 = 6\) Hz. No frequency above 6 Hz can be represented correctly.
  2. Compare: \(f/f_N = 10/6 = 1.67\). The signal is above \(f_N\), so it will fold.
  3. Folding diagram: 1.67 \(f_N\) lies on the first falling leg, directly above 0.33 \(f_N\). So \(f_a = 0.33\times 6 = 2\) Hz.
  4. Same answer by formula: the integer nearest to \(f/f_s = 0.83\) is \(n=1\), so \(f_a=|10-1\times12| = 2\) Hz.
  5. Because it lies on a falling leg, the 2 Hz alias is inverted: the samples equal those of \(-\sin(2\pi\,2\,t)\).

What this means: the record is identical to that of a genuine 2 Hz signal. No later calculation can recover the 10 Hz.

Worked example

Three different tones that collapse into one

A signal \(y(t)=A_1\sin 2\pi(25)t+A_2\sin 2\pi(75)t+A_3\sin 2\pi(125)t\) is sampled at 100 Hz. What frequencies and amplitudes appear in the sampled record?

Known \(f_s = 100\) Hz; \(f_1=25\), \(f_2=75\), \(f_3=125\) Hz. Find the alias of each component and the sampled series. Assumptions constant sample rate.

  1. \(f_N = 100/2 = 50\) Hz. In units of \(f_N\) the components sit at 0.5, 1.5 and 2.5 \(f_N\).
  2. On the folding diagram all three lie directly above 0.5 \(f_N\) = 25 Hz. By formula: \(|25-0|=25\), \(|75-100|=25\), \(|125-100|=25\) Hz.
  3. Phase: 0.5 \(f_N\) is on the bottom (in-phase) leg, 1.5 \(f_N\) on a falling leg (inverted), 2.5 \(f_N\) on a rising leg (in phase).
  4. Check the sign of the 75 Hz term at \(t = r/100\): \(\sin(1.5\pi r)=\sin(1.5\pi r-2\pi r)=-\sin(0.5\pi r)\). Inverted, as the diagram says.
  5. The record is therefore \(y_r = (A_1 - A_2 + A_3)\sin 2\pi(25)\,r\,\delta t\), with \(\delta t = 0.01\) s.

What this means: both the frequency content and the amplitude are wrong. With \(A_1=A_3=1\) and \(A_2=2\), the record would even be identically zero.

Sample the signal at several increasing rates while keeping the total record time fixed. Plot each record and compute its amplitude spectrum. If the waveform shape or the spectral peaks move as \(f_s\) rises, something was aliased at the lower rates. Settle on a rate above which nothing changes, and always fit an analog low-pass filter at \(f_N\).

“I will just sample fast and filter digitally afterwards.” If the sample rate is fast enough that nothing reaches \(f_N\), fine. But if anything above \(f_N\) is present when you sample, it has already folded onto real frequencies, and a digital filter cannot separate it from genuine content.

Animation · aliasing you can see

The wagon wheel that turns backwards

Camera and wheel4 identical spokesone marked spot

Each camera frame is one sample of the wheel's angle. Between frames the marked spot turns \(f/f_s\) of a revolution, but a single frame cannot show how many whole turns happened. Only the leftover fraction is visible, and a leftover of, say, 0.9 turn forwards looks exactly like 0.1 turn backwards.

The four identical spokes are worse: the pattern repeats every quarter turn, so it behaves like a signal at \(4f\) and aliases at a quarter of the wheel speed. Strobe lights used to measure shaft speed rely on this, which is why the shaft is given a single, non-symmetric mark.

Set the frame rate to 9 frames/s. Raise the wheel speed from 0 to 4.5 rev/s. The marked spot turns forward up to 4.5 rev/s (fN), then appears to reverse. At exactly 9 rev/s it would freeze. The spokes reverse much earlier, near 1.1 rev/s.

Photograph a clock once every 55 minutes. In each photo the minute hand stands 5 minutes earlier than in the one before, so the clock seems to run backwards.

Preventing aliasing

Filter before you sample; afterwards it is too late

An anti-alias filter is an analog low-pass filter placed before the sampler, with its cutoff near \(f_N\). It shrinks everything above \(f_N\) until any alias it could create is smaller than the converter can resolve. Choose \(f_s\) from the highest frequency you care about, then set the filter from \(f_s\).

The filter is a bouncer at the door. Once an impostor is inside the party (sampled), he looks like every other guest and no one can pick him out. The only place to stop him is at the entrance.

Waveform fidelity. \(f_s > 2f_m\) gets the frequencies right but not the shape: two or three points per cycle do not look like a sine. For a recognisable waveform a common rule is

Rule for waveform shape $$f_s\approx 5f_m \text{ to } 10f_m$$

The audio compact disc samples at 44.1 kHz so that 20 kHz, the limit of hearing, is not aliased. Near 22 kHz that is only two points per cycle, which drew criticism. The later Super Audio CD format samples at 2822.4 kHz, about 141 points per cycle at 20 kHz.

Amplitude ambiguity

The DFT sees only whole cycles: frequency resolution and leakage

The DFT treats your \(N\) samples as one period of a signal that repeats forever. The only sines that fit such a signal are those with a whole number of cycles in the record length \(N\delta t\). So the DFT reports amplitudes only at multiples of

DFT frequency resolution $$\delta f=\frac{1}{N\,\delta t}=\frac{f_s}{N}$$
\(\delta f\)spacing between DFT frequencies (bins), Hz \(N\delta t\)record length, s; longer record, finer resolution

If the signal frequency falls between two bins, the record holds a fractional number of cycles. Repeat it and there is a jump at every splice. Building that jump needs many extra sines, so the amplitude spreads into neighbouring bins and the main peak comes out too low. This is leakage, and the error in the peak is called amplitude ambiguity.

Spectral amplitudes come out exact when both conditions hold: (1) \(f_s > 2f_m\), and (2) the record holds a whole number of periods, \(N\delta t = m\,T_1\) with \(m\) an integer and \(T_1\) the fundamental period. Condition (1) fixes the largest \(\delta t\); condition (2) fixes \(N\delta t\), and hence \(N\).

A tape loop of music spliced at a random point clicks once per loop. The click is not in the music; it was made by the splice. Leakage is the DFT hearing that click.

For signals with no fundamental period (random or transient), make \(\delta f\) small with a long record; leakage then shrinks.

Worked example

Choosing fs and N for an exact spectrum

The signal \(y(t)=10\cos 628t\) (V, t in s) is to be sampled and its amplitude spectrum computed with an FFT of \(N=256\) points. Choose a sample rate that avoids aliasing, gives a recognisable waveform and produces no leakage.

Known \(\omega = 628\) rad/s, amplitude 10 V, \(N=256\). Find \(\delta t\), \(f_s\), \(\delta f\). Assumptions \(628\) rad/s is \(2\pi\times100\) Hz rounded (exactly 99.95 Hz); treat it as 100 Hz.

  1. Frequency and period: \(f = 628/2\pi \approx 100\) Hz, so \(T_1 = 0.01\) s and \(f_m = 100\) Hz.
  2. Sampling theorem: \(f_s>200\) Hz. For waveform shape aim for \(5f_m\)–\(10f_m\), i.e. 500–1000 Hz or more.
  3. No leakage: \(N\delta t = m T_1\). With \(N=256\), choose \(m=8\) cycles: \(N\delta t = 8(0.01)=0.08\) s.
  4. \(\delta t = 0.08/256 = 0.3125\) ms, so \(f_s = 1/\delta t = 3200\) Hz: well above 1000 Hz, so shape is fine too.
  5. Resolution: \(\delta f = 1/0.08 = 12.5\) Hz. 100 Hz is exactly bin 8, and the DFT returns amplitude 10.00 V with no leakage.

What this means: pick the record length first from whole periods, then let \(N\) fix \(\delta t\). Sampling faster with the same \(N\) (for example \(\delta t=0.1\) ms) would shorten the record to 2.56 periods and spoil the spectrum.

Worked example

A record that suits several tones at once

A signal \(y(t) = 4\sin 8\pi t + 2\sin 20\pi t + 3\sin 42\pi t\) (V) is to be sampled with \(N = 2^M\) points so that its spectrum shows all three components with minimal leakage. Choose \(f_s\) and \(N\).

Known the three angular frequencies. Find \(f_s\), \(N\), \(\delta f\), \(f_N\). Assumptions \(N\) must be a power of 2 for the FFT.

  1. Frequencies: \(8\pi/2\pi = 4\) Hz, \(20\pi/2\pi=10\) Hz, \(42\pi/2\pi=21\) Hz. So \(f_m = 21\) Hz.
  2. Each component must fit a whole number of cycles in \(N\delta t\). A record of 1 s holds 4, 10 and 21 cycles. Any whole number of seconds works, so \(\delta f = 1/(N\delta t)\) must be 1 Hz or 1/2, 1/3 … Hz.
  3. Sample rate: at least \(2f_m = 42\) Hz; for shape, \(5f_m\)–\(10f_m\) = 105–210 Hz.
  4. Take \(N\delta t = 1\) s and \(N = 128\): \(\delta t = 1/128\) s, \(f_s = 128\) Hz (between 105 and 210 Hz). Then \(f_N = 64\) Hz and \(\delta f = 1\) Hz.
  5. Result: the DFT shows exactly 4.00 V at 4 Hz, 2.00 V at 10 Hz and 3.00 V at 21 Hz, and zero elsewhere. An anti-alias filter with cutoff near 64 Hz protects the record from any noise above \(f_N\).

What this means: for several tones, make the record a common multiple of all the periods (here 1 s), then choose \(N\) to give a suitable \(f_s\).

Why can a digital filter, however sharp, not do the job of the analog anti-alias filter?

Because after sampling, a component above \(f_N\) already sits at its alias frequency, mixed with any genuine content there. The numbers carry no label saying which part is false, so no calculation can remove it without removing real data too.
