Analog transmission

Send a voltage or send a current over a long cable?

(a) Voltage signalling. The receiver sees a divider and the pickup in series: \(V_{in}=V_s\,R_{in}/(R_{in}+R_{out}+R_w)+e_n\). Both terms are errors. With 600 m of 0.5 mm² copper (\(R_w=41\) Ω) into a 10 kΩ input, a 10 V signal arrives 0.41 % low, and every millivolt of \(e_n\) adds straight to the reading.

(b) Current signalling. A current source forces a set current whatever the resistance in its path, because its output resistance \(R_{out}\) is very large (megohms). In a single series loop the same current flows through every element, so the receiver reads \(V=IR_s\) with no dependence on \(R_w\). The pickup \(e_n\) can only change the current by \(e_n/R_{out}\): 20 mV over 1 MΩ is 20 nA, about 0.0001 % of a 16 mA span (illustrative values). Interference that injects a current instead develops only a small voltage across the low 250 Ω receiver.

A voltage signal is like judging a pump by the pressure at the end of a long, leaky pipe: friction and leaks change what arrives. A current signal is like a metering pump that delivers a fixed flow: a longer pipe only makes the pump push harder, and the flowmeter at the end still reads the true flow, as long as the pump has enough pressure in reserve.

That last condition, enough "pressure in reserve", is the loop's voltage budget, worked out on slide 5.

The 4–20 mA standard

Why the scale starts at 4 mA and not at zero

Loop current4 mA8 mA12 mA16 mA20 mA
Across 250 Ω1 V2 V3 V4 V5 V

A transmitter is the electronics at the sensor that converts the measurement into the standard signal. Its calibrated range (for example 0–10 bar) is mapped linearly onto 4–20 mA. Three reasons for the 4 mA floor:

  1. Live zero. If 0 bar gave 0 mA, a cut wire, a blown fuse and a true zero would all read the same. With a live zero, a current near 0 mA can only mean a fault. Common practice (the NAMUR NE 43 recommendation) treats 3.8–20.5 mA as a valid signal and ≤ 3.6 mA or ≥ 21 mA as a failure, so a transmitter can also report its own fault by driving the current outside the band.
  2. Loop power. A transmitter can take the 3–3.5 mA its electronics need from the loop itself, so two wires carry both power and signal.
  3. History. Pneumatic instruments used 3–15 psi (a 20 % live zero). 4–20 mA keeps the same 1 : 5 ratio and the same 20 % offset.

The 4–20 mA loop · voltage budget

A two-wire loop must leave the transmitter enough voltage

In a loop-powered (two-wire) transmitter the same two wires carry power and signal: the transmitter acts as a regulated current sink that lets exactly 4–20 mA through. To regulate it needs a minimum voltage across its terminals, \(V_{min}\) (often called the lift-off voltage, typically 10–12 V). Four-wire transmitters have a separate power supply and drive the loop themselves.

  1. One series loop, so Kirchhoff's voltage law gives \(V_s=V_{tx}+I(R_w+R_s)\).
  2. The transmitter regulates only while \(V_{tx}\ge V_{min}\), and the largest drop occurs at the largest current \(I_{max}\).
  3. So \(V_s-I_{max}(R_w+R_s)\ge V_{min}\), which gives the largest total load resistance below.
Loop budget: maximum load resistance $$R_{L,max}=\frac{V_s-V_{min}}{I_{max}},\qquad R_w+R_s+R_{other}\le R_{L,max}$$

A pressure transmitter that needs at least 12 V across its terminals is 600 m from the control room. The loop uses a two-core copper cable with 0.5 mm² cores (\(\rho=1.72\times10^{-8}\) Ω·m), a 24 V DC supply and a 250 Ω sense resistor at the DAQ input. Check the loop, find the voltage range at the DAQ, and find the longest cable this design allows.

Known \(V_s=24\) V, \(V_{min}=12\) V, \(L=600\) m, \(A=0.5\) mm², \(R_s=250\) Ω. Find \(R_w\), drops, \(V_{tx}\), \(L_{max}\). Assumptions design at \(I_{max}=20\) mA; supply at nominal voltage.

  1. Current flows out and back, so the wire length is \(2L=1200\) m and \(R_w=\rho\,2L/A\) \(=1.72\times10^{-8}\times1200\,/\,(0.5\times10^{-6})\) \(=41.3\) Ω.
  2. At 20 mA: wire drop \(0.020\times41.3=0.83\) V, sense drop \(0.020\times250=5.00\) V, so \(V_{tx}=24-0.83-5.00=18.2\) V \(\ge12\) V. The loop works.
  3. At 4 mA: drops 0.17 V and 1.00 V, \(V_{tx}=22.8\) V. The DAQ sees 1.00–5.00 V.
  4. \(R_{L,max}=(24-12)/0.020=600\) Ω. The resistor uses 250 Ω, so the cable may have up to 350 Ω.
  5. \(2L_{max}=350\times0.5\times10^{-6}/1.72\times10^{-8}=10\,170\) m, so \(L_{max}\approx5.1\) km.
  6. If the transmitter must also signal a high alarm at 22 mA: \(R_{L,max}=12/0.022=545\) Ω and \(L_{max}\approx4.3\) km.

What this means: the 250 Ω resistor, not the cable, uses most of the budget. Long cables are rarely the problem; extra series devices (indicators, isolators, safety barriers) and a low supply are.

Simulator · 4–20 mA loop budget

Watch where the supply voltage goes

Two-wire loop, live voltage dropssense resistorcableleft for transmitter

The model: the transmitter tries to pass \(I=4+16\,P/100\) mA for process value \(P\) (it goes above 20 mA if \(P\) exceeds 100 %). If the supply cannot provide that current while keeping \(V_{min}\) across the transmitter, the current stops at \((V_s-V_{min})/(R_w+R_s)\). The DAQ then reads a wrong, clipped value without any warning, which is the dangerous failure of an under-powered loop.

Start at the worked example (24 V, 12 V, 600 m, 0.5 mm², 250 Ω, 100 %): 18.2 V is left for the transmitter. Drag the cable to 10 km: the green part shrinks and turns red near 5.1 km, and the reading clips. Return to 600 m and choose 500 Ω: the loop now starves above about 90 %. Finally set the supply to 18 V with 250 Ω: the loop still works, but 110 % (22 mA) is clipped, so a high alarm would never arrive.

A starved loop does not read zero; it reads a believable but low value near full scale. Check the budget at the highest current the transmitter can send, including its alarm current, and at the lowest supply voltage.

The 4–20 mA loop · scaling

From milliamps (or counts) back to engineering units

The transmitter maps its calibrated range \(x_{lo}\) to \(x_{hi}\) linearly onto 4–20 mA, so the current is

$$I=4\ \text{mA}+16\ \text{mA}\times\frac{x-x_{lo}}{x_{hi}-x_{lo}}$$

Solving for \(x\) gives the scaling the DAQ software applies. With the 250 Ω resistor, \(V=IR_s\) runs from 1 to 5 V, a span of 4 V:

Scaling a 4–20 mA signal $$x=x_{lo}+(x_{hi}-x_{lo})\,\frac{I-4\ \text{mA}}{16\ \text{mA}}=x_{lo}+(x_{hi}-x_{lo})\,\frac{V-1\ \text{V}}{4\ \text{V}}$$

A 0–10 bar transmitter sends 9.6 mA. What is the pressure?

A temperature transmitter is ranged −50 to 150 °C. Its loop current passes through a 250 Ω resistor into a 12-bit converter with a 0–5 V range, which returns the code 2867. Find the temperature, the temperature resolution, and the code below which the software should declare a fault.

Known \(x_{lo}=-50\) °C, \(x_{hi}=150\) °C, \(R_s=250\) Ω, \(M=12\), \(E_{FSR}=5\) V, code 2867. Find \(T\), \(\Delta T\) per code, fault code. Assumptions ideal resistor and converter; fault below 3.6 mA.

  1. Converter step \(Q=5/2^{12}=1.221\) mV, so \(V=2867\times1.221\ \text{mV}=3.500\) V.
  2. Current \(I=V/R_s=3.500/250=14.00\) mA.
  3. \(T=-50+200\times(14.00-4)/16=-50+125.0=75.0\) °C.
  4. Resolution: one code is \(1.221\ \text{mV}/250\ \Omega=4.88\ \mu\)A, which is \(200\ ^\circ\text{C}\times4.88\ \mu\text{A}/16\ \text{mA}=0.061\) °C.
  5. Fault limit: 3.6 mA gives \(0.900\) V, code \(0.900/1.221\ \text{mV}=737\). Any code below 737 means a broken or unpowered loop.

What this means: the 0–1 V part of the converter range is never used by a healthy loop, so only about four-fifths of the codes (3277 of 4096) carry the measurement.

The 4–20 mA loop · digital on top

HART adds digital data on the same two wires

HART (Highway Addressable Remote Transducer) superimposes a small sinusoid of about ±0.5 mA on the analog current. It uses frequency-shift keying (FSK): 1200 Hz means a 1 and 2200 Hz means a 0, at 1200 bit/s. Over each bit the sinusoid averages to zero, and the receiver's analog input responds far too slowly to follow 1–2 kHz, so the 4–20 mA reading is unaffected.

Over HART a handheld communicator or the control system can read the transmitter's tag, range, diagnostics and extra measured variables, and re-range it, without disturbing the analog signal. The FSK voltage is developed across the loop resistance, so HART needs a minimum loop resistance of about 230 Ω, one more reason for the 250 Ω standard.

Fully digital fieldbuses carry many variables on one cable, but 4–20 mA remains the most widely used analog field signal.

Isolation

Isolation breaks the wire path so ground differences cannot enter

An isolation barrier is a bank teller's window: the message (the signal) is passed through the slot, but there is no doorway, so whatever is going on in the queue (the field ground) cannot walk into the vault (the DAQ).

A thermocouple on a motor frame sits 50 V (50 Hz) above the DAQ ground. An isolation amplifier with 120 dB isolation-mode rejection is used. How much 50 Hz appears at its output, referred to the input?

120 dB is a factor of \(10^{120/20}=10^6\), so \(50\ \text{V}/10^6=50\ \mu\)V, small next to a thermocouple's roughly 40 µV/°C output (about 1 °C, and the digital filters later in this part remove most of it). Wired directly, the same 50 V would be five times beyond a ±10 V common-mode range.

A differential input rejects a common-mode voltage only within its rated range, usually about ±10 V. A sensor glued to a motor frame, a busbar or a patient can sit tens or hundreds of volts away from the DAQ ground. Wiring it directly either saturates the amplifier, drives a ground-loop current through the signal leads, or damages the equipment.

Galvanic isolation means there is no conducting path between two circuits; the information crosses by a magnetic field (transformer), an electric field (small capacitor) or light (optocoupler).

An isolation amplifier (a) has an input side with its own floating power supply and an output side referenced to the DAQ ground. The signal crosses the barrier on a modulated carrier and is demodulated on the other side. The ground difference then appears across the barrier, which is rated to withstand it (often 1500 V or more), instead of across the signal. Its isolation-mode rejection says how much of that voltage still leaks into the output.

An optocoupler (b) does the same for on/off signals: an LED driven by the field circuit shines on a phototransistor in the logic circuit. PLC digital inputs and DAQ digital lines are usually opto-isolated. For 4–20 mA loops, loop isolators pass the current across a barrier.

Sigma-delta converters · principle

A 1-bit converter, run very fast, whose bit density tracks the input

First-order modulatorinputword averagebits ±Vintegrator (dashed)

Set a constant 0.5 and replay: three bits in four are 1 and the staircase settles on 0.5. Try 0.05: the pattern is nearly 1010…, with an occasional extra 1. Switch to "slow sine" and change the bits per word from 8 to 32: more bits per word give finer steps but fewer, later words. That is the speed-for-resolution trade.

A sigma-delta converter (also called delta-sigma) is the modulator drawn here followed by a digital filter. At every clock tick the loop subtracts the feedback (\(+V\) after a 1, \(-V\) after a 0) from the input, adds the difference to the integrator, and the comparator outputs 1 if the integrator is positive. If the input is higher than the average feedback, the integrator climbs and more 1s are produced, so the feedback is pulled up to match.

  1. After \(N\) clocks the integrator holds \(u_N=\sum_{k}(V_{in}-v_k)\), with \(v_k=\pm V\).
  2. The feedback keeps \(u_N\) bounded (within about \(\pm2V\)), so dividing by \(N\): \(V_{in}-\frac1N\sum v_k\to0\).
  3. With \(n_1\) ones: \(\frac{V(n_1-(N-n_1))}{N}=V_{in}\), so \(\dfrac{n_1}{N}=\dfrac12\Big(1+\dfrac{V_{in}}{V}\Big)\).

So \(V_{in}=0.5V\) gives 75 % ones, \(0\) gives half, \(-V\) none; counting the 1s over 4096 clocks gives a 12-bit result.

Oversampling. A quantization error spread evenly over \(\pm Q/2\) has mean square \(Q^2/12\), and that noise power spreads evenly from 0 to \(f_s/2\). If the signal only occupies 0 to \(f_B\), the oversampling ratio is \(\text{OSR}=f_s/2f_B\). A digital low-pass filter that keeps only 0 to \(f_B\) keeps only \(1/\text{OSR}\) of the noise: 3 dB, or half a bit, per doubling.

Noise shaping. Inside the loop the integrator makes the quantization error appear differenced: the output is the input plus \(e_n-e_{n-1}\). Differencing is a high-pass filter of magnitude \(2\sin(\pi f/f_s)\), so the noise is pushed up towards \(f_s/2\), where the digital filter removes it. For a first-order loop the in-band noise falls as \(\text{OSR}^{-3}\): 9 dB, 1.5 bits, per doubling. At OSR = 64 a 1-bit quantizer ideally gives about 4 bits by plain oversampling, 9 bits with first-order and 14 bits with second-order shaping.

Decimation. The filtered stream is then kept only once every OSR clocks, giving \(M\)-bit words at the rate \(2f_B\). The simplest decimation filter is the counter of the previous slide, a moving average; set to a 50 Hz or 10 Hz output rate it also notches out mains hum (next slide).

The price is delay and poor multiplexing (table). Sigma-delta suits slow, high-resolution channels (load cells, thermocouples) and sound and vibration with one converter per channel.

Digital filtering · moving average

The moving average: a low-pass filter with perfect notches

A digital filter is a calculation on the stored samples \(x_n\), taken every \(\Delta t\). The moving average of \(N\) samples outputs the mean of the latest \(N\):

$$y_n=\frac1N\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}x_{n-k},\qquad\text{window }T=N\Delta t$$

What does it do to a sine of frequency \(f\)? Derive it:

  1. Write the sine as a complex exponential, \(x_n=e^{j\theta n}\) with \(\theta=2\pi f\Delta t\) (the real part is the actual sine).
  2. Then \(y_n=x_n\cdot\frac1N\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{-j\theta k}\): the output is the input times a complex gain \(H\).
  3. Geometric series: \(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}r^k=\frac{1-r^N}{1-r}\) with \(r=e^{-j\theta}\).
  4. Since \(|1-e^{-j\phi}|=2|\sin(\phi/2)|\), the magnitude is \(|H|=\dfrac{|\sin(N\theta/2)|}{N|\sin(\theta/2)|}\); put back \(\theta\) and \(N\Delta t=T\).
  5. The phase is \(-\theta(N-1)/2\), proportional to \(f\): every frequency is delayed by the same time \((N-1)\Delta t/2\).
Moving-average magnitude ratio $$|H(f)|=\frac{|\sin(\pi f T)|}{N\,|\sin(\pi f\Delta t)|}$$ $$|H|=0\ \text{at}\ f=\frac{m}{T},\quad m=1,2,\dots$$
moving average, N = 20, Δt = 1 ms (T = 20 ms)first-order low-pass, fc = 5 Hz (next slide)

A sine averaged over a whole number of its own periods gives exactly zero: the positive and negative halves cancel. So every frequency whose period fits a whole number of times into the window \(T\) is removed completely: \(f=1/T,\ 2/T,\dots\) A 20 ms window removes 50 Hz mains hum and all its harmonics.

Judging a road's slope while your car bounces on a regular wavy surface: average your height over exactly one wave length (or two, or three) and the bouncing cancels, leaving only the slope. Average over one and a half waves and part of a bump is left over.

Digital filtering · exponential smoothing

A first-order recursive filter is a digital RC circuit

An RC low-pass filter obeys \(\tau\,dy/dt+y=x\) with time constant \(\tau=RC\) and cutoff \(f_c=1/(2\pi\tau)\). Turn it into a calculation on samples:

  1. Replace the derivative by the backward difference over one sample: \(dy/dt\approx(y_n-y_{n-1})/\Delta t\).
  2. \(\tau(y_n-y_{n-1})/\Delta t+y_n=x_n\), so \(y_n(\tau+\Delta t)=\tau\,y_{n-1}+\Delta t\,x_n\).
  3. Divide by \(\tau+\Delta t\) and rearrange: \(y_n=y_{n-1}+\alpha(x_n-y_{n-1})\) with \(\alpha=\Delta t/(\tau+\Delta t)\).
  4. Matching the RC step response exactly at the sample instants gives instead \(\alpha=1-e^{-\Delta t/\tau}\); the two agree closely when \(\Delta t\ll\tau\).
First-order IIR (exponential smoothing) $$y_n=y_{n-1}+\alpha\,(x_n-y_{n-1})$$ $$\alpha=\frac{\Delta t}{\tau+\Delta t}\approx1-e^{-\Delta t/\tau},\qquad \tau=\frac{1}{2\pi f_c}$$
\(\alpha\)smoothing factor, 0 to 1: each new sample moves the output a fraction \(\alpha\) of the way towards it \(\Delta t\)sampling interval, s \(\tau,\ f_c\)equivalent RC time constant (s) and cutoff frequency (Hz)

Data are sampled at 1 kHz. Design an exponential smoothing filter equivalent to an RC low-pass with \(f_c=5\) Hz. How much does it attenuate 50 Hz hum, and how long does it take to follow a step?

  1. \(\tau=1/(2\pi\times5)=31.8\) ms; \(\Delta t=1\) ms.
  2. \(\alpha=1/(31.8+1)=0.0305\) (exact-step version \(1-e^{-1/31.8}=0.0309\)).
  3. At 50 Hz the gain is about \(1/\sqrt{1+(50/5)^2}=0.10\): hum is reduced tenfold, not removed.
  4. After \(n\) samples a step has reached \(1-(1-\alpha)^n\); 95 % needs \(n=\ln0.05/\ln(1-\alpha)=97\) samples, about \(3\tau\).

What this means: one stored number and one multiply per sample give a smooth trend, but only a gentle, first-order cut.

The output depends on its own previous value, so every past input still has a weight, \(\alpha(1-\alpha)^k\) for the sample \(k\) steps back, that decays but never reaches zero. This is an infinite impulse response (IIR) filter.

Exponential smoothing is how a sensible person updates an opinion: each new piece of news moves the view a small fraction \(\alpha\) of the way towards it. Old news is never forgotten outright, it just fades.

Digital filtering · choosing

FIR or IIR, median filters, and zero-phase filtering

A finite impulse response (FIR) filter computes each output from a finite number of recent inputs, each times a weight. A single spike in affects only \(N\) outputs. FIR filters are always stable and can have exactly linear phase (a pure delay, as for the moving average), but a sharp response needs many weights. An IIR filter reuses past outputs, so a few coefficients give a strong effect, but its phase shift varies with frequency, and a badly designed one can become unstable.

Use zero-phase filtering for analysis only. In a control loop or an alarm, the "future" samples do not exist yet, and the delay of a real-time filter is part of the loop's dynamics.

A median filter replaces each sample by the middle value of the sorted window. It is not linear, so it has no frequency response, but it is the right tool for isolated spikes. For the window 2.0, 2.1, 9.7, 2.2, 2.1 the median is 2.1, while the mean is 3.62: the average spreads the spike over five outputs, the median ignores it. It also keeps steps sharp, because at an edge most of the window is on one side or the other.

Real time or offline. A filter that runs while data arrive can only use past samples, so it delays the signal. When the whole record is already stored, it can use "future" samples too. A centred moving average is symmetric and has no delay. Any filter can be made zero-phase by running it forward through the record and then backward: the phase shifts cancel and the magnitude is applied twice, \(|H|^2\).

Digital filtering · the limit

A digital filter cannot replace the analog anti-alias filter

980 Hz vibration (before sampling)20 Hz sinesamples at 1 kHz

A sigma-delta converter seems to break this rule, but it does not. Its modulator samples at MHz rates, so a simple analog RC filter is enough to remove content near that rate. The sharp digital filter acts on the fast bitstream before decimation, while the unwanted components are still at their true frequencies. Only then is the rate reduced. An analog filter is still there; it can just be gentle.

A digital filter works only on the samples. Once a component above \(f_N\) has been sampled, its samples are identical to those of its alias. Sampled at 1 kHz, a 980 Hz pump vibration and a 20 Hz sine give exactly the same numbers, \(\cos(2\pi\cdot980\,n\Delta t)=\cos(2\pi\cdot20\,n\Delta t)\) because \(980=1000-20\). No calculation can tell which one was there; the 20 Hz alias passes every digital low-pass filter that passes real 20 Hz signals.

The information that distinguishes them, the waveform between the samples, is only available before sampling. So the low-pass filter that removes content above \(f_N\) must act on the analog signal, ahead of the sample-and-hold.
