Where we are

From one converter to a complete acquisition system

Part C set the timing: sample faster than \(2f_m\), filter out everything above the Nyquist frequency \(f_N=f_s/2\) before sampling, and choose the record length for the frequency resolution you need.

Part D set the size of the step. An \(M\)-bit converter spreads its full-scale span \(E_{FSR}\) over \(2^M\) codes, so its resolution is \(Q=E_{FSR}/2^M\) and the quantization error is \(\pm Q/2\). A small signal therefore needs gain before conversion. The common successive-approximation converter needs \(M\) clock ticks and a steady input during them; integrating converters are slow but reject noise; flash converters are fastest.

This part assembles these pieces into one system, then asks two practical questions: how to connect a sensor so that ground differences and noise do not enter the reading, and how the finished numbers travel to a computer.

Data-acquisition systems

A data-acquisition system quantifies and stores data

Data-acquisition system (DAS): the part of a measurement system that turns the transducer's analog signal into numbers and stores them. It sits between the transducer and the data reduction.

An engineer who reads a pressure gauge, decides "3.2 bar" and writes it in a logbook performs every task of a DAS: sampling (choosing when to look), quantization (reading to the nearest mark) and storage (the logbook). An automated DAS does the same with electronics, thousands of times a second and on many channels.

Automated systems come in three common forms:

  • a dedicated microprocessor or programmable logic controller (PLC), which measures, stores and controls a process without a PC;
  • a PC-based system: a plug-in data-acquisition board, an external module on USB, or a wireless module, with the PC providing storage, display and network access;
  • a stand-alone instrument (digital voltmeter, oscilloscope) that talks to a computer over a communication bus.

A DAS is a court stenographer. The speakers (sensors) talk continuously; the stenographer listens to each in turn, writes down what was said in a fixed shorthand (binary words), and hands over a transcript (the data file). Mishear, fall behind, or confuse two speakers, and the transcript is wrong forever.

Data-acquisition systems · architecture

Follow one reading from the sensor to the computer

  1. Sensor: produces a small analog voltage or current.
  2. Conditioning, one per channel: amplifies it and passes it through the analog anti-alias low-pass filter.
  3. Multiplexer (MUX): a set of electronic switches that connects one channel at a time to the single converter.
  4. Sample-and-hold (S/H): on command, freezes the voltage of the selected channel.
  5. A/D converter: converts the held voltage into an \(M\)-bit word while the S/H holds it.
  6. Buffer and bus: memory that holds the words until the computer collects them for display, storage or control outputs.
  7. Controller and clock: set the sample instants, select the channel and gain, command the hold and start each conversion. Then the MUX steps to the next channel.

Why the sample-and-hold is not optional. A successive-approximation converter needs time \(t_c\) to decide all its bits. If the input moves by more than \(Q/2\) during \(t_c\), later bits are decided against a different voltage. A sine \(A\sin 2\pi f t\) changes at most at the rate \(2\pi f A\), so without a hold you need

$$2\pi f A\,t_c<\frac{Q}{2}\;\Rightarrow\; f<\frac{Q}{4\pi A t_c}$$

For a 12-bit, ±5 V converter (\(Q=2.44\) mV) with \(t_c=12\ \mu\)s and a full-scale sine (\(A=5\) V): \(f<3.2\) Hz. With a hold, the limit is set by the sampling theorem instead.

Digital filter: a calculation on the stored samples, used to smooth noise or show trends. The simplest is the centred moving average \(\bar y_i=(y_{i-n}+\dots+y_i+\dots+y_{i+n})/(2n+1)\). For \(y_4=3,\ y_5=4,\ y_6=2\), the three-term average at \(i=5\) is 3. A digital filter acts after sampling, so it can never undo aliasing.

Simulator · multiplexed scanning

One converter, many channels: the rate is shared and the instants are staggered

Same signal wired to every channeltrue signalchannel 1last channel

A board quoted at "1000 samples per second" shares that rate among the channels it scans. With \(N\) channels each channel is sampled at

Per-channel rate and channel skew $$f_{s,ch}=\frac{f_{board}}{N},\qquad \Delta t_{1\to N}=\frac{N-1}{f_{board}}$$

and the Nyquist frequency of each channel is \(f_{s,ch}/2\). The second effect is subtler: channel \(N\) is read \((N-1)\) switching intervals after channel 1, but the stored scan treats them as simultaneous. For a sine of frequency \(f\) that looks like a phase shift of \(360°\,f\,\Delta t\).

Keep 1000 S/s, 4 channels and 50 Hz: each channel gets 250 S/s, and the last channel lags by 3 ms, which appears as a 54° phase shift between two identical signals. Raise the board rate to 100 kS/s: the skew shrinks to 0.03 ms (0.5°). Now return to 1000 S/s and scan 16 channels: each channel gets only 62.5 S/s and the 50 Hz signal aliases. Boards that must compare phases use a separate sample-and-hold on every channel, all triggered together (simultaneous sampling).

Analog input · range and gain

Fit the signal to the converter's range

Every DAS input is range limited: below about one \(Q\) a change is invisible, above \(E_{FSR}\) the converter saturates. An amplifier of gain \(G\) in front divides the step size seen at the input:

Resolution referred to the input $$Q_{in}=\frac{E_{FSR}}{G\,2^M}$$

A typical board has a 12-bit converter with a 10 V span: \(Q=2.44\) mV at \(G=1\), but \(10/(1000\times4096)=2.44\ \mu\)V at its maximum gain \(G=1000\). Pick the largest gain that keeps the biggest expected signal inside the range.

Too big a signal? Attenuate it with a voltage divider (a): \(E_o=E_i\,R_2/(R_1+R_2)\). With \(R_1=40\) kΩ and \(R_2=10\) kΩ, a 0–50 V signal becomes 0–10 V.

A current signal? Many industrial transmitters send 4–20 mA, because a current is not reduced by long wire resistance and 0 mA then means a broken wire. A shunt resistor (b) converts it: \(E_o=IR_{shunt}\); 250 Ω turns 4–20 mA into 1–5 V.

A divider has its own output resistance, here \(40\ \text{k}\Omega\parallel10\ \text{k}\Omega=8\) kΩ. Into a 1 MΩ converter input that reads \(1/(1+8\ \text{k}/1\ \text{M})-1=-0.8\,\%\) low. Use larger input impedance, a buffer amplifier, or calibrate the divider in place.

A large mean with a small wiggle? Remove the mean before adding gain: subtract an equal and opposite voltage, or pass the signal through a very low cutoff high-pass filter (AC coupling). A small zero offset of a sensor can likewise be trimmed out with an offset-nulling circuit, a trim potentiometer in a bridge. The next slide shows why this matters.

Simulator · gain and offset removal

A small wiggle on a big mean: remove the mean, then amplify

y(t) = 2 + 0.1 sin 10πt V into a ±5 V converter

The 5 Hz signal \(y(t)=2+0.1\sin10\pi t\) V is to be recorded by a 12-bit converter with a ±5 V range. Only the wiggle matters. Choose the gain with and without removing the mean, and compare the resolution.

Known mean 2 V, amplitude 0.1 V, \(E_{FSR}=10\) V, \(M=12\). Find \(G\), \(Q_{in}\). Assumptions ideal amplifier.

  1. \(Q=10/4096=2.44\) mV.
  2. Without mean removal the peak \(2.1G\) must stay below 5 V: \(G\le2.38\). (\(G=2.5\) gives 5.25 V and saturates.)
  3. With \(G=2.38\): \(Q_{in}=2.44/2.38=1.03\) mV, and the 0.1 V wiggle spans only \(\pm98\) steps.
  4. Subtract 2 V first: the signal is ±0.1 V, so \(G=50\) fills ±5 V. Now \(Q_{in}=2.44\ \text{mV}/50=48.8\ \mu\)V and the wiggle spans ±2048 steps.

What this means: removing the mean buys a 21 times finer resolution of the part you care about.

In the simulator (8 bits, to make steps visible) raise G with no mean removal: the output staircase is coarse, and above G ≈ 2.4 the peaks hit +5 V. Select "subtract 2 V" and raise G towards 50: the recovered wiggle in the lower panel becomes smooth.

Analog input · connections

Single-ended or differential: what is the voltage measured against?

Common-mode voltage (CMV): a voltage present on both signal leads together, for example the difference between two ground points. A ground loop forms when a signal is grounded at two points that are not at the same potential: the difference drives a current and adds to the reading.

Single-ended (a): only the HI line is measured, against the board's ground (GRD), and the return line is tied to that ground. All channels share one ground point. Use it only for large signals on short leads, with every source referenced to the same ground. Long single-ended leads pick up electromagnetic interference, and a grounded source creates a ground loop.

Differential (b): HI and LO both go to the amplifier, which measures HI − LO. The LO line is isolated from ground, so a common-mode voltage appears on both inputs and cancels. Run the pair twisted: interference then induces nearly the same voltage in both wires, which also cancels. This is the preferred connection for low-level signals. For a floating source, connect a 10–100 kΩ resistor from LO to GRD so the input has a path for its bias current, and keep the common-mode voltage within the board's rated CMV range, or the board can be damaged.

To find a person's height on a rocking boat, measuring from the dock to the top of their head (single-ended) includes the boat's motion. Measuring from their feet to the top of their head (differential) does not care how the boat moves.

The same 10 mV signal, wired three ways

2 Hz, 10 mV sensor signaltrue signalwhat the board reads

The model behind the plot: the sensor gives a 2 Hz, 10 mV sine. A 50 Hz ground potential difference appears between the source ground and the board ground. Each lead picks up 0.5 mV of 50 Hz interference per metre. The differential amplifier rejects common-mode voltage by 80 dB (a factor of 10 000). Untwisted leads run apart, so the LO lead picks up only half of what the HI lead does; a twisted pair picks up the same within 2 %.

Start single-ended with 30 mV of ground difference and 10 m of lead: the 10 mV signal is buried in 50 Hz hum. Switch to differential, untwisted: the ground difference disappears, but half the lead pickup remains. Switch to twisted pair: the reading sits on the true signal. Now drag the lead length to 30 m: only the single-ended reading gets much worse. (The pickup and rejection values are illustrative; real ones depend on the installation and the amplifier.)

Worked example

Setting up one strain channel: gain, filter and wiring

A strain transducer has a static sensitivity of 2.5 V per unit strain (2.5 µV/µε) and needs a 5 V DC supply. It feeds a DAS with a ±5 V, 12-bit converter sampling at 1000 Hz. The expected range is 1 to 500 µε. Specify the amplifier gain, the filter, and the connections.

Known \(K=2.5\ \mu\text{V}/\mu\varepsilon\), range 1–500 µε, \(E_{FSR}=10\) V, \(M=12\), \(f_s=1000\) Hz. Find \(G\), filter type and \(f_c\), wiring. Assumptions the signal content of interest lies below 500 Hz.

  1. Signal range: \(1\times2.5=2.5\ \mu\)V up to \(500\times2.5=1.25\) mV.
  2. Resolution: \(Q=10/4096=2.44\) mV. Unamplified, even the largest strain is below one step.
  3. Upper limit: \(1.25\ \text{mV}\times G\le5\) V, so \(G\le4000\).
  4. \(G=1000\) gives 2.5 mV to 1.25 V: the low end is only about 1 \(Q\), lost in quantization noise. \(G=3000\) gives 7.5 mV (about 3 \(Q\)) to 3.75 V (25 % headroom). Choose \(G=3000\).
  5. Filter: \(f_N=1000/2=500\) Hz. Use an analog Butterworth low-pass of 12 dB/octave (two poles) with \(f_c=500\) Hz, between amplifier and board.
  6. Wiring: as drawn, with board excitation, shielded leads, and a twisted pair into channel 0 as a differential input to reduce noise.

What this means: gain is chosen from both ends of the range, resolution at the bottom and saturation at the top.

A device with 600 Ω output impedance drives the 1 MΩ input of a 0–10 V, 12-bit converter at 2 V. How large is the loading error?

The input and source form a divider: \(E_m=E_1/(1+Z_1/Z_m)\). So \(e_I=E_m-E_1=2\big(\tfrac{1}{1+600/10^6}-1\big)=-1.2\) mV. That is below the converter's step of \(10/4096=2.44\) mV, so loading is negligible here.

Worked example

How steep must the anti-alias filter be?

A signal is sampled at 200 Hz by a 12-bit converter with a −10 to +10 V range. It contains an unwanted 200 Hz component of 100 mV amplitude. Specify a Butterworth low-pass filter that shrinks this component to the converter's resolution and also serves as the anti-alias filter.

Known \(f_s=200\) Hz, \(f_1=200\) Hz, \(A_1=100\) mV, \(E_{FSR}=20\) V, \(M=12\). Find \(f_c\) and the number of poles \(k\). Assumptions Butterworth (maximally flat) response.

  1. \(f_N=200/2=100\) Hz, so set \(f_c=100\) Hz.
  2. Unfiltered, the 200 Hz tone sits exactly at \(f_s\): its alias is 0 Hz, a false constant offset in the record.
  3. Resolution: \(Q=20/4096=4.88\) mV. Required magnitude ratio at 200 Hz: \(M(200)\le4.88/100=0.0488\) (−26.2 dB).
  4. Butterworth: \(M=[1+(f/f_c)^{2k}]^{-1/2}\) with \(f/f_c=2\). Need \(1+2^{2k}\ge1/0.0488^2=419\), so \(2k\ge\log_2 418=8.71\) and \(k\ge4.35\).
  5. Round up: \(k=5\). Check: \(M=1/\sqrt{1+2^{10}}=0.0312\), so 100 mV becomes 3.1 mV, below \(Q\). With \(k=4\): \(M=0.0624\), 6.2 mV, still above \(Q\).

What this means: because \(f_c\) sits at \(f_N\), a component only one octave above it still needs a fifth-order filter to vanish below one LSB.

Digital input–output

Digital lines count, trigger and close the control loop

Digital I/O lines carry only HIGH or LOW, usually as 5 V TTL. A single state can switch a relay, power equipment on or off, raise an alarm or report whether a door is shut. A series of pulses carries more:

  • Gate-pulse counting: count the pulses arriving during a fixed gate time to get a frequency. A shaft encoder giving 60 pulses per revolution that produces 1500 counts in a 1 s gate is turning at 25 rev/s (1500 rpm).
  • Pulse stepping: send a set number of pulses to a stepper motor. A 200 step/rev motor given 50 pulses turns 90°.
  • Triggering: start an acquisition by software command, by an external TTL pulse, or from the on-board clock, or send TTL pulses that synchronise other instruments.

Several lines grouped together send a whole word in parallel.

In closed-loop control the DAS measures the controlled variable, compares it with the set point to form the error signal, applies a control law to decide the correction, and sends it out through a D/A converter or a digital line to an actuator. The cycle sample–compare–decide–correct repeats every sample period. Control loops are a subject of their own (control engineering).

Driving in your lane: glance at the lane markings (sample), see you have drifted left (compare), decide on a small right turn (decide), move the wheel (correct), and glance again.

Digital communication · serial

Serial links: one bit at a time, wrapped in a handshake

Serial communication sends data bit by bit on one line; parallel communication sends groups of bits (often a byte) at once on several lines. Both use a handshake: a protocol that starts, permits and ends each transfer.

RS-232C is the classic serial standard between a computer (data terminal equipment, DTE) and an instrument (data communication equipment, DCE). It needs at minimum three wires: transmit, receive and signal ground, on a 9- or 25-pin connector; the other pins are optional handshake lines. 1 and 0 are pulses of opposite polarity between 3 and 25 V. Speed is in baud, signal pulses per second. Half-duplex lets one side talk at a time; full-duplex lets both talk at once.

The start and stop bits frame each byte so the receiver can resynchronise its clock: this is asynchronous transmission, where bytes may come at random intervals. The parity bit makes the number of 1s even (or odd), a simple error check: the byte above has four 1s, so even parity adds 0. A receiver with a nearly full buffer can send XOFF to pause the sender and XON to resume.

A test records 4 channels at 1000 samples/s each for 25 s with a 16-bit converter (2 bytes per sample). How long does sending the record take over RS-232 at 9600 and at 115 200 baud (10-bit frames: start, 8 data, stop), and over USB 2.0 at 480 Mb/s? Can 9600 baud keep up in real time?

Known 4 channels, 1000 S/s, 25 s, 2 bytes per sample. Find transfer times, real-time rate. Assumptions no protocol overhead beyond the frame bits; ideal USB rate.

  1. Samples: \(4\times1000\times25=100\,000\); bytes: \(200\,000\).
  2. 9600 baud with 10 bits per byte moves \(960\) bytes/s: \(200\,000/960=208\) s, about 3.5 min.
  3. 115 200 baud moves 11 520 bytes/s: 17.4 s.
  4. USB 2.0: \(200\,000\times8\) bits \(/\,480\times10^6\) bit/s \(=3.3\) ms (real throughput is lower, but still far faster).
  5. Real time needs \(4\times1000\times2=8000\) bytes/s. 9600 baud (960 bytes/s) cannot keep up; 115 200 baud (11 520 bytes/s) can, just.

What this means: check the link's byte rate against the acquisition rate before you start, or the buffer overflows and data are lost.

Simulator · digital image acquisition

A digital image is a two-dimensional data set

160 × 100 pixel, 8-bit image of five coins

A digital image is an \(m\times n\) array of pixels. In an 8-bit grayscale image each pixel holds an integer from 0 (black) to 255 (white). The sensor is usually a charge-coupled device (CCD): an array of tiny capacitors that collect charge while exposed to light, one per pixel; after the exposure each charge is converted to a number. Cameras range from about 0.3 to over 20 million pixels; a frame grabber board can acquire up to about 200 000 frames per second, but such short exposures need intense lighting.

To find and size the coins, find their edges. An edge lies where the intensity gradient is largest, so the computer estimates the gradient in \(x\) and \(y\) from neighbouring pixels (the Sobel method) and marks pixels whose gradient magnitude exceeds a limit. The Canny method first smooths the image and uses two limits. Thresholding first, setting every pixel above a level to white and the rest to black, removes texture that would produce false edges.

View "edges of grayscale": the coin reliefs and the background texture create clutter. Switch to "thresholded" at 127, then "edges after thresholding": five clean circles, and the readout counts five objects. Drop the threshold to 100: the bright background joins in. Pixels are spatial samples, so stripes finer than two pixels would alias, just like time signals.

Self-test

Check yourself

A 2 mV thermocouple signal must travel 20 m through a workshop to the DAS. How should it be connected?

A board samples at 100 kS/s in total and scans 8 channels. What is the highest signal frequency each channel can record without aliasing?

A 4–20 mA transmitter feeds a 250 Ω shunt into a 0–10 V, 12-bit converter. What is the current resolution?

A board with a 16-channel multiplexer and one converter is used to measure the phase between the input and output of a vibrating system at 200 Hz. Channels 0 and 15 are used, and the board runs at 50 kS/s in total. What goes wrong, and how do you fix it?

Channel 15 is read \(15/50\,000=0.3\) ms after channel 0, which at 200 Hz is a false phase of \(360\times200\times0.0003=21.6°\). Fix it by scanning only the two channels needed (skew \(1/50\,000=20\ \mu\)s, 1.4°), by correcting the known skew in software, or by using a board with simultaneous sample-and-hold on every channel.
