Piezoelectric, Optical, Hall-Effect and Digital Transducers

Squeeze a crystal and it gives a charge. Shine light on a semiconductor and it conducts or makes a voltage. Push a current through a magnetic field and a voltage appears sideways. Chop a light beam with a slotted disc and you count position. This part derives each effect and the sensors built on it.

Where we are

Part A’s sensors needed a supply; most of these make their own signal

Part A built the passive transducers: a potentiometer, an LVDT and a capacitor, each changing R, L or C when something moves. All of them need an excitation voltage, and all of them must be read without loading them. Two lessons carry over: a sensor’s output is only linear over a limited range, and a high output impedance makes the next stage’s job harder.

This part turns to effects that generate an electrical signal from the measurand’s own energy (the piezoelectric crystal, the photovoltaic cell, the search coil), to optical and magnetic effects that modulate a current (the photoconductor, the Hall plate), and to transducers whose output is already a count of pulses (the encoder).

A self-generating transducer converts some of the measurand’s energy into the electrical signal itself. It needs no excitation, but it can deliver only a little energy, so the circuit reading it must draw very little, or the signal decays or collapses.

A passive sensor is a dimmer switch: it controls power you supply. A self-generating sensor is a bicycle dynamo: the motion itself makes the electricity. A dynamo lights the lamp only while you pedal, and a weak one dims if you connect too big a lamp.

Piezoelectric transducers · 1

Squeeze a crystal, get a charge; the crystal’s own capacitance gives a voltage

In crystals such as quartz the positive and negative ions sit in a lattice with no centre of symmetry. Deforming the lattice shifts the centre of positive charge relative to the centre of negative charge, and equal and opposite charges appear on two faces. This is the piezoelectric effect. It works in reverse too: a voltage deforms the crystal, which is how ultrasonic probes and some actuators work.

  1. The charge is proportional to the force: \(q=dF\), where \(d\), the piezoelectric charge constant, is in C/N.
  2. The loaded faces, area \(A\), are electrodes a thickness \(t\) apart with the crystal as dielectric (permittivity \(\varepsilon\)), so they form a capacitor \(C=\varepsilon A/t\).
  3. The open-circuit voltage is \(E=q/C=\dfrac{dF\,t}{\varepsilon A}=\dfrac{d}{\varepsilon}\,t\,p\), with \(p=F/A\) the pressure.
  4. Name \(g=d/\varepsilon\) the voltage sensitivity (V·m/N): $$E=g\,t\,p$$
\(d\)charge per newton, C/N (same as C/m² per N/m²)\(g\)volts per metre of thickness per pascal, V·m/N\(t,\ p\)crystal thickness (m) and pressure on it (Pa)

Piezoelectric transducers · 2

The charge leaks away, so piezo sensors only see changes

Electrically the sensor is a charge generator \(q(t)=dF(t)\) in parallel with the total capacitance \(C\) (crystal, cable and amplifier input) and the total leakage resistance \(R\) (crystal insulation and amplifier input).

  1. Charge balance: the generated charge either sits on C or leaks through R, \(\dfrac{dq}{dt}=C\dfrac{dE}{dt}+\dfrac{E}{R}\).
  2. For a force stepped to \(F_0\) and then held, \(dq/dt=0\) after the step, so \(E\) decays: \(E(t)=\dfrac{dF_0}{C}\,e^{-t/\tau}\), with \(\tau=RC\).
  3. For a sinusoidal force the output ratio is that of a high-pass filter: \(\dfrac{\lvert E\rvert}{dF/C}=\dfrac{\omega\tau}{\sqrt{1+(\omega\tau)^2}}\), with corner frequency \(f_c=1/(2\pi\tau)\).

The crystal fills a bucket (C) with charge, and the bucket has a small hole (R). A sudden splash shows as a level that then drains away. Fast sloshing is measured faithfully because little drains between sloshes; a steady inflow never shows at all.

Piezo sensor with leakagetrue force (scaled)sensor voltage

With the step and τ = 0.5 s, the reading falls to 37 % in one τ. Switch to an impact of 0.01 s: the output follows the force almost perfectly, with a small undershoot afterwards. Choose the sinusoid and shorten τ until the output shrinks and leads the force: that is the high-pass corner.

Worked examples

Hundreds of volts, for a moment

A quartz crystal 2 mm thick, with voltage sensitivity \(g=0.055\) V·m/N, is used as a pressure transducer and subjected to 200 psi (1.379 MPa). What open-circuit voltage does it produce?

Known: \(t=2\times10^{-3}\) m, \(g=0.055\) V·m/N, \(p=200\times6895=1.379\times10^6\) Pa. Find: E. Assumptions: uniform compression across the faces; no leakage during the measurement.

  1. \(E=g\,t\,p=0.055\times(2\times10^{-3})\times(1.379\times10^6)\).
  2. \(E=151.7\) V.
  3. The charge density behind it is small: \(q/A=dp=2.25\times10^{-12}\times1.379\times10^6=3.10\ \mu\)C/m², about 3 nC on a 1 cm² face.
  4. What this means: the voltage is large but sits on a tiny charge. A cable and meter adding capacitance would divide it down, and any leakage would drain it. The measurement must be fast compared with the time constant, or use a charge amplifier (Part E).

A piezoelectric force sensor and its cable have a total capacitance of 500 pF and feed a voltage amplifier of 1 GΩ input resistance. (a) What is the time constant and corner frequency? (b) A load is applied suddenly and held for 0.1 s. How low does the reading fall? (c) What is the lowest sinusoidal frequency measured with less than 1 % amplitude error?

  1. (a) \(\tau=RC=10^9\times500\times10^{-12}=0.50\) s; \(f_c=1/(2\pi\times0.5)=0.32\) Hz.
  2. (b) \(E/E_0=e^{-0.1/0.5}=0.819\): the reading is 18 % low after only 0.1 s.
  3. (c) Need \(\omega\tau/\sqrt{1+(\omega\tau)^2}\ge0.99\), so \(\omega\tau\ge7.02\) and \(f\ge7.02/(2\pi\times0.5)=2.2\) Hz.
  4. What this means: such a sensor is excellent for vibration and impacts but useless for weighing. A charge amplifier can stretch τ to many seconds, which is why it is the standard partner.

Optical transducers · 1

Three ways light becomes an electrical signal

Light arrives as photons of energy \(E_{ph}=hc/\lambda\) (Planck’s constant times the speed of light, over the wavelength). A photon can free an electron only if its energy exceeds a threshold: the work function of a metal surface, or the band gap \(E_g\) of a semiconductor. That sets a longest usable wavelength,

Cut-off wavelength$$\lambda_c=\frac{hc}{E_g}=\frac{1.24}{E_g\ [\text{eV}]}\ \mu\text{m}$$

Silicon (1.12 eV) responds to about 1.1 µm, germanium (0.66 eV) to 1.9 µm, cadmium sulphide (2.4 eV) only to 0.5 µm, close to the eye’s range. Lead sulphide (about 0.4 eV) reaches about 3 µm, which is why it is used for thermal radiation.

  • Photoemissive tube: light frees electrons from a cathode in a vacuum or gas-filled envelope; an anode at positive voltage collects them. Current \(I=S\Phi\), proportional to the illumination \(\Phi\), with sensitivity \(S\) in A/W or A/lm. Responds from about 0.2 to 0.8 µm; quartz windows are needed below 0.4 µm.
  • Photoconductive cell: freed carriers stay inside the semiconductor and lower its resistance. With a supply and a meter it reads light as a current or, in a divider, a voltage.
  • Photovoltaic cell and photodiode: a junction’s built-in field separates the carriers, so the cell makes its own voltage and current (next slides).

A phototransistor is a photodiode with built-in transistor gain: more current per watt, but slower and less linear. Response times run from nanoseconds (fast photodiodes) to tens of milliseconds (cadmium sulphide cells).

Optical transducers · 2

How weak a light can a detector see?

For radiation detectors, the useful question is not only how large the output is but how it compares with the detector’s own noise.

Responsivity \(R_v\) = rms output voltage / rms incident power (V/W). Noise-equivalent power (NEP) = the incident power that gives a signal-to-noise ratio of 1. Detectivity \(D=1/\text{NEP}=R_v/E_{noise}\) (1/W). Noise grows with detector area \(A\) and with the electrical bandwidth \(\Delta f\), both as square roots, so a size- and bandwidth-free figure is the normalised detectivity:

Normalised detectivity$$D^*=(A\,\Delta f)^{1/2}\,D=(A\,\Delta f)^{1/2}\,\frac{E_o}{E_{noise}}\,\frac{1}{P_{inc}}$$
\(D^*\)cm·Hz1/2/W; larger is better\(E_o/E_{noise}\)signal-to-noise voltage ratio; in decibels \(20\log_{10}(E_o/E_{noise})\)\(P_{inc}\)incident radiant power, W

Lead sulphide works from 1 to 3 µm at room temperature; cooling it extends it to 4–5 µm and raises \(D^*\). Indium antimonide reaches further into the infrared but with lower \(D^*\) than lead sulphide at 2 µm.

A lead-sulphide detector at room temperature has an area of 1 mm². At 2 µm its detectivity is \(D^*=1.5\times10^{11}\) cm·Hz1/2/W (for a 1 Hz bandwidth). What incident radiant power gives a signal-to-noise ratio of 40 dB?

Known: \(A=1\ \text{mm}^2=10^{-2}\ \text{cm}^2\), \(\Delta f=1\) Hz, S/N = 40 dB. Find: \(P_{inc}\). Assumption: \(D^*\) applies at this wavelength and bandwidth.

  1. Convert decibels: \(40=20\log_{10}(E_o/E_{noise})\Rightarrow E_o/E_{noise}=100\).
  2. Rearrange the definition: \(P_{inc}=\dfrac{(A\,\Delta f)^{1/2}\,(E_o/E_{noise})}{D^*}\).
  3. \(P_{inc}=\dfrac{(10^{-2}\times1)^{1/2}\times100}{1.5\times10^{11}}=\dfrac{0.1\times100}{1.5\times10^{11}}=6.7\times10^{-11}\) W.
  4. What this means: about 67 picowatts gives a clean signal 100 times the noise. Widen the bandwidth to 100 Hz and the required power rises tenfold, as \(\sqrt{\Delta f}\).

Hearing a whisper in a room: what matters is not how loud the whisper is but how it compares with the background hum. A bigger room (area) or listening to more pitches at once (bandwidth) lets in more hum, so a fair comparison of ears divides those out. That is \(D^*\).

Optical transducers · 3

A cell that reads light on a log scale, and a fibre that reads strain as colour

A photovoltaic cell is a junction that light drives like a battery. The photocurrent \(I_L\) is proportional to light power, but the junction itself (a diode) leaks a current that grows exponentially with voltage:

Photovoltaic cell$$I=I_L-I_0\left(e^{V/V_T}-1\right),\qquad V_{oc}=V_T\ln\!\left(1+\frac{I_L}{I_0}\right)$$

\(I_0\) is the diode’s tiny saturation current and \(V_T=kT/q\approx25.7\) mV at room temperature. With no load (open circuit) the voltage is logarithmic in light: each tenfold increase adds only about 59 mV. With a small load resistance the current is nearly \(I_L\) and the output is linear. The plot shows an illustrative cell with \(I_L=0.1\ \mu\)A per lux and \(I_0=1\) nA.

open circuit (log)10 kΩ load1 kΩ load (linear to ~1000 lx)

The log response suits photographic exposure meters (wide range); the short-circuit current suits radiation measurement (linear).

A fibre Bragg grating (FBG) is a short length of optical fibre whose core has a periodic variation of refractive index, with period \(\Lambda\). Each step reflects a little light; the reflections add in phase only at one wavelength, the Bragg wavelength:

Bragg condition and its shift$$\lambda_B=2n_{eff}\Lambda,\qquad\frac{\Delta\lambda_B}{\lambda_B}=(1-p_e)\,\varepsilon+(\alpha+\xi)\,\Delta T$$

\(n_{eff}\approx1.45\) is the effective index, \(p_e\approx0.22\) the photo-elastic coefficient, \(\alpha\) the thermal expansion and \(\xi\) the thermo-optic coefficient of silica. At 1550 nm this gives about 1.2 pm per microstrain and about 10 pm per kelvin.

Quick numbers: 500 µε shifts the peak by 0.60 nm; a 10 K temperature change shifts it by about 0.11 nm, which would be mistaken for about 90 µε. Installations therefore add a strain-free reference grating. Many gratings at different \(\Lambda\) share one fibre, carry no electric current to the measuring point and ignore electromagnetic interference, which suits bridges, pipelines, generators and composite structures.

Magnetic transducers · 1

The Hall effect: a field pushes the moving charges sideways

Pass a current \(I\) along a thin plate of thickness \(t\) and width \(w\), with a magnetic flux density \(B\) perpendicular to it. Each carrier of charge \(q\) drifting at speed \(v\) feels the magnetic force \(qvB\) across the plate, and charge piles up on one edge until the electric field of that pile balances the force.

  1. Balance on a carrier: \(qE_H=qvB\), so the transverse field is \(E_H=vB\).
  2. Current through the cross-section \(wt\) with \(n\) carriers per m³: \(I=nqvwt\), so \(v=I/(nqwt)\).
  3. Hall voltage across the width: \(V_H=E_Hw=vBw\): $$V_H=\frac{1}{nq}\,\frac{IB}{t}=K_H\frac{IB}{t}$$
  4. \(K_H=1/(nq)\) is the Hall coefficient. Few carriers (a semiconductor) and a thin plate give a large voltage; its sign tells whether the carriers are electrons or holes.

Cars driving down a highway in a steady crosswind drift toward one shoulder until the crowding there pushes back as hard as the wind. The crowding difference across the road is the Hall voltage; the stronger the wind or the faster the traffic, the larger it is.

Tables often give \(K_H\) in V·cm/(A·G) for I in A, B in gauss (1 G = 10⁻⁴ T) and t in cm. The SI coefficient in m³/C is 100 times that number.

A Hall transducer measures a field of 5000 G (0.5 T). It uses a bismuth slab 2 mm thick carrying 3 A. What voltage does it give?

  1. \(E_H=K_H\dfrac{IB}{t}=\dfrac{(-1\times10^{-8})(3)(5000)}{0.2\ \text{cm}}\).
  2. \(E_H=-7.5\times10^{-4}\) V \(=-0.75\) mV. Check in SI: \(K_H=-10^{-6}\) m³/C, \(-10^{-6}\times3\times0.5/0.002=-7.5\times10^{-4}\) V.
  3. What this means: a metal-like material needs amperes for a sub-millivolt signal. With n-type germanium the same plate would give 6 V from 3 A, or 60 mV from only 30 mA, which is why practical Hall sensors use lightly doped semiconductors.

Magnetic transducers · simulator

Watch carriers pile up on one edge

Hall platetop view, B perpendicular to the plate

Start with germanium, 5 mA and 0.5 T: about 40 mV. Reverse B: the carriers swerve to the other edge and the voltage changes sign. Switch to tellurium: the carriers are holes, they drift the other way, yet are pushed to the same edge, so the edge charge and the voltage flip sign. Choose copper and the reading collapses to fractions of a nanovolt.

Uses. A Hall plate fed with constant current is a magnetometer (\(V_H\propto B\)) for fields from about 0.1 mT to 2 T. Placed in the gap of an iron ring around a wire it becomes a clamp-on current probe for AC and DC, reading the wire’s own field without breaking the circuit. With a small magnet it senses position and gear-tooth speed without contact, and it commutates brushless motors.

Hall coefficients vary from plate to plate and change markedly with temperature, and a slight misalignment of the voltage contacts gives an output even at zero field (offset). Each transducer must be calibrated, and integrated sensors include temperature compensation and offset trimming.

Magnetic transducers · 2

A coil reads a changing field; a rotating coil reads a steady one

A flat coil of N turns and area \(A\), its normal at angle \(\alpha\) to a field \(B\), links a flux \(\phi=BA\cos\alpha\). Faraday’s law gives the voltage \(E=N\,d\phi/dt\). A stationary coil in a steady field gives zero, so a steady field is measured by rotating the coil at angular speed \(\omega\):

  1. \(\alpha=\omega t\), so \(\phi=BA\cos\omega t\) and \(E=N\,d\phi/dt=-NAB\omega\sin\omega t\).
  2. Peak \(NAB\omega\); for a sinusoid the rms value is peak/\(\sqrt2\): $$E_{rms}=\frac{NAB\omega}{\sqrt2}$$

A rotating search coil has 10 turns of area 5 cm² and turns at 100 rpm. Its output is 40 mV rms. Find B and the field strength H.

  1. \(\omega=100\times2\pi/60=10.47\) rad/s; \(A=5\times10^{-4}\) m².
  2. \(B=\dfrac{\sqrt2E_{rms}}{NA\omega}=\dfrac{\sqrt2\times0.040}{10\times5\times10^{-4}\times10.47}=1.08\) T.
  3. In air \(H=B/\mu_0=1.08/(4\pi\times10^{-7})=8.6\times10^5\) A/m.
  4. What this means: the accuracy depends only on knowing N, A and ω, which is why search coils (accuracies of 0.05 % are reported) serve as references. The coil must be small enough that B is uniform over it.

Magnetoresistive sensors. In a thin film of permalloy (a nickel–iron alloy) the resistance depends on the angle \(\theta\) between the film’s magnetization and the current, \(R=R_0+\Delta R\cos^2\theta\), with \(\Delta R/R_0\) of a few per cent: the anisotropic magnetoresistive (AMR) effect. An external field turns the magnetization and so changes R, which is read in a Wheatstone bridge (topic 04, part B). Giant magnetoresistance (GMR) stacks of nanometre-thin magnetic and non-magnetic layers give much larger changes. These sensors read weak fields, such as the Earth’s field for electronic compasses, wheel speed in anti-lock brakes and data in hard-disk read heads.

Ionization transducer (older design): a radio-frequency field makes a glow discharge in a low-pressure gas tube between two plates; when the tube moves off centre, two electrodes in the plasma show a DC voltage difference of about 1–10 V/mm, usable for fine displacement.

Digital transducers · 1

Count slots instead of measuring a voltage

A digital displacement transducer turns motion into pulses. A disc with N transparent slots on the shaft passes between a light source and a photodetector; each slot gives one pulse. The count is proportional to angle and the pulse rate to speed. A linear version reads reflecting strips on a scale.

One channel cannot tell direction. An incremental encoder therefore has two detectors, A and B, a quarter of a slot pitch apart, giving square waves 90° apart (quadrature). If A leads B the shaft turns one way; if B leads A, the other. Counting every edge of both channels (×4 decoding) gives four counts per slot. A third track gives one index pulse per revolution as a reference.

Encoder resolution and speed$$\Delta\theta=\frac{360^\circ}{4N},\qquad n=\frac{60\,f}{N}\ \text{rpm}$$
\(N\)slots (lines) per revolution\(f\)pulse frequency of one channel, Hz

An absolute encoder has n concentric tracks read in parallel, giving an n-bit code for each of \(2^n\) positions, so the angle is known at power-up without counting. The tracks use Gray code (000, 001, 011, 010, 110, 111, 101, 100), in which only one bit changes between neighbours, so a reading taken on a boundary is never wrong by more than one step.

1024 slots with ×4 decoding: 4096 counts per revolution, \(\Delta\theta=0.088^\circ\). If 2048 counts arrive in 10 ms, the shaft turns 0.5 rev in 0.01 s: 50 rev/s, 3000 rpm. A 12-bit absolute encoder also resolves 4096 positions but needs no counting. A linear scale of 20 µm pitch read with ×4 decoding resolves 5 µm.

Digital transducers · simulator

Direction comes from which channel leads

Quadrature encoderchannel Achannel B

The disc turns; detector A sits on the slot track and B a quarter pitch further round. Each slot passing gives one high pulse on each channel. The decoder watches the pair (A, B) step through the four states (A, B) = 00 → 10 → 11 → 01 in one direction and the reverse sequence in the other, adding or subtracting one count at each step.

Watch the traces at +0.3 rev/s: A rises while B is low, so A leads. Drag the speed below zero: B now leads and the count runs down. Compare ×1 and ×4 decoding: the angle from the count steps in \(360^\circ/N\) or \(360^\circ/4N\). The error between true and counted angle never exceeds one step.

Two friends stand a quarter of a fence-post spacing apart, watching posts go by from a train. Each shouts as a post passes. If the front friend always shouts first, the train goes forward. Counting every shout from both doubles your resolution, and counting the start and end of each post doubles it again.

Modern sensors

MEMS chips and sensors that speak digital

MEMS (micro-electro-mechanical systems) are moving structures etched in silicon alongside their electronics. A capacitive MEMS accelerometer is a seismic mass on silicon springs, with comb fingers forming the differential capacitor pair of Part A. Newton’s law on the mass, \(kx=ma\), gives a displacement

Seismic displacement per acceleration$$x=\frac{m\,a}{k}=\frac{a}{\omega_n^2},\qquad\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}$$

With a natural frequency of 5 kHz, 1 g (9.81 m/s²) moves the mass only 9.9 nm. Against comb gaps of about 1.5 µm that is a 0.7 % change of each capacitance, read differentially on the chip. A higher \(\omega_n\) widens the bandwidth but shrinks the signal: the same trade-off as any seismic sensor.

A MEMS gyroscope keeps a mass vibrating at velocity \(v\). Rotation at rate \(\Omega\) adds a Coriolis force \(F=2m\Omega v\) at right angles to the vibration, and that sideways motion is sensed capacitively. Phones, drones and vehicle stability systems use both.

A smart sensor puts the sensing element, amplifier, filter, analog-to-digital converter, calibration memory and a digital interface in one package. It sends numbers, not millivolts.

I²C

Two shared wires: clock (SCL) and data (SDA). Each device has an address, so many sensors share one bus. Typical clock rates 100 kHz to 1 MHz.

SPI

Clock, data out, data in and one chip-select line per device. Faster (several MHz and above) and simple, but needs more wires.

Gains: a digital signal does not pick up noise or lose accuracy along the wire, and the chip can correct its own nonlinearity and temperature drift. Costs: the value is sampled and quantized (Topic 05 shows what that means), arrives with some delay, and the internal filtering must suit your signal.

Digital displays remove parallax and reading disagreements and feed computers directly. Analog indicators still have a place: a needle is easier to watch while nulling a circuit, and an operator scanning a panel of dials spots an abnormal one faster than in a wall of numbers.
