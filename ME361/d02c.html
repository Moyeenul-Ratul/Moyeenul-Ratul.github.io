Transfer Functions & Distortion

Real signals are many sine waves at once. This part shows how an instrument treats each of them, why a square wave can come out rounded or ringing, and how to predict what a whole chain of sensor, amplifier and recorder does to a signal.

Where we are

Two models, two pairs of curves; now put them to work on real signals

Parts A and B modelled an instrument by a differential equation and applied one sine wave, \(F(t)=A\sin\omega t\). After the transient dies away, the output is a sine at the same frequency, scaled by the magnitude ratio \(M(\omega)\) and shifted by the phase shift \(\Phi(\omega)\):

\(y_{\text{steady}}=K A\,M(\omega)\sin\big(\omega t+\Phi(\omega)\big)\)

Model\(M(\omega)\)\(\Phi(\omega)\)
First order, \(\tau\dot y+y=KF\)\(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\omega\tau)^2}}\)\(-\tan^{-1}\omega\tau\)
Second order, \(\ddot y/\omega_n^2+2\zeta\dot y/\omega_n+y=KF\)\(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2}}\)\(-\tan^{-1}\dfrac{2\zeta r}{1-r^2}\)

τ: time constant; ωₙ: natural frequency; ζ: damping ratio; \(r=\omega/\omega_n\); K: static sensitivity.

Three questions remain.

  1. Is there one compact object that contains \(M\) and \(\Phi\) at every frequency, for any order? Yes: the transfer function.
  2. A square pulse, a heartbeat or an engine pressure trace contains many frequencies. Each is scaled and shifted differently. When does the shape survive?
  3. A measurement is a chain: sensor, amplifier, filter, recorder. How do their individual responses combine?

An instrument is like a pair of tinted glasses. One colour of light (one frequency) comes through dimmer or brighter by a fixed factor. A full scene contains many colours, so the glasses change not only the brightness but the look of the picture. This part is about predicting that change.

An instrument as an operator

The transfer function turns calculus into multiplication

The Laplace transform turns a function of time \(y(t)\) into a function \(Y(s)=\int_0^\infty y(t)\,e^{-st}\,dt\) of a variable \(s\) (units 1/s). Its useful property: a derivative becomes a multiplication, \(\mathcal L\{\dot y\}=sY(s)-y(0)\).

Logarithms turn multiplication into addition, which is why slide rules worked. The Laplace transform does the same trick one level up: it turns differential equations into algebra.

  1. Transform the first-order model \(\tau\dot y+y=KF(t)\): \(\tau\,[sY(s)-y_0]+Y(s)=KF(s)\).
  2. Solve for the output: $$Y(s)=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\tau s+1}}_{G(s)}\,KF(s)+\underbrace{\frac{1}{\tau s+1}}_{G(s)}\,\tau y_0$$
  3. The first term is driven by the input and gives the steady response; the second comes from the starting state and decays (the transient). Both pass through the same \(G(s)\).
  4. The transfer function \(G(s)\) is the output-to-input ratio \(Y(s)/KF(s)\) with the system starting at rest. For second order the same steps give $$G(s)=\frac{1}{s^2/\omega_n^2+2\zeta s/\omega_n+1}$$
  5. Pattern: replace each \(d/dt\) by \(s\). Any linear instrument \(a_n y^{(n)}+\dots+a_0y=b_0x\) has \(KG(s)=\dfrac{b_0}{a_ns^n+\dots+a_1s+a_0}\).

So \(G(s)\) is the instrument’s complete identity card: it is fixed by the instrument alone, not by the input.

From G(s) to M and Φ

Put s = iω and the transfer function hands you M and Φ

Feed in the complex sinusoid \(e^{i\omega t}=\cos\omega t+i\sin\omega t\). Each time derivative brings out a factor \(i\omega\), exactly as each \(s\) did. So the steady output is \(G(i\omega)\,K\,e^{i\omega t}\), and the complex number \(G(i\omega)\) does two things at once:

Frequency response from the transfer function $$G(i\omega)=M(\omega)\,e^{i\Phi(\omega)}$$

Its length is the magnitude ratio, its angle the phase shift. First order:

\(G(i\omega)=\dfrac{1}{1+i\omega\tau}=\dfrac{1-i\omega\tau}{1+(\omega\tau)^2}\)

Length \(1/\sqrt{1+(\omega\tau)^2}\), angle \(-\tan^{-1}\omega\tau\): the results of part A, with no trial solution needed. Second order: \(G(i\omega)=1/(1-r^2+i\,2\zeta r)\) gives the part B formulas.

At ωτ = 1, a first-order G(iω) = 1/(1 + i) = 0.5 − 0.5i. M and Φ are…

Complex planepath as ω risesG(iω) now

First order: as ω rises the point slides along a half-circle from 1 to 0, never passing −90°. Switch to second order with ζ = 0.3: the path bulges out beyond 1 (resonance), crosses the −90° axis at ω = ωₙ and ends approaching 0 from the −180° side.

When does the shape survive?

A waveform keeps its shape only if every component is delayed by the same time

Distortion is a change in the shape of a waveform, as opposed to a uniform change of size or a uniform delay. Amplitude distortion happens when different frequencies are scaled by different \(M\). Phase distortion happens when different frequencies are delayed by different times.

Take a signal made of harmonics, \(u(t)=\sin\omega t+\sin2\omega t+\sin3\omega t+\dots\), and suppose \(M=1\) for all of them. A phase shift \(\Phi\) at frequency ω is a time delay \(\beta=-\Phi/\omega\).

  1. Suppose the phase is linear in frequency, \(\Phi(\omega)=-\alpha\,\omega\). The \(n\)th harmonic is shifted by \(-n\alpha\omega\).
  2. Output: \(v(t)=\sin(\omega t-\alpha\omega)+\sin(2\omega t-2\alpha\omega)+\dots\)
  3. Factor each term: \(\sin\big(n\omega(t-\alpha)\big)\). So \(v(t)=u(t-\alpha)\): the same waveform, merely \(\alpha\) seconds late.
  4. If \(\Phi\) is not proportional to ω, the delay \(-\Phi(n\omega)/n\omega\) differs from harmonic to harmonic, and the components slide out of step: the shape changes.
Condition for no phase distortion $$\Phi(\omega)=-\alpha\,\omega\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\beta=-\frac{\Phi}{\omega}=\alpha\ \text{for every }\omega$$

A marching band passes behind a building. If every row is hidden for the same 10 seconds, the band reappears in perfect formation, just later. If the front rows are hidden for 10 s and the back rows for 30 s, the formation comes out scrambled. Harmonics are the rows; the instrument is the building.

For a faithful waveform you need both: \(M(\omega)\) flat (no amplitude distortion) and \(\Phi(\omega)\) a straight line through the origin (no phase distortion) over every frequency the signal contains. A constant delay is harmless; you can always shift the time axis back.

Worked example 1

Same two sine waves, two delays: one keeps its shape, one does not

Phase-distortion labu(t) = sin t + sin 5tmeasured signal

The sin t part is always delayed by 0.35 rad (0.35 s). Set the 5t lag to 1.75 rad: both parts are 0.35 s late and the orange curve is the grey one shifted right. Now try 5.00 rad (system 2 of the problem) and 0.35 rad: same amplitudes, different shape.

A signal \(u(t)=\sin t+\sin5t\) (t in s) is measured by two systems. The first records \(v(t)=\sin(t-0.35)+\sin(5t-1.75)\); the second records \(w(t)=\sin(t-0.35)+\sin(5t-5)\). Neither changes an amplitude. Which record is distorted?

  1. Delay of each component \(=\) phase lag ÷ angular frequency.
  2. System 1: \(0.35/1=0.35\) s and \(1.75/5=0.35\) s. Equal. Phase lag is proportional to frequency (0.35 rad per rad/s).
  3. So \(v(t)=u(t-0.35)\): an exact copy, 0.35 s late.
  4. System 2: \(0.35/1=0.35\) s but \(5/5=1.00\) s. The fast component arrives 0.65 s later than the slow one.
  5. Meaning: \(w(t)\) is distorted even though both amplitudes are perfect. Peaks appear where \(u\) had none. Phase alone can ruin a waveform.

Linear phase in real instruments

ζ between 0.7 and 0.8 gives a nearly constant delay up to ωₙ

Plot the time delay \(\beta(\omega)=-\Phi(\omega)/\omega\) of each instrument. A horizontal line means linear phase.

First order. For small ωτ, \(\tan^{-1}\omega\tau\approx\omega\tau\), so \(\beta\approx\tau\): slow signals are simply delayed by one time constant (the same lag found for a ramp in part A). The delay falls to \(0.93\tau\) at ωτ = 0.5 and \(0.79\tau\) at ωτ = 1.

Second order. For small r, \(\Phi\approx-2\zeta r\), so \(\beta\approx2\zeta/\omega_n\). How well this holds up to \(r=1\) depends strongly on ζ:

Distortion you can see

Send a square wave through a second-order instrument

Distortion labinput (odd harmonics to 39th)instrument output

A square wave of height ±1 and frequency ω is the sum of odd harmonics:

\(u(t)=\dfrac{4}{\pi}\Big[\sin\omega t+\tfrac13\sin3\omega t+\tfrac15\sin5\omega t+\dots\Big]\)

The sharp corners live in the high harmonics. The instrument multiplies the \(n\)th harmonic by \(M(n\omega)\) and shifts it by \(\Phi(n\omega)\); the output is the sum of the modified harmonics. The lower plot compares harmonic amplitudes going in (grey) and coming out (orange).

Start at ζ = 0.7 and ω/ωₙ ≈ 0.02: an almost perfect square, slightly late. Drop ζ to 0.1: harmonics near ωₙ are amplified and the corners ring. Back to ζ = 0.7 and raise ω/ωₙ to 0.3: harmonics above ωₙ are cut and the corners round off. Try ζ = 2: lagging, sagging edges.

A sine-wave calibration at one frequency says nothing about how a sharp pulse will look. For waveforms with steep edges, check \(M\) and \(\Phi\) up to the highest harmonic that matters (roughly 10 times the fundamental for a recognisable square wave).

Many frequencies at once

Superposition: treat each frequency on its own, then add

The square wave already used an idea worth stating properly. The models of parts A and B are linear: \(y\) and its derivatives appear only to the first power, with constant coefficients.

  1. Suppose \(y_1\) is the response to \(F_1\): \(\tau\dot y_1+y_1=KF_1\); and \(y_2\) to \(F_2\): \(\tau\dot y_2+y_2=KF_2\).
  2. Add the two equations: \(\tau(\dot y_1+\dot y_2)+(y_1+y_2)=K(F_1+F_2)\).
  3. So \(y_1+y_2\) is the response to \(F_1+F_2\). The same works for second order and any number of terms. This is the principle of superposition.
Steady response to a multi-frequency input $$F(t)=A_0+\sum_k A_k\sin\omega_kt$$ $$y(t)=KA_0+\sum_k KA_k\,M(\omega_k)\,\sin\big(\omega_kt+\Phi(\omega_k)\big)$$

Superposition needs a linear instrument. Saturation, friction with a dead band, or a sensor driven beyond its linear range breaks it, and then frequencies mix and new ones appear.

The constant part \(A_0\) has zero frequency, where \(M=1\) and \(\Phi=0\), so it passes with gain \(K\). Every sine term is scaled and shifted by the instrument’s own values at its own frequency. The transient is ignored here because its form depends only on the instrument and it dies out.

Music heard through a wall. The bass passes almost untouched, the treble is muffled. The wall does nothing clever: it treats each note independently, and the dull sound you hear is just the sum of the separately weakened notes.

A second-order instrument (K = 1) measures \(F=5+10\sin25t+20\sin400t\) with ωₙ = 628 rad/s and ζ = 2. Its M(400) = 0.38. The 400 rad/s term in the output has amplitude…

Worked example 2

Two frequencies through a first-order sensor

The gas temperature in an intake duct fluctuates about its mean as \(T'(t)=10\sin(2\pi t)+5\sin(20\pi t)\) °C: a 1 Hz and a 10 Hz component. A thermocouple with \(\tau=20\) ms and \(K=1\) °C/°C measures it. Predict the recorded fluctuation.

Known: \(A_1=10\) °C at \(\omega_1=6.28\) rad/s; \(A_2=5\) °C at \(\omega_2=62.8\) rad/s; τ = 0.020 s. Assumptions: first order, linear, steady state.

  1. 1 Hz: \(\omega_1\tau=6.28\times0.020=0.126\). \(M=1/\sqrt{1+0.0158}=0.992\); \(\Phi=-\tan^{-1}0.126=-7.2^\circ=-0.125\) rad.
  2. 10 Hz: \(\omega_2\tau=1.257\). \(M=1/\sqrt{1+1.579}=0.623\); \(\Phi=-51.5^\circ=-0.899\) rad.
  3. Superpose: $$y(t)=9.92\sin(2\pi t-0.125)+3.11\sin(20\pi t-0.899)\ ^\circ\text{C}$$
  4. Delays: \(0.125/6.28=19.9\) ms for 1 Hz, \(0.899/62.8=14.3\) ms for 10 Hz.
  5. Meaning: the slow swing is recorded almost perfectly (0.8 % low, 20 ms late). The 10 Hz ripple is shrunk by 38 % and shifted by a different delay, so the recorded shape differs from the real one. To capture 10 Hz within 5 % you would need \(\omega\tau\le0.33\), i.e. \(\tau\le5.2\) ms.

Instruments in a chain

Chain the stages: magnitudes multiply, phases add

  1. Input \(A\sin\omega t\) to stage 1. Its steady output is \(y_1=K_1M_1A\sin(\omega t+\Phi_1)\): still a sine at ω.
  2. Stage 2 receives a sine of amplitude \(K_1M_1A\), already shifted by \(\Phi_1\). It scales by \(K_2M_2\) and shifts by \(\Phi_2\): \(y_2=K_1K_2M_1M_2A\sin(\omega t+\Phi_1+\Phi_2)\).
  3. Repeat for every stage.
Coupled (cascaded) systems without loading $$K=K_1K_2\cdots K_H,\qquad M(\omega)=M_1M_2\cdots M_H$$ $$\Phi(\omega)=\Phi_1+\Phi_2+\dots+\Phi_H$$

In transfer-function form the whole chain is one block, \(KG(s)=K_1G_1(s)\,K_2G_2(s)\cdots\), because multiplying complex numbers multiplies their lengths and adds their angles.

A decibel value is \(20\log_{10}M\). Because the log of a product is a sum of logs, the overall magnitude in dB is simply the sum of the stages’ dB values: −3 dB followed by −6 dB is −9 dB (M = 0.35).

A rumour passed along three people. Each one waters the story down by some fraction and adds some delay before passing it on. The watering-down multiplies, the delays add, and the chain is always worse than its weakest link.

When the product rule fails

The rule assumes each stage does not disturb the one before it

Loading occurs when connecting a stage draws enough energy (current, force, heat) from the stage before it to change that stage’s own behaviour. A transfer function measured on a stage standing alone then no longer describes it inside the chain.

Test case: two RC filters. A resistor R feeding a capacitor C to ground obeys \(RC\,\dot v_{out}+v_{out}=v_{in}\): first order with \(\tau=RC\) and \(G=1/(1+sRC)\). Connect two identical ones directly.

  1. Current balance at \(v_1\): \((v_{in}-v_1)/R=sCv_1+(v_1-v_2)/R\). At \(v_2\): \((v_1-v_2)/R=sCv_2\), so \(v_1=v_2(1+sRC)\).
  2. Substitute: \(v_{in}=v_2\big[(1+sRC)(2+sRC)-1\big]\), giving $$G_{\text{loaded}}=\frac{1}{1+3sRC+(sRC)^2}\ \ne\ \frac{1}{(1+sRC)^2}=\frac{1}{1+2sRC+(sRC)^2}$$
  3. At \(\omega RC=1\): the product rule predicts \(M=0.5\) (−6.0 dB); the real chain gives \(|1/(3i)|=0.333\) (−9.5 dB). At \(\omega RC=0.5\): 0.80 predicted, 0.60 actual.
  4. Cure: put a buffer (an amplifier with very high input impedance and unity gain) between the stages, so stage 2 draws almost no current. Then \(M=M_1M_2\) holds again.

Two first-order stages each measured alone have M = 0.9 at some frequency. Connected directly, the chain reads 0.70. Most likely…

Worked example 3

A first-order sensor feeding a second-order recorder

A temperature oscillates as \(5\sin(4\pi t)\) °C about its mean (2 Hz). A sensor with signal conditioning (first order, \(\tau=0.05\) s, \(K_1=10\) mV/°C) drives a pen recorder (second order, \(f_n=10\) Hz, \(\zeta=0.7\), \(K_2=1\) mm/mV), buffered so there is no loading. Find the recorded amplitude and its time lag.

Known: \(A=5\) °C, \(\omega=4\pi=12.57\) rad/s, τ, \(K_1\), \(\omega_n=2\pi(10)=62.8\) rad/s, ζ, \(K_2\). Assumptions: linear stages, no loading, steady state.

  1. Overall sensitivity: \(K=K_1K_2=10\) mm/°C. A perfect chain would draw ±50 mm.
  2. Sensor: \(\omega\tau=0.628\). \(M_1=1/\sqrt{1+0.395}=0.847\), \(\Phi_1=-\tan^{-1}0.628=-32.1^\circ\).
  3. Recorder: \(r=12.57/62.8=0.200\). \((1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2=0.9216+0.0784=1.000\), so \(M_2=1.000\); \(\Phi_2=-\tan^{-1}(0.28/0.96)=-16.3^\circ\).
  4. Chain: \(M=0.847\times1.000=0.847\); \(\Phi=-32.1-16.3=-48.4^\circ\).
  5. Recorded amplitude \(=KAM=10\times5\times0.847=42.3\) mm. Lag \(=0.845\ \text{rad}/12.57=67\) ms.
  6. Meaning: the recorder is not the problem (no amplitude error, though it adds 16° of lag); the sensor loses 15 %. To reach 5 % error at 2 Hz the sensor needs \(\omega\tau\le0.33\), i.e. \(\tau\le26\) ms.

For linear stages without loading, no. Multiplication of the M’s and addition of the Φ’s do not care about order. With loading, or with a nonlinear stage such as a saturating amplifier, order does matter.

Bode plots

On a Bode plot, a chain is just the sum of its stages

Bode plot · sensor + recordersensorrecorderchain

A Bode plot shows the frequency response as two graphs against log frequency: magnitude in decibels and phase in degrees. Because dB values and phases both add along a chain, the chain’s curve is the vertical sum of the stages’ curves.

Reading the slopes. Far above its corner frequency \(1/\tau\), a first-order stage has \(M\approx1/\omega\tau\): ten times the frequency gives one tenth the amplitude, a fall of 20 dB per decade. A second-order stage far above ωₙ has \(M\approx1/r^2\): 40 dB per decade. A chain of both falls at 60 dB per decade at high frequency.

The defaults are worked example 3 (τ = 0.05 s, fₙ = 10 Hz, ζ = 0.7, f = 2 Hz): the readout gives about −1.45 dB (M ≈ 0.85) and −48°. Drop the recorder ζ to 0.15: a bump appears near 10 Hz in both the recorder and the chain. Raise τ to 0.5 s: the sensor curve now dominates everything above 0.3 Hz.

One more, by hand. A first-order sensor (τ = 0.1 s, K = 1) measures \(F(t)=2+\sin5t+\sin50t\). Write the steady output and say which part is distorted.

Constant: passes as 2. At 5 rad/s, ωτ = 0.5: \(M=0.894\), \(\Phi=-26.6^\circ\) (−0.464 rad, delay 93 ms). At 50 rad/s, ωτ = 5: \(M=0.196\), \(\Phi=-78.7^\circ\) (−1.373 rad, delay 27 ms). So \(y=2+0.894\sin(5t-0.464)+0.196\sin(50t-1.373)\). The mean is exact; the 50 rad/s ripple is cut to a fifth and delayed differently from the 5 rad/s part, so the shape is changed.
