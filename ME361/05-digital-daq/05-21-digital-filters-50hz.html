Clean a noisy, humming record and see each filter's price

A load-cell signal with content below 5 Hz is sampled at \(f_s=1\) kHz and carries 50 Hz hum (with harmonics) of 20 mV amplitude. Choose a moving average that removes the hum, and find its effect on the signal, its delay, and what happens if the mains drifts to 50.5 Hz or if \(N=15\) is used.

  1. The window must hold one mains period: \(T=1/50=20\) ms, so \(N=T f_s=20\). Zeros fall at 50, 100, 150 Hz…
  2. Signal: \(|H(5\ \text{Hz})|=\sin(\pi\cdot5\cdot0.02)/(20\sin(\pi\cdot5\cdot0.001))=0.984\); at 1 Hz, 0.9993.
  3. Delay \((N-1)\Delta t/2=9.5\) ms.
  4. At 50.5 Hz, \(|H|=0.0099\): 20 mV of hum becomes 0.20 mV, still a hundredfold reduction.
  5. With \(N=15\) (15 ms) there is no zero at 50 Hz: \(|H(50)|=0.30\), and 6 mV of hum remains.

What this means: the averaging window must be a whole number of hum periods; then even a short average beats a much stronger RC-type filter at 50 Hz.

Compare "moving average", N = 20, with "first-order IIR" at 5 Hz: both smooth, but only the average removes the hum. Set N = 15 and watch the hum return. Turn on spikes: both linear filters smear each spike into a bump; the median removes them and keeps the step sharp. Note how the real-time filters shift the step later; the offline ones do not.
