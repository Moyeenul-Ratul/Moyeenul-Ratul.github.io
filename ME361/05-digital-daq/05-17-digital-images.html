Simulator · digital image acquisition

A digital image is a two-dimensional data set

160 × 100 pixel, 8-bit image of five coins

A digital image is an \(m\times n\) array of pixels. In an 8-bit grayscale image each pixel holds an integer from 0 (black) to 255 (white). The sensor is usually a charge-coupled device (CCD): an array of tiny capacitors that collect charge while exposed to light, one per pixel; after the exposure each charge is converted to a number. Cameras range from about 0.3 to over 20 million pixels; a frame grabber board can acquire up to about 200 000 frames per second, but such short exposures need intense lighting.

To find and size the coins, find their edges. An edge lies where the intensity gradient is largest, so the computer estimates the gradient in \(x\) and \(y\) from neighbouring pixels (the Sobel method) and marks pixels whose gradient magnitude exceeds a limit. The Canny method first smooths the image and uses two limits. Thresholding first, setting every pixel above a level to white and the rest to black, removes texture that would produce false edges.

View "edges of grayscale": the coin reliefs and the background texture create clutter. Switch to "thresholded" at 127, then "edges after thresholding": five clean circles, and the readout counts five objects. Drop the threshold to 100: the bright background joins in. Pixels are spatial samples, so stripes finer than two pixels would alias, just like time signals.
