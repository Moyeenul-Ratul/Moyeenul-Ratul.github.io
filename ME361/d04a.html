Meters and Electrical Instruments

A transducer hands you a current, a voltage, a resistance or a train of pulses. This part shows the instruments that read them: moving-coil and AC meters, the null-balance potentiometer, digital and electronic voltmeters, oscilloscopes, recorders and counters, and how each one can disturb what it measures.

Where we are

The transducers are built; now we read them

Topic 03 built the transducers. The passive ones of its part A (potentiometer, strain gauge, RTD, LVDT, capacitive and eddy-current sensors) change a resistance, an inductance or a capacitance, and must be excited and read without being loaded. The ones of its part B mostly generate a signal: a piezoelectric crystal gives a charge, a photocell a current or voltage, a Hall plate or a search coil a voltage, and an encoder a train of pulses.

Every one of those outputs ends up at an instrument that turns it into a number or a trace. This part is about those instruments: how they work inside, what quantity they really respond to, how fast they can follow, and how much they disturb the circuit they are connected to.

The rms value (root mean square) of a periodic current is \(I_{rms}=\sqrt{\tfrac1T\int_0^Ti^2\,dt}\): the steady current that would heat a resistor equally. For a sine wave of peak \(I_p\) it is \(I_p/\sqrt2\). The average (rectified) value \(\tfrac1T\int_0^T\lvert i\rvert\,dt\) is \(2I_p/\pi\) for a sine. Their ratio, 1.11 for a sine, is the form factor.

A transducer writes a message in an electrical language; an instrument is the reader. Some readers only skim the average, some read every word (rms), some can follow a fast speaker (an oscilloscope), and some simply count the words (a counter). Choosing an instrument means choosing the right reader for the message.

Measuring current

The D’Arsonval movement: a coil that twists in a magnet

A straight wire of length \(l\) carrying current \(I\) at right angles to a field \(B\) feels a force \(F=IlB\). A rectangular coil of \(N\) turns and area \(A\) has two such sides pushed in opposite directions, so it feels a torque:

Torque on a current loop$$T_m=N\,I\,A\,B\sin\alpha$$
\(\alpha\)angle between the coil’s normal and the field\(B\)flux density, T (Wb/m²)

The curved pole faces and the iron core make the field radial, so the coil sides always cut it at right angles and \(\sin\alpha=1\) at every pointer angle. A spiral spring of stiffness \(k\) resists with torque \(k\theta\). At rest:

Steady deflection$$k\theta=NIAB\quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta=\frac{NAB}{k}\,I$$

The scale is linear in current. Quick number: 20 turns of area 1 in² (6.45 cm²) carrying 20 mA in 0.4 T give \(T_m=20(0.020)(6.45\times10^{-4})(0.4)=1.03\times10^{-4}\) N·m, about the torque of a 1 g mass hung 1 cm from a pivot.

Measuring current · two modes of use

Deflection mode reads the pointer; null mode drives it to zero

Galvanometerpointer = mass–spring–damper

The moving coil has inertia \(J\), bearing friction acts as damping \(c\), and the spring gives stiffness \(k\). Newton’s law for rotation gives \(J\ddot\theta+c\dot\theta+k\theta=T_m\): a second-order system, exactly the model of Topic 02. A lightly damped pointer overshoots and swings before it settles.

Deflection mode: the pointer’s steady angle is the reading. The coil takes energy from the circuit to turn, so the circuit is disturbed; this is a loading error (Part D quantifies it).

Null mode: a galvanometer is a very sensitive movement with its zero in the middle of the scale. You adjust a known quantity until it reads zero. At zero current the meter takes no energy, so the loading error vanishes. Sensitive mechanical galvanometers resolve about 0.1 µA per division.

Set ζ = 0.15 and flip I from +30 to −30 µA: the pointer rings. Raise ζ to about 0.7: it arrives quickly with a small overshoot. Meters are damped near this value, partly by eddy currents in the coil frame.

Worked example

One meter movement, many ranges: shunts and multipliers

A movement reads full scale at \(I_m=50\ \mu\)A and its coil resistance is \(R_m=1000\ \Omega\). (a) What shunt resistor makes it a 0–10 mA ammeter? (b) What series resistor makes it a 0–10 V voltmeter? (c) The 0–2 V range is used on a source of 1.000 V with 10 kΩ internal resistance. What does it read?

Known: \(I_m\), \(R_m\), ranges. Find: \(R_{sh}\), \(R_{se}\), reading. Assumptions: linear movement; resistors exact.

  1. (a) At full scale only 50 µA may pass the coil, so the shunt in parallel carries \(10-0.05=9.95\) mA. Both see the same voltage \(I_mR_m=50\times10^{-6}\times1000=0.05\) V.
  2. \(R_{sh}=\dfrac{0.05\ \text{V}}{9.95\times10^{-3}\ \text{A}}=5.03\ \Omega.\) Smaller shunts give larger ranges: 0.050 Ω for 0–1 A.
  3. (b) At 10 V the total series resistance must limit the current to 50 µA: \(R_{se}+R_m=\dfrac{10}{50\times10^{-6}}=200\ \text{k}\Omega\), so \(R_{se}=199\ \text{k}\Omega\). The meter’s figure of merit is \(1/I_m=20\ \text{k}\Omega/\text{V}\).
  4. (c) On the 2 V range the meter’s resistance is \(2\times20\ \text{k}\Omega=40\ \text{k}\Omega\). The current is \(1.000/(10\,000+40\,000)=20\ \mu\)A, and the meter shows \(20\times10^{-6}\times40\,000=0.800\) V.
  5. What this means: the meter reads 20 % low, not because it is badly calibrated, but because it draws current through the source’s own resistance. That is a loading error.

Measuring alternating current

A permanent-magnet meter cannot follow AC; these movements can

The D’Arsonval coil turns in proportion to the instantaneous current. On a 50 Hz current its inertia averages the torque to zero and it reads nothing. Other movements give a torque that does not change sign:

  • Iron-vane (moving-iron): the current flows in a fixed coil and pulls a soft-iron vane against a spring. The pull depends on the square of the current, so the deflection follows \(I_{rms}^2\). Simple and robust; eddy currents and hysteresis in the iron limit accuracy. Low frequencies, about 25–125 Hz.
  • Electrodynamometer: the permanent magnet is replaced by fixed coils carrying the same current as the moving coil. Torque \(\propto i_{fixed}\,i_{moving}=i^2\), averaging to \(I_{rms}^2\) for any waveform. It can be calibrated with DC and used for AC, up to about 2 kHz in special designs.
  • Rectifier type: diodes rectify the AC and a D’Arsonval movement reads the average. The scale is multiplied by the sine form factor 1.11, so it reads rms correctly only for sine waves.
  • Thermocouple meter: the current heats a small resistor (power \(I_{rms}^2R\)); a thermocouple on it drives a DC movement. True rms for any waveform, up to about 100 MHz.
  • Electrostatic voltmeter: charged plates attract against a spring with force \(\propto V_{rms}^2\). Needs above about 100 V, works to about 50 MHz and draws no DC current (about 20 pF for a 5000 V meter).

Measuring resistance

The ohmmeter: Ohm’s law with a backwards scale

The simplest way to measure resistance is to apply a known voltage and measure the current, \(R=E/I\). An analog ohmmeter puts a battery \(E\), a zero-adjust resistor and a meter movement in series with the unknown \(R_x\). With the leads shorted you trim the zero-adjust until the meter reads full scale \(I_{fs}\); the internal resistance is then \(R_{int}=E/I_{fs}\).

  1. With \(R_x\) connected the current is \(I=\dfrac{E}{R_{int}+R_x}\).
  2. Divide by the full-scale current: \(\dfrac{I}{I_{fs}}=\dfrac{R_{int}}{R_{int}+R_x}\).
  3. So \(R_x=0\) reads full scale, \(R_x=R_{int}\) reads half scale, and \(R_x\to\infty\) reads zero. The scale runs backwards and is crowded at the high end.
  4. Numbers: \(E=1.5\) V and \(I_{fs}=1\) mA give \(R_{int}=1500\ \Omega\). Then 500 Ω reads 75 %, 1.5 kΩ reads 50 %, 4.5 kΩ reads 25 %, 13.5 kΩ reads 10 %. Range switches change \(R_{int}\) with shunts so the useful middle of the scale covers other decades.

The test current heats the resistor by \(I^2R\). A fine RTD or strain-gauge wire can be shifted, or even burnt out like a fuse, by an ohmmeter’s current. Check the test current before measuring delicate sensors.

With \(R_{int}=1500\ \Omega\), a resistor makes the pointer stop at one third of full scale. What is \(R_x\)?

Measuring voltage · null balance

The potentiometer weighs an unknown voltage against a known one

The slide wire is a voltage divider: a uniform resistor whose voltage drop is proportional to length, so the voltage between B and the slider A is \(E_{AB}=(R_x/R_T)\,E_i=x\,E_i\). When G reads zero, no current flows through the unknown source, and

Potentiometer at balance$$E_m=E_{AB}=\frac{R_x}{R_T}\,E_i$$

The supply \(E_i\) is first set against a standard cell of known emf. Good instruments reach uncertainties below 1 µV, which is why they once calibrated thermocouples. Digital voltmeters replaced them by using a very high input resistance instead of a null.

Deflection vs nulltrue source: 1.000 V behind Rs

Deflection: with Rs = 10 kΩ and Rm = 40 kΩ the meter reads 0.800 V. Switch to null, wire supply 2.000 V, and move x until G sits at zero: x = 0.5000 reads 1.000 V for any Rs. With Rs = 1 MΩ the needle barely moves near balance, so finding the null gets harder.

Digital meters

A digital voltmeter turns voltage into time, and time into a count

Ramp-type digital voltmeterreference rampunknown Eₓcounting window

The heart of a digital meter is a clock, usually a quartz crystal oscillator, shaped into one pulse per cycle. In the simplest voltmeter an internal reference falls linearly from 10 V to 0 at a known rate, here 0.1 V/s. A comparator starts a counter when the reference equals the unknown, and a second comparator stops it when the reference reaches zero. The time elapsed is \(E_x/(0.1\ \text{V/s})\), so with one pulse per second a count of 23 means 2.3 V.

With 1 pulse/s the display steps in 0.1 V and Eₓ = 2.337 V shows 2.3. Switch to 100 pulses/s: the same ramp now gives 233 counts and 2.33 V. Resolution is the ramp rate divided by the clock rate. Real meters run the clock at megahertz, and most bench meters integrate the input over a fixed time instead (dual-slope), which also averages out mains hum.

An ammeter is a voltmeter across a precision internal resistor; an ohmmeter passes a known current through the unknown. A 3½-digit display counts to ±1999, so on a 2 V range it resolves 1 mV. Analog meters respond to current; digital meters respond to voltage and draw very little.

Electronic voltmeters · worked example

High input impedance is what makes an electronic voltmeter honest

An electronic voltmeter (EVM) puts an amplifier between the input and the display. A function switch sends DC straight to a range divider; AC first passes a divider and a rectifier (or a true-rms converter). The amplifier then drives a pointer or a digital display. Because the amplifier draws almost no current, the input resistance is set by the divider: typically 10 MΩ for a digital multimeter and above 100 MΩ for laboratory electrometers.

Measuring the pressure in a bicycle tyre with a gauge that lets out half the air gives a low reading of a tyre you have just changed. A low-resistance voltmeter does the same to a high-resistance circuit. An EVM is a gauge that lets out almost nothing.

A constant 100 V is applied across two 0.5 MΩ resistors in series. The voltage across the lower one (true value 50 V) is measured (a) with a volt-ohmmeter of internal resistance 100 kΩ on its 100 V range, and (b) with an EVM of input resistance 17 MΩ. What does each read?

Known: \(E=100\) V, \(R_1=R_2=0.5\) MΩ, \(R_m=0.1\) or 17 MΩ. Find: indicated \(E_{AB}\) and error. Assumptions: ideal supply; meters are pure resistances.

  1. The meter is in parallel with the lower resistor, and that combination forms a divider with the upper one: $$R_p=\frac{R_2R_m}{R_2+R_m},\qquad E_{AB}=E\,\frac{R_p}{R_p+R_1}$$
  2. (a) \(R_p=\dfrac{0.5\times0.1}{0.6}=0.0833\) MΩ, \(E_{AB}=100\times\dfrac{0.0833}{0.5833}=14.3\) V: an error of −71 %.
  3. (b) \(R_p=\dfrac{0.5\times17}{17.5}=0.4857\) MΩ, \(E_{AB}=100\times\dfrac{0.4857}{0.9857}=49.28\) V: an error of −1.4 %.
  4. What this means: the cheap meter is not inaccurate, it is the wrong tool for a megohm circuit. If the circuit resistance is known, the reading can be corrected; better, use a meter whose resistance is at least 100 times the source resistance seen at the terminals (here 0.25 MΩ).

Measuring voltage · seeing the waveform

The oscilloscope draws voltage against time

An electron gun fires a narrow beam at a phosphor screen, which glows where it is hit. Electrons are charged, so a voltage across a pair of plates bends the beam. The input signal, after a calibrated amplifier, drives the vertical plates. A sawtooth voltage on the horizontal plates sweeps the beam across the screen at constant speed and snaps it back. The trace is therefore voltage (volts per division) against time (seconds per division).

The trigger starts each sweep when the input crosses a chosen level on a chosen slope, so successive sweeps land on top of each other and a repeating waveform stands still.

A digital oscilloscope does the same job by sampling the input with an analog-to-digital converter, storing the samples, and redrawing them on a screen. It can also store long records, average, and compute spectra.

A single-number meter tells you one value. A scope shows magnitude, frequency, distortion, noise and the AC and DC parts all at once, which is why it is the first tool to reach for when a signal looks wrong.

Oscilloscope · X–Y mode

Two signals against each other reveal phase and frequency ratio

Switch off the sweep and drive the horizontal plates with a second signal: the beam then draws one signal against the other, a Lissajous figure. To measure the phase shift \(\varphi\) of a network, feed the oscillator to the horizontal input and the network’s output to the vertical input.

  1. Horizontal \(x=X\sin\omega t\), vertical \(y=A\sin(\omega t+\varphi)\): one frequency, so the trace is an ellipse.
  2. Where the ellipse crosses the vertical axis, \(x=0\), so \(\sin\omega t=0\) and \(y=\pm A\sin\varphi\). Call that intercept \(B\).
  3. The ellipse’s highest point is \(y=A\). So $$\varphi=\sin^{-1}\frac{B}{A}$$
  4. \(\varphi=0\): a straight line sloping up to the right. 90°: an ellipse with axes along x and y (a circle if \(X=A\)). 180°: a line sloping up to the left.

With different frequencies the figure closes only if their ratio is a ratio of whole numbers, and then vertical frequency / horizontal frequency = number of maxima along the top edge / number of maxima along the side edge. Against a known oscillator this measures an unknown frequency.

Lissajous figurex = horizontal input, y = vertical input

At 1 : 1 set φ = 30°: the intercept B is half the height A, and \(\sin^{-1}0.5=30^\circ\). Slide φ to 90° (upright ellipse) and on to 180° (a line leaning left). Then choose 2 : 1 and count two tops across the top edge against one along the side.

Oscilloscope · digital, sampling, selection

Choosing a scope: bandwidth, rise time, sampling and triggering

A digital storage oscilloscope digitizes the input and stores it in memory; a 12-bit converter gives 4096 vertical levels. Stored records can be expanded, averaged, transformed to spectra and sent to a computer. Real-time sampling captures a whole waveform from a single trigger, so it is the only choice for a one-shot event such as an impact.

A sampling oscilloscope works like a stroboscope on a repetitive signal too fast for its amplifiers: it takes one sample per cycle, each a little later in the cycle than the last, and assembles a slowed-down replica. Equivalent-time sampling of this kind reaches bandwidths of gigahertz.

Choosing a scope starts from the signal, not the catalogue:

  • One-shot or repetitive? One-shot needs real-time sampling at a high rate.
  • Fastest edge. A first-order system’s 10–90 % rise time and bandwidth are linked by \(f_{BW}\approx0.35/t_r\). A 3.5 ns edge needs about 100 MHz.
  • Rise times add in quadrature: \(t_{shown}=\sqrt{t_{signal}^2+t_{scope}^2}\). A scope five times faster than the signal shows its edge only 2 % slow.
  • Vertical sensitivity and range: the smallest volts per division needed, and DC offset to view a small ripple riding on a large level (1 V of variation on 40 V needs 40 V of offset).
  • Time base and record length: the longest and shortest times to view set the memory: samples = sampling rate × record time.
  • Triggering: level and slope, single sweep for transients, and qualified triggers (too wide, too narrow, missing pulse) to catch rare faults.

A pulse edge truly rises in 10 ns. It is viewed on a 35 MHz scope. Roughly what rise time does the screen show?

Worked example · with simulator

How fast must a scope be to show a 12 MHz square wave?

Square wave from harmonicsideal square wavesum of harmonics kept

A serial data line carries 12 Mbit/s for frames 1 ms long. Treat it as a 12 MHz square wave. What bandwidth, sampling rate and memory must a digital scope have to show one frame with recognisable edges?

  1. A square wave of frequency \(f\) is a sum of sines at \(f, 3f, 5f, \dots\) with amplitudes \(1, \tfrac13, \tfrac15,\dots\). The simulator shows that keeping up to the 5th harmonic gives a recognisable square shape.
  2. Highest frequency to pass: \(5\times12\ \text{MHz}=60\) MHz, so the scope’s bandwidth must be at least 60 MHz.
  3. A sampled record can only represent frequencies below half the sampling rate (the sampling theorem, treated in Topic 05). So \(f_s\ge2\times60\ \text{MHz}=120\times10^{6}\) samples/s.
  4. Memory for one frame: \(120\times10^{6}\ \text{s}^{-1}\times10^{-3}\ \text{s}=120\,000\) samples.
  5. What this means: scope bandwidth is set by the harmonics, not the fundamental. Real scopes sample at 4 to 5 times their bandwidth for cleaner pictures.

Recorders and counters · worked example

Keeping a record, and counting pulses against a crystal clock

Recorders. A strip-chart recorder is a self-balancing potentiometer: a servo motor keeps moving the slider (and a pen) to the null point, so the trace is a loading-free record of a slow voltage, useful up to about 5 Hz, for example for thermocouples. Today most records are digital: data loggers, digitizers and storage scopes write directly to memory.

Counters. Almost every frequency measurement is a count of events in a known time. An electronic counter has four blocks: input conditioning (turns the signal into clean pulses), a crystal time base, a gate that opens for a set time, and a counter with display.

Frequency and period modes$$f=\frac{N}{T_g}\ \ (\pm1\ \text{count}),\qquad T=\frac{N}{f_{clock}}\ \ (\pm1\ \text{count})$$

An engine runs at about 1500 rpm. A counter measures its speed. (a) A magnetic pickup faces a 60-tooth wheel; the counter uses a 1 s gate. (b) A single mark gives one pulse per revolution; 1 s gate, then 10 s gate. (c) The one-per-revolution signal is timed in period mode with a 1 MHz time base. Find the reading and its ±1-count uncertainty each time.

  1. (a) Pulse rate \(60\times1500/60=1500\) Hz; in 1 s, \(N=1500\pm1\). With 60 teeth and a 1 s gate the count is the speed in rpm: 1500 ± 1 rpm, ±0.07 %.
  2. (b) 25 Hz gives \(N=25\pm1\) in 1 s: ±4 %, ±60 rpm. A 10 s gate gives \(250\pm1\): ±0.4 %, but the reading is ten times slower to arrive.
  3. (c) One period is \(1/25=0.04\) s, so \(N=0.04\times10^6=40\,000\pm1\) clock counts: ±0.0025 %. Speed \(=60/T=1500.0\) rpm.
  4. What this means: for low frequencies measure the period; for high frequencies count in a gate. A stopwatch and a revolution counter, with about 0.2 s of human reaction time, would need a run of minutes for the same accuracy.
