Second-Order Systems

Where we are

First-order sensors creep up on the answer; now add inertia

Part A modelled an instrument as a differential equation whose order is its highest derivative. A first-order instrument, such as a thermometer, obeys \(\tau\dot y+y=KF(t)\). It stores energy but has no inertia. Its main results were:

InputFirst-order result
Step\(\Gamma=e^{-t/\tau}\): 63.2 % at τ, 90 % at 2.3τ
Sine, frequency ω\(M=1/\sqrt{1+(\omega\tau)^2}\), \(\Phi=-\tan^{-1}\omega\tau\)

Here \(K\) is the static sensitivity, \(M\) the magnitude ratio (output amplitude divided by the amplitude a perfect instrument would give) and \(\Phi\) the phase shift.

A first-order reading can never overshoot. Its rate of change is proportional to the error still left, so it slows down as it nears the answer and arrives gently.

What changes in this part. Instruments with a moving mass, such as accelerometers, pressure transducers with a diaphragm and microphones, carry momentum. The moving part reaches the correct position still travelling, overshoots, is pulled back by the spring, and swings to and fro until friction removes the energy. A second derivative appears in the model, and two new numbers describe the instrument.

A car crossing a speed bump. The body is the mass, the springs pull it back and the shock absorbers (dampers) eat the energy. Worn shock absorbers: the car bounces several times. Very stiff ones: a harsh, slow return. A well-tuned car bounces about once. An accelerometer faces exactly the same trade-off.

Order 2 · build the model

An accelerometer is a small mass on a spring inside a box

  1. Let \(x(t)\) be the body (and housing) position and \(y(t)\) the mass position, both upward from rest. The spring is stretched by \(y-x\); the damper is sheared at speed \(\dot y-\dot x\).
  2. Newton’s second law on the mass: \(m\ddot y=-k(y-x)-c(\dot y-\dot x)\).
  3. The crystal senses the relative motion \(z=y-x\). Substitute \(y=z+x\), so \(\ddot y=\ddot z+\ddot x\): $$m\ddot z+c\dot z+kz=-m\ddot x$$
  4. Output terms are on the left; the input on the right is the body’s acceleration \(a=\ddot x\). In the general model this is \(a_2=m,\ a_1=c,\ a_0=k\): second order.
  5. At steady acceleration, \(kz=-ma\): the spring deflection is proportional to acceleration, \(z=-(m/k)\,a\). That is why the device measures acceleration. (The minus sign says only that the mass lags behind: accelerate the box upward and the spring is compressed.)

Assumptions: one rigid mass, a linear spring, damping force proportional to relative speed (viscous damping), motion along one axis.

Order 2 · the two numbers

Divide by the stiffness and two numbers appear: ωₙ and ζ

Every second-order instrument obeys \(a_2\ddot y+a_1\dot y+a_0y=F(t)\). Divide by \(a_0\) and name the coefficients:

Second-order standard form $$\frac{1}{\omega_n^2}\ddot y+\frac{2\zeta}{\omega_n}\dot y+y=K\,F(t)$$ $$\omega_n=\sqrt{\frac{a_0}{a_2}},\qquad \zeta=\frac{a_1}{2\sqrt{a_0a_2}},\qquad K=\frac{1}{a_0}$$
\(\omega_n\)natural frequency, rad/s: the frequency at which the system would oscillate for ever if it had no damping \(\zeta\)damping ratio, dimensionless: actual damping ÷ the damping that just stops oscillation \(K\)static sensitivity: output per unit input once everything is steady

For the accelerometer, \(a_2=m\), \(a_1=c\), \(a_0=k\):

\(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m},\quad \zeta=\dfrac{c}{2\sqrt{km}},\quad |K|=\dfrac{m}{k}=\dfrac{1}{\omega_n^2}\)

Where does \(\sqrt{k/m}\) come from? With no damping and no input, \(m\ddot z=-kz\). A sine \(z=\sin\omega t\) satisfies this only if \(m\omega^2=k\). The quantity \(c_c=2\sqrt{km}\) is the critical damping coefficient; we will see that \(c=c_c\) is exactly where oscillation stops, so \(\zeta=c/c_c\).

Numbers. A seismic mass \(m=10\) g on a spring \(k=1.1\times10^4\) N/m gives \(\omega_n=\sqrt{1.1\times10^6}=1049\) rad/s (\(f_n=\omega_n/2\pi=167\) Hz). For \(\zeta=0.7\) the damper needs \(c=2(0.7)\sqrt{(1.1\times10^4)(0.010)}=14.7\) N·s/m.

You make the seismic mass four times heavier, keeping k and c. What happens?

Order 2 · free motion

Released from a displaced position, the mass can do three different things

Before any input, ask how the system moves on its own. Set \(F=0\) and try \(y=e^{\lambda t}\). Each derivative brings out a factor λ, so the characteristic equation is

Characteristic equation and its roots $$\frac{\lambda^2}{\omega_n^2}+\frac{2\zeta}{\omega_n}\lambda+1=0\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda_{1,2}=-\zeta\omega_n\pm\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}$$

The sign under the root decides everything:

DampingRootsFree (transient) motion
\(\zeta<1\), underdampedcomplex\(Ce^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\sin(\omega_d t+\theta)\), with \(\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\)
\(\zeta=1\), critically dampedequal, \(-\omega_n\)\((C_1+C_2t)\,e^{-\omega_n t}\)
\(\zeta>1\), overdampedtwo real, negative\(C_1e^{\lambda_1t}+C_2e^{\lambda_2t}\)

\(C, \theta, C_1, C_2\) are fixed by the starting position and speed.

A door with a hydraulic closer. Too little oil: it slams and bounces (underdamped). Far too much: it creeps shut over half a minute (overdamped). Adjusted just right, it closes quickly and does not bounce (critical).

Order 2 · step input

Hit it with a sudden step and the damping ratio sets the shape

Apply \(F(t)=A\,U(t)\) (a jump from 0 to \(A\) at \(t=0\)) to a system at rest, \(y(0)=\dot y(0)=0\). The answer is the steady value \(KA\) plus the free motion of the previous slide, with constants chosen to start at rest:

Second-order step response $$\zeta<1:\ \ \frac{y}{KA}=1-e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\Big[\cos\omega_d t+\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\sin\omega_d t\Big]$$ $$\zeta=1:\ \ \frac{y}{KA}=1-(1+\omega_n t)\,e^{-\omega_n t}$$ $$\zeta>1:\ \ \frac{y}{KA}=1-\frac{\zeta+\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}}{2\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}}e^{\lambda_1t}+\frac{\zeta-\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}}{2\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}}e^{\lambda_2t}$$

with \(\lambda_{1,2}=(-\zeta\pm\sqrt{\zeta^2-1})\,\omega_n\). Check the first one: at \(t=0\) the bracket is 1, so \(y=0\); its slope there is also zero, as it must be for a mass starting at rest.

Time appears only as \(\omega_nt\). One chart in units of \(\omega_n t\) therefore serves every second-order instrument: a faster instrument (larger ωₙ) simply runs through the same curve in less time.

Order 2 · ringing

Turn the damping knob and watch the mass ring

Simulator

When \(\zeta<1\) the output oscillates about \(KA\) at the ringing frequency

Ringing (damped natural) frequency and period $$\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2},\qquad T_d=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_d}$$

Ringing is a property of the instrument, not of the input: any sudden disturbance makes it ring at \(\omega_d\), slightly below \(\omega_n\). The oscillation shrinks inside the envelope \(\pm e^{-\zeta\omega_nt}/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\), so \(1/\zeta\omega_n\) acts like a time constant for the decay.

The first peak comes half a period after the step, at \(t=\pi/\omega_d\). Substituting gives the overshoot, the peak excess as a fraction of the step:

\(\text{OS}=e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\)

Start at ζ = 0.25: about 44 % overshoot and many swings. Move to 0.7: one small overshoot of 4.6 %. At 1.0 the overshoot vanishes; at 2.0 the rise becomes slow. Watch the readout: the 90 % rise time grows steadily with ζ, but the settling time is shortest near ζ ≈ 0.6–0.7.

Order 2 · how long to wait

Little damping rises fast but settles late: ζ ≈ 0.7 balances the two

The rise time \(t_r\) of a second-order system is the time to first reach 90 % of the change \(KA-y(0)\). The settling time \(t_s\) is the time after which the output stays within ±10 % of the change around \(KA\) (tighter bands, ±5 % or ±2 %, are also quoted).

All times scale with \(1/\omega_n\). The table is computed from the step-response formulas; multiply by \(1/\omega_n\) to get seconds.

Instruments meant to follow sudden changes are usually built with ζ between about 0.6 and 0.8.

Order 2 · from data to ζ and ωₙ

Two successive peaks give the damping; their spacing gives the frequency

A step test of an underdamped instrument shows peaks that shrink at a fixed ratio. Measure each peak from the final value: \(y_1=(y_{\max})_1-y_\infty\), \(y_2=(y_{\max})_2-y_\infty\), for two successive peaks on the same side.

  1. The deviation from the final value is an oscillation inside the envelope ±e−ζωₙt/√(1−ζ²). Same-side peaks are one period \(T_d\) apart.
  2. So their ratio is \(\dfrac{y_1}{y_2}=e^{\zeta\omega_nT_d}=e^{2\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\), using \(T_d=2\pi/(\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2})\).
  3. Take logs and call \(\Lambda=\ln(y_1/y_2)\), the logarithmic decrement: \(\Lambda\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}=2\pi\zeta\).
  4. Square and solve for ζ: $$\zeta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\big(2\pi/\Lambda\big)^2}}$$
  5. The peak spacing gives \(T_d\), hence \(\omega_d=2\pi/T_d\) and \(\omega_n=\omega_d/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\).

Why ratios? The envelope is an exponential, and an exponential loses the same fraction in every equal time interval, exactly like the first-order error fraction Γ of part A. A ratio of two peaks cancels the step size \(KA\) and the sensitivity, so the result does not depend on calibration.

Quick check. If each positive peak is one fifth of the one before, \(\Lambda=\ln5=1.609\), \(2\pi/\Lambda=3.90\) and \(\zeta=1/\sqrt{1+15.2}=0.248\).

With only one overshoot visible, use the overshoot instead: \(\text{OS}=e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\) inverts to \(\zeta=\dfrac{-\ln \text{OS}}{\sqrt{\pi^2+\ln^2\text{OS}}}\).

Do not mistake \(\omega_d\), read off the trace, for \(\omega_n\). They differ by the factor \(\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\): 3 % at ζ = 0.25 but 29 % at ζ = 0.7.

Order 2 · periodic input

Feed it a sine wave: the output may come out bigger, not only smaller

Apply \(F(t)=A\sin\omega t\). After the transient dies, the output is a sine at the same frequency, \(y=B\sin(\omega t+\Phi)\). Write \(r=\omega/\omega_n\), the frequency ratio.

  1. Substitute \(y\), \(\dot y=B\omega\cos(\cdot)\), \(\ddot y=-B\omega^2\sin(\cdot)\) into the standard form: \(B(1-r^2)\sin(\omega t+\Phi)+2\zeta rB\cos(\omega t+\Phi)=KA\sin\omega t\).
  2. Expand and match the \(\sin\omega t\) and \(\cos\omega t\) terms: \(B[(1-r^2)\cos\Phi-2\zeta r\sin\Phi]=KA\) and \((1-r^2)\sin\Phi+2\zeta r\cos\Phi=0\).
  3. The second gives \(\tan\Phi=-2\zeta r/(1-r^2)\). Squaring and adding gives \(B^2[(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2]=(KA)^2\).
Second-order frequency response $$M(\omega)=\frac{B}{KA}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\big[1-r^2\big]^2+\big[2\zeta r\big]^2}}$$ $$\Phi(\omega)=-\tan^{-1}\frac{2\zeta r}{1-r^2},\qquad r=\frac{\omega}{\omega_n}$$

Take Φ between 0 and −180°: the phase passes −90° at r = 1, so use the quadrant of the point \((1-r^2,\,2\zeta r)\).

Three facts to remember.

  • \(r\ll1\): \(M\approx1\), \(\Phi\approx0\). The input passes through unchanged.
  • \(r=1\): \(M=1/2\zeta\) and \(\Phi=-90^\circ\) for every ζ. With ζ = 0.1, the instrument reports five times the true amplitude.
  • \(r\gg1\): \(M\approx1/r^2\), \(\Phi\to-180^\circ\). The mass cannot follow; the signal is filtered out.

Keep three frequencies apart. \(\omega\) belongs to the input signal. \(\omega_n\) and \(\omega_d\) belong to the instrument: \(\omega_n\) is set by mass and stiffness, \(\omega_d\) is what you see ringing after a step.

Pushing a child on a swing. Push in time with the swing’s own rhythm and small pushes build a huge swing. Push much faster than its rhythm and the swing hardly moves, always opposite to your hands (−180°).

Order 2 · resonance

Below, at and above resonance: where an instrument is honest

Frequency responseyour ζζ = 0.7

Set ζ = 0.2 and slide the frequency to the peak: about 2.5 times too big. At r = 1 the phase is −90°. Raise ζ past 0.71 and the peak disappears. The dashed ζ = 0.7 curve stays inside the green ±5 % band up to r ≈ 0.6.

Where is \(M\) largest? Minimise the denominator: \(\frac{d}{dr}\big[(1-r^2)^2+4\zeta^2r^2\big]=-4r(1-r^2)+8\zeta^2r=0\) gives \(r^2=1-2\zeta^2\).

Resonance frequency and peak $$\omega_R=\omega_n\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2},\qquad M_{\max}=\frac{1}{2\zeta\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$$

A peak exists only if \(1-2\zeta^2>0\), i.e. \(\zeta<0.707\). The resonance frequency \(\omega_R\) is where a steady sine is most amplified; it is lower than \(\omega_d\), which is free ringing.

The transmission band is the low-frequency range where \(M\approx1\): measure here. The resonance band is around \(\omega_R\), where \(M>1\). The filter band is at high frequency, where \(M\) is below 0.707 (−3 dB) and the signal is attenuated.

Worked example 2

From a ringing test to a full frequency response

A step test of a pressure transducer shows ringing at \(f_d=1200\) Hz, and the peak ratio gives \(\zeta=0.5\). Find ωₙ, the resonance frequency, and \(M\) and \(\Phi\) over a range of input frequencies.

Known: \(\zeta=0.5\), \(f_d=1200\) Hz. Assumptions: second order; linear.

  1. \(\omega_d=2\pi(1200)=7540\) rad/s.
  2. \(\omega_n=\omega_d/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}=7540/0.866=8706\) rad/s.
  3. \(\omega_R=\omega_n\sqrt{1-2(0.25)}=8706\times0.707=6156\) rad/s; \(M_{\max}=1/(2\times0.5\times0.866)=1.155\).

  5. Meaning: up to about 2600 rad/s (414 Hz, \(r\approx0.3\)) the error stays under about 4 %. Near 6000–7500 rad/s the transducer reads 11–15 % high. Far above ωₙ it reads almost nothing.

Worked example 3

Selecting an accelerometer for motion below 100 Hz

An accelerometer must measure a motion whose important frequencies all lie below 100 Hz, with a dynamic error no larger than 5 %. Select acceptable values of ωₙ and ζ.

Known: \(f\le100\) Hz, so \(\omega\le2\pi(100)=628\) rad/s; \(|\delta|=|M-1|\le0.05\). Find: ωₙ, ζ. Assumptions: second-order instrument.

  1. Requirement: \(0.95\le M(\omega)\le1.05\) for every \(0\le\omega\le628\) rad/s.
  2. Two unknowns, one requirement: many designs work. Fix one. Choose \(\zeta=0.7\): its peak is \(M_{\max}=1.0002\), so \(M\) never exceeds 1.05 and only the lower limit matters.
  3. With ζ = 0.7, \(1/M^2=(1-r^2)^2+1.96r^2=1-0.04r^2+r^4\). Set \(M=0.95\): \(r^4-0.04r^2-0.1080=0\).
  4. Quadratic in \(r^2\): \(r^2=\tfrac12\big[0.04+\sqrt{0.0016+0.4321}\big]=0.3493\), so \(r_{\max}=0.591\). (Reading the drawn curve by eye gives about 0.6.)
  5. Need \(628/\omega_n\le0.591\): \(\omega_n\ge1063\) rad/s, i.e. \(f_n\ge169\) Hz. The eye-read 0.6 would give 1047 rad/s, which is 5.3 % low at 100 Hz: just outside the limit.
  6. One acceptable design: ζ = 0.7 and ωₙ ≥ 1063 rad/s. At 100 Hz it lags by about 52°, a time delay of 1.4 ms.

For an accelerometer \(|K|=1/\omega_n^2\). With \(\omega_n=1063\) rad/s, 1 g (9.81 m/s²) deflects the mass only \(9.81/1063^2=8.7\ \mu\)m. A higher ωₙ widens the band but shrinks the signal, which is why accelerometers use stiff piezoelectric crystals that turn micrometres into a measurable charge.

Design choice

Why so many instruments are built with ζ ≈ 0.7

1. The flattest magnitude curve. Put \(\zeta=1/\sqrt2=0.707\) in the denominator of \(M\):

\((1-r^2)^2+4(0.5)r^2=1-2r^2+r^4+2r^2=1+r^4\)

so \(M=1/\sqrt{1+r^4}\). The \(r^2\) terms cancel: \(M\) departs from 1 only as \(r^4\), as slowly as any second-order curve can, without a resonance peak. This is called a maximally flat response. Its −3 dB point is exactly \(r=1\).

2. Fast settling. The step response overshoots only 4.6 % and settles to ±5 % in 2.9/ωₙ (critical damping: 4.7/ωₙ).

3. Nearly linear phase. Over \(0\le r\le1\) the phase of ζ ≈ 0.7 grows almost in proportion to frequency, so all frequency components are delayed by about the same time and a complex waveform keeps its shape (part C).

Tuning shock absorbers: soft enough that the car does not feel every pebble late, firm enough that it never wallows. Engineers land in the same narrow window for the same reason.

One more, by hand. A second-order instrument is modelled by \(\ddot y+400\,\dot y+2.5\times10^5\,y=2.5\times10^5\,F(t)\) (time in s). Find ωₙ, ζ, K, the ringing frequency and the amplitude it reports for a 1-unit sine at 60 Hz.

Divide by \(2.5\times10^5\): \(1/\omega_n^2=4\times10^{-6}\), so \(\omega_n=500\) rad/s; \(2\zeta/\omega_n=1.6\times10^{-3}\), so \(\zeta=0.40\); \(K=1\). \(\omega_d=500\sqrt{1-0.16}=458\) rad/s (72.9 Hz). At 60 Hz, \(\omega=377\) rad/s, \(r=0.754\): \(M=1/\sqrt{(1-0.569)^2+(0.603)^2}=1/\sqrt{0.186+0.364}=1.35\). It reports 1.35 units, 35 % high, because 60 Hz lies in the resonance band.
