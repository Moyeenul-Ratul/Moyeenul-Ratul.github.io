Boolean Algebra, Combinational Logic and Flip-Flops

Gates decide; flip-flops remember. With a little algebra you can turn a safety rule into the smallest possible circuit, and with a few flip-flops you can count pulses, tell which way a shaft turns and measure frequency.

Where we are

Part A gave the alphabet; this part writes sentences and adds memory

Part A showed that a bit is a HIGH or LOW voltage, that numbers and characters are coded as rows of bits (binary, 2's complement, BCD, Gray, ASCII), and that seven gates (NOT, AND, OR, NAND, NOR, XOR, XNOR) each produce an output fixed by a truth table. In Boolean notation \(\overline{A}\) is NOT, \(AB\) is AND and \(A+B\) is OR.

Two questions remain. First, given a word statement such as "trip the compressor if any two of three sensors agree", how do you find the circuit, and the smallest one? That is Boolean algebra and the Karnaugh map. Second, how does a circuit count pulses, which needs it to remember what came before? That needs a new element, the flip-flop.

In a combinational circuit the outputs depend only on the inputs present now. In a sequential circuit the outputs also depend on past inputs, because the circuit stores a state.

A pocket calculator's adder is combinational: press 2 + 3 and the answer depends only on those keys. A hand tally counter at a factory gate is sequential: pressing it once gives a different display each time, because it remembers how many people already passed.

Boolean algebra

Twelve rules that let you shrink a logic expression

Boolean algebra is the algebra of variables that can only be 0 or 1. A literal is a variable or its complement (\(A\) or \(\overline{A}\)). The ordinary commutative (\(A+B=B+A\), \(AB=BA\)), associative (\(A+(B+C)=(A+B)+C\)) and distributive (\(A(B+C)=AB+AC\)) laws hold, plus the rules below. Each can be proved by trying the two values of \(A\), or seen with switches: a switch that is always closed (1) in parallel makes the branch always conduct.

#Rule#Rule
1\(A+0=A\)7\(A\cdot A=A\)
2\(A+1=1\)8\(A\cdot\overline{A}=0\)
3\(A\cdot0=0\)9\(\overline{\overline{A}}=A\)
4\(A\cdot1=A\)10\(A+AB=A\)
5\(A+A=A\)11\(A+\overline{A}B=A+B\)
6\(A+\overline{A}=1\)12\((A+B)(A+C)=A+BC\)

Rules 1–9 follow from the gate truth tables. The last three are the workhorses of simplification; reveal how each follows from the earlier ones.

  1. Rule 10. \(A+AB=A(1+B)=A\cdot1=A\) (distributive law, then rules 2 and 4). In words: "alarm if A, or if A and B" is just "alarm if A".
  2. Rule 11. Start with \(A+\overline{A}B\). Replace \(A\) by \(A+AB\) (rule 10): \(A+AB+\overline{A}B\).
  3. Factor the last two terms: \(A+B(A+\overline{A})=A+B\cdot1=A+B\) (rules 6 and 4).
  4. Rule 12. Multiply out: \((A+B)(A+C)=AA+AC+AB+BC=A+AC+AB+BC\) (rule 7).
  5. Factor \(A\): \(A(1+C+B)+BC=A\cdot1+BC=A+BC\) (rules 2 and 4).

Boolean algebra is not ordinary algebra. \(A+A=A\), not \(2A\), and \(A+1=1\). There is no subtraction or division, so you cannot cancel a term from both sides: \(A+B=A+C\) does not imply \(B=C\) (try \(A=1\)).

Boolean algebra

DeMorgan's theorems: break the bar, change the sign

DeMorgan's theorems $$\overline{AB}=\overline{A}+\overline{B}\qquad\qquad \overline{A+B}=\overline{A}\,\overline{B}$$

In words: the complement of a product is the sum of the complements, and the complement of a sum is the product of the complements. To apply them, break the long bar over a group, put a bar over each piece, and swap AND with OR. They extend to any number of variables: \(\overline{XYZ}=\overline{X}+\overline{Y}+\overline{Z}\).

AB\(\overline{AB}\)\(\overline{A}+\overline{B}\)\(\overline{A+B}\)\(\overline{A}\,\overline{B}\)
001111
011100
101100
110000

Proof by exhaustion: the columns agree for all four input combinations, so the expressions are equal.

The gate pictures say the same thing. A NAND gate gives a HIGH output when A is LOW or B is LOW, so it can be drawn as an OR gate with inverting bubbles on its inputs, a negative-OR. A NOR gives HIGH only when A and B are LOW: a negative-AND.

Three doors on a test cell each have a limit switch that outputs LOW when its door is closed (an active-LOW signal, A, B, C). A "cell ready" lamp needs a HIGH when all three doors are closed. Find the expression and the single gate.

  1. "All closed" means A = 0 and B = 0 and C = 0, so \(X=\overline{A}\,\overline{B}\,\overline{C}\): an AND gate with three inverted inputs.
  2. DeMorgan backwards: \(\overline{A}\,\overline{B}\,\overline{C}=\overline{A+B+C}\). That is one 3-input NOR gate; no separate inverters are needed.
  3. Check: if any door opens, its input goes HIGH, \(A+B+C=1\), and the NOR output falls LOW: lamp off.

What is \(\overline{A\,\overline{B}}\) after DeMorgan?

Worked example

A compressor trip circuit: five gates become two

A compressor protection circuit has three sensor inputs: \(A\) = oil pressure low, \(B\) = discharge temperature high, \(C\) = motor current high (1 = abnormal). A previous designer wired the trip signal as \(X=AB+A(B+C)+B(B+C)\), the circuit below. Find the simplest equivalent circuit.

  1. Known: \(X=AB+A(B+C)+B(B+C)\). Find: the minimum form. Tools: the laws and rules of slide 3.
  2. Distributive law on both brackets: \(X=AB+AB+AC+BB+BC\).
  3. Rule 5 (\(AB+AB=AB\)) and rule 7 (\(BB=B\)): \(X=AB+AC+B+BC\).
  4. Rule 10 (\(B+BC=B\)): \(X=AB+AC+B\).
  5. Rule 10 again (\(AB+B=B\), since \(B+BA=B\)): \(X=B+AC\).
  6. Meaning: trip on high discharge temperature alone, or on low oil pressure together with high motor current. The original 5 gates (and 11 gate inputs) become one AND and one OR. Fewer gates mean fewer wires, fewer parts that can fail, and less delay.

Always check a simplification with a truth table: both forms give X = 1 for inputs 010, 011, 101, 110, 111 and X = 0 otherwise.

Design from a specification

From a truth table to a sum of products: two-out-of-three voting

A product term is an AND of literals (\(A\overline{B}C\)); a sum term is an OR of literals. A sum of products (SOP) is an OR of product terms, built from AND gates feeding one OR gate. A product of sums (POS) is an AND of sum terms. In the standard form every term contains every variable; such a product term is a minterm, and it is 1 for exactly one row of the truth table.

Recipe: for every row where the output is 1, write the minterm that is 1 on that row (variable plain if it is 1 in that row, barred if it is 0), then OR them. For the POS form, use the rows where the output is 0 and write sum terms the opposite way (plain for 0, barred for 1).

The problem. A reactor has three independent over-temperature switches A, B, C. One switch may fail, stuck at 0 or 1. To avoid both false trips and missed trips, the shutdown signal X must be 1 when at least two switches read 1. This two-out-of-three voting is standard in safety systems.

  1. Standard SOP from the four 1-rows: \(X=\overline{A}BC+A\overline{B}C+AB\overline{C}+ABC\).
  2. Rule 5 lets us copy \(ABC\) twice more (\(ABC=ABC+ABC+ABC\)) and pair it with each other term: \(X=(\overline{A}+A)BC+(\overline{B}+B)AC+(\overline{C}+C)AB\).
  3. Each bracket is 1 (rule 6): \(X=AB+BC+AC\). Three 2-input ANDs and one 3-input OR.
  4. Check: one switch stuck at 1 cannot trip alone (each term needs two), and one stuck at 0 cannot block a trip when the other two read 1. The Karnaugh map on the next slide finds this answer by eye.

Karnaugh maps

The Karnaugh map: a truth table folded so that neighbours differ in one variable

A Karnaugh map (K-map) is a truth table drawn as a grid with one cell per input combination: 8 cells for three variables, 16 for four. Rows and columns are labelled in Gray code order (00, 01, 11, 10), so any two cells that touch, including across the outer edges, differ in exactly one variable.

Why that helps: two adjacent 1s, such as \(AB\overline{C}\) and \(ABC\), combine as \(AB(\overline{C}+C)=AB\). The variable that changes inside a group drops out. A group of 2 cells removes one variable, 4 cells remove two, 8 cells remove three.

Grouping rules.

  1. Groups are rectangles of 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16 cells containing only 1s (or don't-cares, next slide).
  2. Make each group as large as possible, and use as few groups as possible.
  3. Every 1 must be in at least one group; groups may overlap.
  4. The map wraps round: the top row touches the bottom row, the left column touches the right.
  5. Read each group by keeping only the variables that stay constant in it; OR the groups.

The voting map has 1s in cells \(\overline{A}BC\), \(A\overline{B}C\), \(AB\overline{C}\), \(ABC\). Three pairs cover them, each sharing the \(ABC\) cell. In the orange pair \(C\) changes, so it reads \(AB\); the blue pair loses \(A\) (\(BC\)); the green pair loses \(B\) (\(AC\)). The result, \(X=AB+BC+AC\), matches the algebra of the previous slide in a fraction of the time.

A K-map is a seating plan where neighbours are guaranteed to have all but one thing in common. Find a block of neighbours who all want the same thing, and you can address them by what they share and ignore what differs.

Worked example · four variables

Driving one segment of a digital panel meter, with don't-cares

A digital panel meter shows each BCD digit (inputs \(A\,B\,C\,D\), A the MSB) on a seven-segment display, seven LED bars named a to g. Segment a, the top bar, is lit for the digits 0, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and dark for 1 and 4. Find the minimum SOP expression for a.

A don't-care (X) is an input combination that can never occur, so you may treat it as 1 or 0, whichever gives larger groups. BCD never uses 1010 to 1111 (10 to 15), so those six cells are don't-cares.

  1. Known: 1s at cells 0, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9; 0s at 1, 4; X at 10–15. Find: minimum SOP.
  2. Orange, 8 cells: the bottom two rows (A = 1: cells 8, 9 and X's). Only A is constant: term A.
  3. Blue, 8 cells: the right two columns (C = 1: cells 2, 3, 6, 7 and X's). Term C.
  4. Green, 4 cells: 5, 7, 13, 15, the middle square (B = 1, D = 1). Term BD.
  5. Yellow, 4 corners: 0, 2, 8, 10 touch by wrap-round both ways (B = 0, D = 0). Term \(\overline{B}\,\overline{D}\).
  6. \(a=A+C+BD+\overline{B}\,\overline{D}\): 6 literals. Had the don't-cares been taken as 0, the best answer would be \(\overline{A}\,\overline{B}\,\overline{D}+\overline{A}BD+\overline{A}C+A\overline{B}\,\overline{C}\), with 11 literals. Using impossible inputs wisely nearly halves the circuit.

Combinational building blocks

NAND builds everything; decoders, encoders and multiplexers route signals

NAND and NOR are called universal gates: any logic function can be built from NANDs alone (or NORs alone). That is why a single 74HC00 package and a few spare gates can patch almost any circuit.

A decoder has \(n\) input lines and up to \(2^n\) outputs; exactly one output, the one whose number equals the input code, is active. Each output is one minterm. A 3-to-8 decoder (74HC138) turns a 3-bit channel number into "enable channel 5". An encoder does the reverse: it outputs the code of whichever input is active. A priority encoder reports the highest-numbered active input if several are active at once, for example which of eight limit switches along a slide was reached first in the sequence.

A multiplexer (MUX, data selector) has \(2^n\) data inputs, \(n\) select lines and one output; the select code chooses which input reaches the output. A demultiplexer (DEMUX) sends one input to one of \(2^n\) outputs.

4-to-1 multiplexer $$Y=D_0\overline{S_1}\,\overline{S_0}+D_1\overline{S_1}S_0+D_2S_1\overline{S_0}+D_3S_1S_0$$

Each term is a data input ANDed with one select minterm, so only the selected input can reach the OR. The analog multiplexer of a data-acquisition card (part E) uses exactly this decoding, but the AND gates are replaced by analog switches that connect one sensor at a time to the single converter.

Sequential logic

The latch: two gates that hold each other in place

Connect the output of each of two NOR gates back to an input of the other. If Q is 1, it holds the lower gate's output at 0, which in turn keeps Q at 1. If Q is 0, the opposite pair of levels holds itself just as firmly. The circuit has two stable states, so it stores one bit. It is a latch (a bistable element). \(\overline{Q}\) is always the complement of \(Q\) in normal use.

SRQMeaning
00no changehold (memory)
010reset
101set
11invalidboth outputs 0; avoid

The S-R latch has a set input S and a reset input R. A gated D latch adds an enable input EN and a single data input D: while EN = 1 the output Q follows D (the latch is transparent); when EN goes to 0, Q keeps the last value of D. The invalid state cannot occur.

A measurement use: debouncing a limit switch. When a mechanical contact closes, its metal parts bounce apart and together several times over a few milliseconds. A counter connected directly would count each bounce as a separate event. Wire the two contacts of a changeover switch to the S and R inputs (with pull-down resistors): the first touch on the S contact sets the latch, and the bounces that follow only "set" an already-set latch, so Q makes one clean step.

A latch is a light switch with a spring-loaded catch. Once you push it on, it stays on by itself; pressing "on" again does nothing; only pressing "off" changes it. Pressing both at once is the jam you must not do.

Sequential logic

Flip-flops act only at the clock edge

An edge-triggered flip-flop changes state only at one edge of its clock input C: the rising edge (positive edge-triggered) or the falling edge. Between edges the inputs may change freely without affecting Q. The small triangle at C on the symbol marks an edge-triggered input; a bubble in front of it would mean the falling edge.

The D flip-flop copies D into Q at each active edge: it takes a snapshot. The J-K flip-flop adds a toggle mode: with J = K = 1, Q reverses at every edge, which halves the clock frequency. Asynchronous preset (PRE) and clear (CLR) inputs force Q to 1 or 0 at once, regardless of the clock, for example at power-up.

Real flip-flops need the input to be steady for a short window around the edge:

The set-up time \(t_s\) is how long D must be steady before the edge, the hold time \(t_h\) how long after it, and the propagation delay the time from the edge to the change of Q.

An incremental quadrature encoder gives two square waves, channels A and B, a quarter period apart. Turning one way, A rises before B; turning the other way, B rises first. Connect A to D and B to the clock: at each rising edge of B the flip-flop stores A. Forward, A is already HIGH (Q = 1); in reverse, A is still LOW (Q = 0). Q then drives the up/down input of a position counter.

Timing circuits

The 555 timer: a capacitor, two comparators and a latch

A one-shot (monostable multivibrator) outputs a single pulse of fixed width \(t_W\) each time it is triggered, then returns to its stable state. An astable multivibrator has no stable state and oscillates by itself: a pulse generator. The 555 timer IC does both.

The astable 555 is a cistern with a float valve. Water (charge) flows in until the float reaches the top mark, the flush opens and drains it to the bottom mark, the flush closes and filling restarts. The two marks are the two comparator levels.

  1. Charging law. A capacitor charging through resistance \(R\) toward \(V_{CC}\) from \(V_0\) follows \(v(t)=V_{CC}-(V_{CC}-V_0)\,e^{-t/RC}\).
  2. Solving for the time to reach \(V_1\): \(t=RC\ln\dfrac{V_{CC}-V_0}{V_{CC}-V_1}\).
  3. One-shot: C starts at 0 and the pulse ends at \(\tfrac23V_{CC}\): \(t_W=RC\ln3=1.0986\,RC\approx1.1\,RC\).
  4. Astable, output HIGH: C charges through \(R_1+R_2\) from \(\tfrac13\) to \(\tfrac23V_{CC}\): \(t_H=(R_1+R_2)C\ln\dfrac{2/3}{1/3}=0.693(R_1+R_2)C\).
  5. Output LOW: the discharge switch grounds the R1–R2 junction, and C discharges through \(R_2\) alone from \(\tfrac23\) to \(\tfrac13V_{CC}\): \(t_L=0.693R_2C\).
  6. So \(T=0.693(R_1+2R_2)C\), \(f=\dfrac{1}{T}\approx\dfrac{1.44}{(R_1+2R_2)C}\), duty cycle \(=\dfrac{R_1+R_2}{R_1+2R_2}\). The supply voltage cancels out, so the frequency does not drift with \(V_{CC}\).
  7. Example. \(R_1=2.2\) kΩ, \(R_2=4.7\) kΩ, \(C=0.022\) µF: \(t_H=0.693(6.9\text{ k}\Omega)(0.022\ \mu\text{F})=105.2\) µs, \(t_L=71.7\) µs, \(T=176.9\) µs.
  8. \(f=1/T=5.65\) kHz (the 1.44 rule gives 5.64 kHz); duty cycle \(=6.9/11.6=59.5\%\). Always above 50 %, because charging uses both resistors.
  9. One-shot example. \(R=2.2\) kΩ, \(C=0.01\) µF: \(t_W=1.1(2200)(0.01\times10^{-6})=24.2\) µs. In a plant, a one-shot stretches a 20 µs pulse from a photo-interrupter into one that a PLC input scanned every few milliseconds cannot miss.

Counting

Counters, shift registers, and how a frequency counter works

A toggling flip-flop halves its clock frequency. Chain three, each clocked by the previous one's output, and an 8 kHz input becomes \(8/2^3=1\) kHz. Read the outputs \(Q_0, Q_1, Q_2, \dots\) together and the chain is a binary counter: after every input pulse the word \(Q_{n-1}\dots Q_1Q_0\) is one larger. With \(n\) flip-flops it counts 0 to \(2^n-1\) and then wraps to 0, so counting up to 1000 events needs \(\lceil\log_2 1000\rceil=10\) flip-flops. In this ripple (asynchronous) counter each stage waits for the one before, so delays add up; a synchronous counter clocks all stages together. A decade counter resets after 9 and outputs one BCD digit, ready for a display.

A shift register is a row of D flip-flops sharing one clock, each feeding the next. Serial-in, parallel-out: bits arriving one per clock on a serial line are assembled into a byte, which is what the receiver of a serial link does. Parallel-in, serial-out does the reverse in the transmitter.

A frequency counter opens an AND gate for an accurately known gate time \(T_g\) (from a crystal oscillator) and counts the input pulses that pass. A Schmitt trigger first turns the sensor waveform into clean logic edges.

Frequency from a gated count $$f=\frac{N}{T_g}\qquad\text{resolution}=\pm\frac{1}{T_g}$$
\(N\)pulses counted during the gate \(T_g\)gate time, s

The gate opens at a random moment relative to the input, so a pulse just inside or just outside the window changes \(N\) by one: the ±1-count error.

A magnetic pickup faces a 60-tooth gear on a turbine shaft. With a 0.5 s gate the counter reads 1250. Find the frequency, the shaft speed and its resolution.

  1. \(f=N/T_g=1250/0.5=2500\) Hz.
  2. Each tooth gives one pulse, so speed \(=2500/60=41.67\) rev/s \(=2500\) rpm. (A 60-tooth gear makes hertz equal to rpm, which is why it is a popular choice.)
  3. ±1 count \(=\pm1/0.5=\pm2\) Hz \(=\pm2\) rpm, or \(\pm0.08\%\).
  4. At 10 Hz the same 1 s gate gives ±10 %. For slow signals, measure the period instead: count a fast clock (say 1 MHz) during one input cycle; the ±1 count is then ±1 µs on a 0.1 s period.

Self-test

Check yourself

Minimise \(X=\overline{A}\,\overline{B}C+\overline{A}BC+A\overline{B}C+AB\overline{C}+ABC\).

A J-K flip-flop has J = K = 1 and a 1 kHz clock. What is Q?

A frequency counter with a 0.1 s gate shows 437 counts. What are the frequency and its count resolution?

A 555 one-shot must stretch each pulse from a flow-meter reed switch to 0.1 s so that a slow PLC input never misses it. With a 10 µF capacitor, what resistor is needed?

\(t_W=1.1RC\), so \(R=t_W/(1.1C)=0.1/(1.1\times10\times10^{-6})=9.09\) kΩ. A standard 9.1 kΩ resistor gives \(t_W=1.1(9100)(10\times10^{-6})=0.100\) s. Check the timing: the reed switch must not pulse faster than once per 0.1 s, or the stretched pulses merge.
