Models and First-Order Systems

The question of this topic

A correct instrument can still give a wrong reading if the input moves

Measure the length of a board with a ruler. The length does not change while you look, so the reading is right the moment the ruler is in place. This is a static measurement: the input is constant during the measurement and only its size matters.

Now measure the vibration of a motor. The input changes in amplitude and in time, so the instrument must follow it. This is a dynamic measurement: we need the amplitude, the frequency content and the shape of the waveform.

A clinical thermometer shows that the two can mix. Your body temperature is constant, but the thermometer starts at room temperature. When you put it under your tongue, the input it feels jumps suddenly from about 20 °C to 37 °C. To the thermometer this is a dynamic event, and it needs time to absorb heat before it agrees with your body. Read it after five seconds and you would “measure” a fever-free 30 °C.

Every instrument has its own speed. This topic builds simple models that predict that speed, so you can tell when a reading can be trusted and how fast an instrument you must buy.

One model for all instruments

Every instrument is a differential equation in disguise

A lumped-parameter model replaces properties that are spread through a device (mass, stiffness, heat capacity) by a few ideal elements: one mass, one spring, one damper, one heat store. The governing equations then become ordinary, not partial, differential equations.

Apply a law of physics to the lumped model, put every output term on the left and every input term on the right, and you always get the same shape:

General linear instrument model $$a_n\frac{d^n y}{dt^n}+\dots+a_1\frac{dy}{dt}+a_0\,y = F(t)$$ $$F(t)=b_m\frac{d^m x}{dt^m}+\dots+b_1\frac{dx}{dt}+b_0\,x$$

Here \(x(t)\) is the measured quantity and \(y(t)\) the reading. The order \(n\) is the highest derivative of \(y\). The coefficients \(a_i, b_i\) are physical properties:

InstrumentLaw appliedCoefficients
Spring-piston pressure gaugeForce balance\(a_0=k\) (order 0)
Bulb thermometerEnergy balance\(a_1=mc_v,\ a_0=hA_s\) (order 1)
Seismic accelerometerNewton’s second law\(a_2=m,\ a_1=c,\ a_0=k\) (order 2)

When the input is steady, every derivative is zero and \(a_0y=b_0x\). So the reading per unit input at equilibrium, the static sensitivity, is always \(K=b_0/a_0\).

Order 0

Zeroth order: the instrument that answers instantly

If no derivative matters, the model is \(a_0y=F(t)\), or

Zeroth-order model $$y(t)=K\,F(t)$$

No storage, no inertia, so no lag: the reading follows the input at every instant. \(K\) is the slope of the static calibration curve, \(K=dy/dx\).

  1. Model. A pencil-type tyre gauge: tyre pressure pushes a piston of area \(A\) against a spring of stiffness \(k\). Ignore piston mass and friction.
  2. Force balance at equilibrium: \(pA = ky + p_{atm}A\).
  3. Solve: \(y=\dfrac{A}{k}\,(p-p_{atm})\). This is \(y=KF\) with \(K=A/k\): output displacement per unit gauge pressure.
  4. Numbers. \(D=8\) mm gives \(A=5.03\times10^{-5}\) m². With \(k=500\) N/m, \(K=1.01\times10^{-7}\) m/Pa \(=0.10\) mm/kPa, so 200 kPa moves the piston 20 mm.
  5. Limit. Because inertia and friction were dropped, this model says nothing about how the gauge behaves while the pressure is changing. Zeroth order describes any instrument at static equilibrium, and only there.

A pressure sensor reads 2.0 V at 100 kPa and 4.0 V at 300 kPa, linearly. What is \(K\)?

Order 1 · build the model

A thermometer obeys a first-order equation: here is why

Filling a bucket from a tank through a hose, where the flow depends on the difference in water level. Big difference, fast flow; nearly level, a trickle. “Rate of change proportional to what is still missing” is the signature of every first-order system.

  1. First law for the bulb: rate of energy stored = rate of heat in, \(\dfrac{dE}{dt}=\dot Q\).
  2. Storage raises the bulb temperature: \(\dfrac{dE}{dt}=mc_v\dfrac{dT}{dt}\). Convection brings heat in: \(\dot Q=hA_s\,(T_\infty-T)\). $$mc_v\frac{dT}{dt}=hA_s\,\big[T_\infty-T(t)\big]$$
  3. Output terms left, input right: \(mc_v\,\dot T+hA_s\,T=hA_s\,T_\infty\). So \(a_1=mc_v\), \(a_0=hA_s\), \(b_0=hA_s\).
  4. Divide by \(a_0=hA_s\): $$\underbrace{\frac{mc_v}{hA_s}}_{\tau}\frac{dT}{dt}+T=\underbrace{1}_{K}\cdot T_\infty$$
  5. Every first-order instrument has this form: \(\tau\dot y+y=KF(t)\) with \(\tau=a_1/a_0\). Units check: J/K divided by W/K is seconds.
\(m,\ c_v\)mass (kg) and specific heat (J/(kg·K)) of the bulb liquid \(h\)convection coefficient between fluid and bulb, W/(m²·K) \(A_s\)bulb surface area, m² \(\tau\)time constant, s: heat stored per degree ÷ heat let in per degree

Assumptions: uniform bulb temperature, no conduction along the stem, no radiation, constant properties.

Order 1 · step input

Solve it once for a sudden change, and you know every first-order sensor

A step input \(F(t)=A\,U(t)\) jumps from 0 to the constant \(A\) at \(t=0\). \(U(t)\), the unit step, is 0 for \(t<0\) and 1 for \(t>0\). Plunging a thermometer into hot water is a step in temperature.

  1. Equation for \(t>0\): \(\tau\dfrac{dy}{dt}=KA-y\), with starting value \(y(0)=y_0\).
  2. Separate variables: \(\dfrac{dy}{KA-y}=\dfrac{dt}{\tau}\).
  3. Integrate from 0 to \(t\): \(-\ln\dfrac{KA-y}{KA-y_0}=\dfrac{t}{\tau}\).
  4. Undo the log: $$y(t)=\underbrace{KA}_{\text{steady response}}+\underbrace{(y_0-KA)\,e^{-t/\tau}}_{\text{transient response}}$$

The steady response \(y_\infty=KA\) is what remains after a long time. The transient response decays to zero. Only the transient depends on τ, so τ alone sets the speed.

Why 63.2 %? At \(t=\tau\) the transient is \(e^{-1}=0.368\) of its starting size, so \(1-0.368=0.632\) of the change is done. The initial slope is \((KA-y_0)/\tau\): if the sensor kept its first speed, it would finish in exactly one τ.

Order 1 · how long to wait

The error fraction tells you what share of the change is still missing

\(\Gamma\) is the share of the step not yet covered: 1 just after the step, 0 at the end. The percent response is \((1-\Gamma)\times100\).

The rise time is the time to reach 90 % of the step. Set \(\Gamma=0.10\): \(t=\tau\ln 10=2.30\,\tau\), for every first-order system. (Some datasheets quote a 10–90 % rise time instead, \(\tau\ln 9=2.20\,\tau\).)

Set τ = 3 s and slide t to 3 s, 6.9 s and 15 s. You should read 63 %, 90 % and 99 % of the way from 20 to 37 °C. Then double τ: every one of those times doubles.

Worked example 1

How long must you hold the thermometer?

A liquid-in-glass thermometer reading 20 °C is plunged into a stirred bath at 37 °C. The bulb holds \(m=1.0\) g of mercury (\(c_v=140\) J/(kg·K)), its outer area is \(A_s=1.0\) cm², and the convection coefficient in the stirred water is \(h=280\) W/(m²·K). Find τ and K, the reading after 5 s and 10 s, the 90 % rise time, and how long until the reading is within 0.1 °C of 37 °C.

Known: \(T(0)=20\) °C, \(T_\infty=37\) °C, \(m, c_v, A_s, h\). Find: τ, K, T(5 s), T(10 s), \(t_{90}\), \(t_{0.1}\). Assumptions: lumped bulb, no stem conduction or radiation, step input.

  1. \(\tau=\dfrac{mc_v}{hA_s}=\dfrac{(0.0010)(140)}{(280)(1.0\times10^{-4})}=\dfrac{0.14\ \text{J/K}}{0.028\ \text{W/K}}=5.0\) s, and \(K=hA_s/hA_s=1\).
  2. Response: \(T(t)=T_\infty+[T(0)-T_\infty]e^{-t/\tau}=37-17\,e^{-t/5.0}\) °C.
  3. \(T(5)=37-17e^{-1}=37-6.25=30.75\) °C. \(T(10)=37-17e^{-2}=37-2.30=34.70\) °C.
  4. Rise time: \(t_{90}=2.30\,\tau=11.5\) s (reading 35.3 °C).
  5. Within 0.1 °C: need \(\Gamma=0.1/17=0.00588\), so \(t=\tau\ln(170)=5.0\times5.14=25.7\) s.
  6. Meaning: after 5 s the reading is 6.25 °C low. You must wait about 26 s (about 5τ) for a 0.1 °C reading. In still air, \(h\approx6\) W/(m²·K) gives \(\tau=0.14/(6\times10^{-4})=233\) s, about 4 minutes: the same thermometer is 47 times slower.

τ is not a property of the thermometer alone. It contains \(h\), which depends on the fluid and how fast it moves. A τ measured in stirred water does not apply in still air. Quote τ together with the conditions it was measured in.

τ falls if you reduce the stored heat per unit area, \(mc_v/A_s\) (a thinner bulb, a smaller bead), or raise \(h\) (put the sensor where the fluid moves fast). A 1 mm copper-alloy thermocouple bead (\(\rho c=3.5\) MJ/(m³·K)) has \(\tau=\rho cD/6h\) of 5.8 s in air at \(h=100\) but 0.29 s in water at \(h=2000\) W/(m²·K).

Order 1 · from data to τ

Turn the curve into a straight line and read τ from its slope

To measure τ, apply a known step and record the output from \(t=0\) until it stops changing. You could read the time at which 63.2 % of the change is done, but that uses one point only. A better way takes the logarithm of the error fraction:

Linearised step response $$\ln\Gamma = -\frac{1}{\tau}\,t \qquad\big(\ln\Gamma = 2.303\log_{10}\Gamma\big)$$

This is a straight line \(Y=mX+B\) with \(Y=\ln\Gamma\), \(X=t\), slope \(m=-1/\tau\) and intercept \(B=0\). Plot \(\Gamma\) on a logarithmic axis against \(t\) on a linear axis (a semilog plot), fit a straight line, and \(\tau=-1/m\).

Why it is better than the 63.2 % point:

  • A real sensor that is not truly first order shows up at once as points bending away from a straight line.
  • The fit uses every data point, so random scatter in any single reading matters less.
  • You need not locate the exact instant of the step or the exact Γ = 0.368 level, both easy to misjudge.

Practise the method

Find the hidden time constant in noisy test data

The simulator creates a step test of a thermometer going from 20 °C to 37 °C with a secret τ and some random reading noise of about ±0.25 °C. Your job is to find τ.

The upper plot shows the raw temperatures and your model curve \(37-17e^{-t/\tau_{guess}}\). The lower plot shows the same data as \(\ln\Gamma\) against \(t\), with your line of slope \(-1/\tau_{guess}\).

On the upper plot, many values of τ look almost right, because the curve flattens out. On the lower plot a wrong τ is obvious: the line tilts away from the points.

Move the slider until the orange line on the lower plot runs through the middle of the early and middle points. Ignore the last few points: there Γ is tiny and 0.25 °C of noise is a large fraction of it, so ln Γ jumps around. Then reveal the true τ and press “New test data” to try again.

Taking the log magnifies noise at small Γ. In a real test, weight the early and middle part of the record and stop using points once the remaining change is comparable with the noise.

Game · find τ

Order 1 · ramp input

A steadily rising input: the sensor follows, but one τ behind

Many processes change at a steady rate: an oven heating up, a tank filling. Model the input as a ramp, \(F(t)=a\,t\) for \(t\ge0\), with \(a\) the rate (for example °C/s). With \(y(0)=0\):

First-order ramp response $$y(t)=Ka\,(t-\tau)+Ka\,\tau\,e^{-t/\tau}$$

Check it: substituting gives \(\tau\dot y+y=Ka\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})+Ka(t-\tau)+Ka\tau e^{-t/\tau}=Kat\). After a few τ the exponential has gone and

\(y(t)\approx Ka\,(t-\tau)\)

The output rises at the right rate but is exactly τ seconds late. The reading is too low by a constant amount \(Ka\tau\), the steady ramp error.

An oven heats at 2 °C/min. Its thermocouple has τ = 30 s and K = 1. How far behind is the reading?

  1. \(a=2/60=0.0333\) °C/s, so the error is \(a\tau=0.0333\times30=1.0\) °C, and the reading shows each temperature 30 s after the oven reaches it.

Order 1 · periodic input

Feed it a sine wave: the output comes out smaller and later

Chart recorderinput KA sin ωtoutput y(t)

Start at ωτ ≈ 1: the output is 71 % as tall and lags 1/8 of a period. Drop to 0.1: it tracks almost perfectly. Raise to 10: a tiny ripple, a quarter period late. Also watch the first cycle, where the decaying transient \(Ce^{-t/\tau}\) is still present.

Apply \(F(t)=A\sin\omega t\), with \(\omega=2\pi f\) in rad/s. Try a steady output \(y=B\sin(\omega t+\Phi)\) in \(\tau\dot y+y=KA\sin\omega t\). Matching the sine and cosine terms gives \(B\cos\Phi-\omega\tau B\sin\Phi=KA\) and \(B\sin\Phi+\omega\tau B\cos\Phi=0\), so:

First-order frequency response $$M(\omega)=\frac{B}{KA}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\omega\tau)^2}}$$ $$\Phi(\omega)=-\tan^{-1}(\omega\tau),\qquad \beta_1=\frac{\Phi}{\omega}$$
\(M(\omega)\)magnitude ratio: output amplitude ÷ the amplitude a perfect instrument would give \(\Phi(\omega)\)phase shift, rad or degrees; negative means the output lags \(\beta_1\)time delay, s: how much later each peak arrives (negative = late)

The full solution is \(y=Ce^{-t/\tau}+B\sin(\omega t+\Phi)\): a transient that dies out plus the steady sinusoid, at the same frequency as the input. Both \(M\) and \(\Phi\) depend only on the product \(\omega\tau\).

Push a heavy shopping trolley back and forth. Push slowly and it follows your hands. Push quickly and it hardly moves, always a beat behind.

Order 1 · universal curves

Two curves describe every first-order instrument at every frequency

Frequency responsex-axis: ωτ (log scale)

For \(\omega\tau\ll1\), \(M\approx1\) and \(\Phi\approx0\): the instrument passes the signal almost unchanged. For \(\omega\tau\gg1\), \(M\approx1/\omega\tau\) and \(\Phi\to-90^\circ\): it filters the signal out. A fast signal needs a small τ; a slow one tolerates a large τ.

The dynamic error \(\delta(\omega)=M(\omega)-1\) is the fractional amplitude error at frequency ω. A first-order instrument always reads low (δ ≤ 0).

Amplitude ratios are often given in decibels, \(\text{dB}=20\log_{10}M\). The bandwidth is the frequency band with \(M\ge0.707\), i.e. above −3 dB. For first order it ends at \(\omega\tau=1\), where \(\Phi=-45^\circ\).

Worked example 3

Choosing a fast-enough temperature sensor

The temperature inside a reaction vessel oscillates sinusoidally at some frequency between 1 and 5 Hz. Sensors with various time constants are available. Choose τ so that the dynamic error is no worse than ±2 %.

Known: \(1\le f\le5\) Hz, \(|\delta|\le0.02\). Find: largest allowed τ. Assumptions: first-order sensor, \(F(t)=A\sin\omega t\).

  1. \(|\delta|\le0.02\) means \(0.98\le M\le1.02\). A first-order \(M\) never exceeds 1, so the condition is \(M(\omega)\ge0.98\).
  2. \(M\) falls as ω rises, so the worst case is the highest frequency: \(\omega=2\pi(5)=31.4\) rad/s.
  3. Solve \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\omega\tau)^2}}=0.98\): \((\omega\tau)^2=\dfrac{1}{0.98^2}-1=0.0412\), so \(\omega\tau=0.203\).
  4. \(\tau\le\dfrac{0.203}{31.4}=6.46\times10^{-3}\) s. Choose a sensor with τ ≤ 6.4 ms (rounding down keeps a little margin).
  5. Check at 5 Hz with τ = 6.46 ms: \(\Phi=-\tan^{-1}0.203=-11.5^\circ\), so the time delay is \(0.200/31.4=6.4\) ms. At 1 Hz, \(M=0.999\) and \(\Phi=-2.3^\circ\).
  6. Meaning: the amplitude will be right within 2 %, but the record is about 6 ms late at 5 Hz. If you compare it with a pressure signal from the same vessel, correct for that delay.

Rules worth remembering for first-order sensors: \(\omega\tau\le0.2\) keeps the amplitude error below 2 %; \(\omega\tau\le0.33\) keeps it below 5 %; \(\omega\tau=1\) costs 29 %.

One more, by hand. A first-order system modelled by \(0.5\,\dot y+y=F(t)\) sits at 75 V; the input suddenly rises to 100 V. Write \(y(t)\) and find the output after 1 s.

\(\tau=0.5\) s, \(K=1\), \(y_0=75\) V, \(y_\infty=100\) V. So \(y(t)=100-25\,e^{-t/0.5}\) V. At \(t=1\) s (2τ): \(y=100-25e^{-2}=100-3.38=96.6\) V, i.e. 86.5 % of the step.
