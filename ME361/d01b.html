Fourier Series and the Frequency Spectrum

A clarinet and a trumpet can play the same note, yet you never confuse them. A healthy fan and one with a loose bearing can turn at the same speed, yet an accelerometer tells them apart. The difference is which frequencies the signal contains, and how strongly. This part teaches you to find them.

Where we are

From one sine to many

Part A reduced a signal to a few numbers. The mean \(\bar y\) is its DC level, the RMS measures its overall size, and \(y_\text{rms}^2 = \bar y^2 + y'^2_\text{rms}\) splits it into DC and AC parts. You also met the simple periodic signal \(y = A_0 + C\sin(\omega t + \phi)\), fixed by an amplitude \(C\), a circular frequency \(\omega = 2\pi f\) and a phase \(\phi\).

This part asks: when a measured waveform is not a single sine, which sines is it made of? There are two practical reasons to care.

1. Predicting instrument response. Many measuring systems (a thermometer, a pressure transducer, a spring-mass accelerometer) answer a sine input with a sine output of the same frequency, only scaled and delayed. For such linear systems the superposition principle holds: the response to a sum of inputs is the sum of the responses. Split any input into sines and you can predict the output. Topic 02 is built on this.

2. Diagnosis and bandwidth. The frequencies present point to their physical sources (shaft speed, blade passing, mains hum) and tell you how fast an instrument must be.

A prism splits white light into a band of colours. Each colour is light of one frequency, and its brightness is its amplitude. Fourier analysis is a prism for signals: a complex waveform goes in, a list of frequencies and amplitudes comes out.

Periodic signals

Fundamental and harmonics: why a guitar is not a violin

A signal is periodic with period \(T\) if \(y(t + T) = y(t)\) for every \(t\). If \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) both have period \(T\), so does \(a\,y_1 + b\,y_2\) for any constants \(a, b\).

A sine of frequency \(n f_0\) (\(n\) a whole number, \(f_0 = 1/T\)) completes exactly \(n\) cycles in \(T\), so any sum of such sines repeats every \(T\).

The fundamental is the component at \(f_0 = 1/T\). The component at \(n f_0\) is the n-th harmonic.

A plucked string vibrates in several mode shapes at once; mode \(n\) has \(n\) half-waves and frequency \(n f_0\). Plucked at its centre, it holds only odd modes, with amplitudes falling as \(1/n^2\): each even mode has a node (a point that never moves) at the centre, and a pluck at a node cannot excite it. Plucked at one fifth of its length, modes 5, 10, 15 are missing for the same reason.

The ear hears the fundamental as pitch and the harmonic mix as timbre. A clarinet’s B-flat peaks at 231, 693, 1155, 1617 Hz (odd harmonics only); a brass section near 700 Hz shows 700, 1400, 2100, 2800 Hz (all harmonics).

Fourier series

Any periodic signal as a constant plus harmonics

Take a periodic signal \(y(t)\) with period \(T\). Its fundamental circular frequency is \(\omega = 2\pi/T\). Fourier’s claim is that the signal can be written as a constant plus cosines and sines at \(\omega, 2\omega, 3\omega, \dots\):

Trigonometric Fourier series $$y(t) = A_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big(A_n\cos n\omega t + B_n\sin n\omega t\big)$$
\(A_0\)constant term: the DC level, same unit as \(y\) \(A_n,\ B_n\)Fourier coefficients: how much cosine and sine of harmonic \(n\) the signal holds \(\omega\)fundamental circular frequency \(2\pi/T\), rad/s; harmonic \(n\) sits at \(n\omega\)

Part A showed that \(A\cos + B\sin\) at one frequency is a single shifted sine. Applied to each harmonic:

Amplitude–phase form $$y(t) = A_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} C_n\sin(n\omega t + \phi_n^*)$$ $$C_n = \sqrt{A_n^2 + B_n^2},\qquad \tan\phi_n^* = \frac{A_n}{B_n}$$

\(C_n\) is the amplitude of harmonic \(n\). A list of the \(C_n\) is what a spectrum plots.

The signal must be piecewise continuous over a period (any jumps are finite and few) and have a left- and right-hand slope at every point. Practically every engineering signal qualifies. The series then equals \(y(t)\) everywhere, except at a jump, where it converges to the midpoint of the jump.

A Fourier series is a recipe: a pinch of DC, this much fundamental, that much third harmonic. Two signals with the same period differ only in their recipe, just as two cakes from the same oven differ only in the ingredients.

A pressure signal repeats exactly every 20 ms. Which frequencies can its Fourier series contain?

The key idea · orthogonality

Products of different harmonics average to zero

Orthogonality explorer, T = 1 sfirstsecond+ area− area

Default n = 2, m = 3: green and red areas balance, integral 0. Set m = 2: the product never goes negative and the integral is 0.5 s = T/2. Now pick sin·cos with n = m = 2: zero again; a sine never “contains” a cosine.

To pull one harmonic out of a sum: multiply by a sine or cosine of that harmonic, then integrate over one period. It works because (\(n, m = 1, 2, 3, \dots\)):

Orthogonality over one period $$\int_0^T\!\cos n\omega t\,dt = \int_0^T\!\sin n\omega t\,dt = 0$$ $$\int_0^T\!\cos n\omega t\,\cos m\omega t\,dt = \begin{cases}0 & n \ne m\\ T/2 & n = m\end{cases}$$ $$\int_0^T\!\sin n\omega t\,\sin m\omega t\,dt:\ \text{the same}$$ $$\int_0^T\!\sin n\omega t\,\cos m\omega t\,dt = 0\ \text{ for all } n, m$$

\(\cos a\cos b = \tfrac12[\cos(a-b) + \cos(a+b)]\). With \(a = n\omega t\), \(b = m\omega t\) these are harmonics \(n-m\) and \(n+m\): whole cycles in \(T\), zero net area. Only for \(n = m\) is the first term \(\cos 0 = 1\), adding \(\tfrac12 T\).

The \(x\)-component of a force is its dot product with \(\hat{\imath}\); the \(y\) and \(z\) parts drop out because the axes are perpendicular. The integral of a product is the dot product for functions, and different harmonics are “perpendicular”: orthogonal.

Fourier coefficients

Multiply, integrate, and only one term survives

  1. Start from \(y = A_0 + \sum_n (A_n\cos n\omega t + B_n\sin n\omega t)\) and integrate both sides over one period. Every sine and cosine encloses zero area, leaving \(\int_0^T y\,dt = A_0 T\). So \(A_0 = \frac1T\int_0^T y\,dt\): the mean value of Part A.
  2. Pick a harmonic \(m\). Multiply both sides by \(\cos m\omega t\) and integrate over a period. The left side becomes \(\int_0^T y\cos m\omega t\,dt\). The right side splits into three kinds of integral: $$A_0\!\int_0^T\!\cos m\omega t\,dt,\qquad A_n\!\int_0^T\!\cos n\omega t\,\cos m\omega t\,dt,$$ $$B_n\!\int_0^T\!\sin n\omega t\,\cos m\omega t\,dt\quad\text{for every } n.$$
  3. Orthogonality wipes the right side clean: the \(A_0\) term is zero, every sin·cos term is zero, and every cos·cos term is zero except \(n = m\), which gives \(A_m\,T/2\).
  4. Therefore \(A_m = \frac2T\int_0^T y\cos m\omega t\,dt\). Multiplying by \(\sin m\omega t\) instead isolates \(B_m\) in exactly the same way.

A radio picks one station out of hundreds by mixing the antenna signal with its own oscillator at that station’s frequency and averaging. All other stations average away. The coefficient formulas “tune in” to harmonic \(n\) the same way.

Fourier coefficients (Euler formulas) $$A_0 = \frac1T\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} y(t)\,dt$$ $$A_n = \frac2T\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} y(t)\cos n\omega t\,dt$$ $$B_n = \frac2T\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} y(t)\sin n\omega t\,dt$$

The integral may run over any interval one period long, \(t_0\) to \(t_0 + T\): 0 to \(T\), or \(-T/2\) to \(T/2\). Choose whichever makes the algebra easiest.

\(A_n\) and \(B_n\) carry \(2/T\) rather than \(1/T\) because \(\cos^2\) and \(\sin^2\) average to \(\tfrac12\) over a period; the 2 undoes that half. \(A_0\) multiplies a constant, whose square averages to 1, so it keeps \(1/T\).

\(A_0 = \frac1T\int_0^T\cos^2\omega t\,dt = \tfrac12\). For \(A_2\): \(\cos^2\omega t\cos 2\omega t = (\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos 2\omega t)\cos 2\omega t\), whose average is \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac14\); times 2 gives \(A_2 = \tfrac12\). \(A_1\) and all others are zero. So \(\cos^2\omega t = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos 2\omega t\): squaring a signal of frequency \(f\) produced DC and \(2f\), and no \(f\) at all. Remember this when you meet the rectifier.

Symmetry shortcuts

Even signals need only cosines; odd signals only sines

A function is even if \(g(-t) = g(t)\): its left half is the mirror image of its right half (\(\cos n\omega t\) is even). It is odd if \(h(-t) = -h(t)\): a half-turn about the origin leaves it unchanged (\(\sin n\omega t\) is odd). Many functions are neither.

Products follow sign rules: even × even = even, odd × odd = even, even × odd = odd. Over \(-T/2\) to \(T/2\) an odd function’s left area cancels its right area, so its integral is zero; an even function’s integral is twice that over \(0\) to \(T/2\).

If y(t) isThenRemaining coefficients
Even\(y\sin n\omega t\) is odd, so all \(B_n = 0\)\(A_0 = \frac2T\int_0^{T/2}\!y\,dt\), \(A_n = \frac4T\int_0^{T/2}\!y\cos n\omega t\,dt\)
Odd\(y\cos n\omega t\) and \(y\) are odd, so \(A_0 = A_n = 0\)\(B_n = \frac4T\int_0^{T/2}\!y\sin n\omega t\,dt\)
Neitherno shortcutboth sines and cosines

Evenness depends on where you put \(t = 0\). Shift a square wave by a quarter period and its sine series becomes a cosine series; the amplitudes \(C_n\) do not change, only the phases. Adding a constant to an odd wave spoils the oddness, but only \(A_0\) changes.

The full-wave rectified sine \(|\sin t|\) is mirror symmetric about \(t = 0\). Which coefficients can be non-zero?

Worked example

Fourier series of a square wave

A square-wave voltage is \(y = -5\) V for \(-\pi < t < 0\) and \(y = +5\) V for \(0 < t < \pi\), repeating every \(2\pi\) s. Find its Fourier coefficients, write the series, and check it at \(t = \pi/2\) and at the jump \(t = 0\).

Known: amplitude 5 V, \(T = 2\pi\) s. Find: \(A_0\), \(A_n\), \(B_n\). Assume: ideal jumps; series conditions met (finite jumps).

  1. \(\omega = 2\pi/T = 1\) rad/s. Symmetry: \(y(-t) = -y(t)\), so the wave is odd. Hence \(A_0 = 0\) and every \(A_n = 0\).
  2. Odd-function formula: \(\displaystyle B_n = \frac4T\int_0^{T/2}\! y\sin n\omega t\,dt = \frac{4}{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi}\! 5\sin nt\,dt = \frac{10}{\pi}\Big[-\frac{\cos nt}{n}\Big]_0^{\pi}\)
  3. \(\displaystyle B_n = \frac{10}{n\pi}(1 - \cos n\pi)\). Since \(\cos n\pi = (-1)^n\): for even \(n\), \(1 - 1 = 0\); for odd \(n\), \(1 + 1 = 2\), so \(B_n = 20/(n\pi)\).
  4. \(\displaystyle y(t) = \frac{20}{\pi}\Big(\sin t + \tfrac13\sin 3t + \tfrac15\sin 5t + \dots\Big)\) V, with \(B_1 = 6.37\), \(B_3 = 2.12\), \(B_5 = 1.27\), \(B_7 = 0.91\) V.
  5. Check at \(t = \pi/2\): \(\sin t = 1\), \(\sin 3t = -1\), \(\sin 5t = 1\), … so \(5 = \frac{20}{\pi}(1 - \tfrac13 + \tfrac15 - \tfrac17 + \dots)\), i.e. \(\frac\pi4 = 1 - \tfrac13 + \tfrac15 - \dots\), a known series for \(\pi\) (Leibniz, 1673). ✓ At \(t = 0\) every sine is zero: the series gives 0 V, the midpoint of the jump from −5 to +5 V.

What this means: a square wave holds only odd harmonics, with amplitudes falling as \(1/n\). The 5th harmonic is still 20 % of the fundamental, so an instrument that must show the sharp corners needs to follow many times the fundamental frequency.

For any square wave of amplitude \(a\): \(B_n = \dfrac{4a}{n\pi}\) for odd \(n\), zero for even \(n\). With \(a = 1\) and \(T = 10\) s, for instance, \(\omega = 0.628\) rad/s and \(B_1 = 1.27\), \(B_3 = 0.42\).

All waves have amplitude 1; the coefficients follow from the same Euler formulas. Jumps (square, sawtooth) give coefficients falling as \(1/n\): slow convergence. Mere corners (triangle, rectified sine) give \(1/n^2\): a few terms suffice. A smoother signal has a narrower spectrum.

The Gibbs phenomenon: next to a jump every partial sum overshoots by about 9 % of the jump. More terms squeeze the overshoot closer to the jump but never remove it.

Square wave: step from 1 to 5 terms and count the humps per half-cycle (one per term). Push to 40: the RMS error falls, but the peak stays near 1.18, 9 % of the jump of 2. The triangle looks right with 3 terms. The rectified sine has DC plus even multiples of the input frequency only.

Worked example

A 60 Hz rectifier has no 60 Hz in its output

A full-wave rectifier flips the negative halves of \(E = 120\sin 120\pi t\) V (60 Hz) upward, giving \(E(t) = |120\sin 120\pi t|\) V. Find its DC level and the frequencies and amplitudes of its first three harmonics.

Known: input amplitude 120 V, 60 Hz. Find: \(A_0\), harmonic amplitudes. Assume: ideal rectifier (no diode voltage drop).

  1. Each hump lasts half an input cycle, so the output period is \(T = 1/120\) s: fundamental \(f_0 = 120\) Hz, \(\omega_0 = 240\pi\) rad/s. The wave is even, so all \(B_k = 0\).
  2. Over \(0 < t < T\), \(\sin 120\pi t \ge 0\), so \(E = 120\sin 120\pi t\) there. Substitute \(\theta = 120\pi t\) (\(\theta\): 0 to \(\pi\); \(dt = d\theta/120\pi\)): $$A_0 = \frac1T\!\int_0^T\! E\,dt = \frac{120\cdot120}{120\pi}\!\int_0^\pi\!\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{240}{\pi} = 76.4\ \text{V}$$
  3. For harmonic \(k\), \(k\omega_0 t = 2k\theta\), so $$A_k = \frac2T\!\int_0^T\! E\cos k\omega_0 t\,dt = \frac{240}{\pi}\!\int_0^\pi\!\sin\theta\cos 2k\theta\,d\theta$$
  4. Product-to-sum: \(\sin\theta\cos 2k\theta = \tfrac12[\sin(1+2k)\theta + \sin(1-2k)\theta]\). For odd \(m\), \(\int_0^\pi \sin m\theta\,d\theta = 2/m\), so the integral is \(\frac{1}{1+2k} + \frac{1}{1-2k} = \frac{2}{1-4k^2}\). Hence \(A_k = -\dfrac{480}{\pi(4k^2-1)}\) V.
  5. \(k = 1\) (120 Hz): −50.9 V. \(k = 2\) (240 Hz): −10.2 V. \(k = 3\) (360 Hz): −4.37 V. So \(E(t) = 76.4 - 50.9\cos 240\pi t - 10.2\cos 480\pi t - 4.37\cos 720\pi t - \dots\) V.

What this means: the output contains no 60 Hz. Its content has moved to DC (the useful part) and to ripple at 120, 240, 360 Hz. A smoothing filter for this rectifier must suppress 120 Hz.

The amplitude spectrum

A spectrum shows how much of each frequency a signal holds

The amplitude spectrum plots \(C_n\) against frequency \(nf_0\), with the DC level \(A_0\) at \(f = 0\). The phase spectrum plots \(\phi_n\) against frequency. Together they hold all the information in \(y(t)\): you can rebuild the signal from them.

How much does each line add to the signal’s size? Square the series and average over a period: cross products of different harmonics average to zero (orthogonality), and each harmonic’s own square averages to \(C_n^2/2\), as in Part A.

Mean square from the spectrum (Parseval’s relation) $$y_\text{rms}^2 = A_0^2 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{C_n^2}{2}$$

The power spectrum plots \(C_n^2/2\) against frequency: how the mean square (proportional to power in a resistor) is shared among the frequencies.

Quick numbers. Square wave of amplitude 1: \(y_\text{rms}^2 = 1\); the fundamental holds \((4/\pi)^2/2 = 0.811\) (81 %), harmonics 1, 3, 5 together 93 %. Rectifier: \(76.4^2 + (50.9^2 + 10.2^2 + 4.37^2)/2 = 7195\) V², against the exact \(120^2/2 = 7200\) V².

A pressure transducer must reproduce a 50 Hz square wave well enough to show its 1st, 3rd and 5th harmonics. Up to what frequency must it respond?

250 Hz. A square wave has odd harmonics only, at 50, 150 and 250 Hz; together they carry 93 % of the mean square. Answering 50 Hz (the fundamental) would round off the corners into a near-sine.

From series to transform

Signals that never repeat: let the period grow without limit

Pulse train: stretch the periodspectrum lines|Y(f)| of one pulse

A hammer blow, a valve slamming shut, a start-up transient: these never repeat, so they have no Fourier series. Trick: treat such a signal as periodic with an enormous period, \(T \to \infty\). The lines sit at \(n/T\), so their spacing \(1/T\) shrinks to zero and they merge into a continuous curve. Each \(C_n\) also shrinks like \(1/T\), but \((T/2)C_n\) settles on a fixed curve: the Fourier transform.

Fourier transform and amplitude spectrum $$Y(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} y(t)\,e^{-i2\pi f t}\,dt = A(f) - iB(f)$$ $$A = \int y\cos 2\pi f t\,dt,\quad B = \int y\sin 2\pi f t\,dt$$ $$|Y(f)| = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2},\qquad \phi(f) = \tan^{-1}\frac{B}{A}$$

Euler’s identity \(e^{-i\theta} = \cos\theta - i\sin\theta\) (with \(i = \sqrt{-1}\)) packs the cosine and sine integrals into one complex number. \(Y\) has units of \(y\) × seconds: an amplitude per hertz. The inverse transform \(y(t) = \int Y(f)\,e^{i2\pi f t}\,df\) rebuilds the signal from its spectrum.

Pulses are 0.1 s wide, height 1. At T = 0.40 s the lines are 2.5 Hz apart and land on the dashed curve. Drag T to 4 s: lines every 0.25 Hz fill the curve, which is the transform of a single pulse. Switch to raw \(C_n\) and watch the lines shrink as T grows. Notice the zeros at 10, 20, 30 Hz = \(1/\tau\), \(2/\tau\), …: a shorter pulse spreads wider in frequency.

Sampled data

The discrete Fourier transform: the spectrum of N numbers

  1. A data system stores \(N\) samples \(y_r = y(r\,\delta t)\), \(r = 0, 1, \dots, N-1\), covering a record of length \(N\delta t\).
  2. Treat the record as one period: \(T = N\delta t\). Its Fourier series can only hold the harmonics \(f_k = k/T = k/(N\delta t)\). Their spacing is the frequency resolution \(\delta f = 1/(N\delta t)\).
  3. Write the coefficient integral as \(\frac2T\int_0^T y\,e^{-i2\pi f_k t}dt\) and replace it by a sum over the samples: \(\frac{2}{N\delta t}\sum_r y_r\,e^{-i2\pi f_k r\delta t}\,\delta t\). Since \(f_k\,r\,\delta t = kr/N\), \(\delta t\) cancels.
  4. Only \(k = 0\) to \(N/2 - 1\) are meaningful: to describe a frequency you need at least two samples per cycle, so the top is just below \(1/(2\delta t)\). Topic 05 shows what goes wrong above it.
Discrete Fourier transform (one-sided) $$Y(f_k) = \frac2N\sum_{r=0}^{N-1} y_r\,e^{-i2\pi rk/N}$$ $$f_k = k\,\delta f,\quad k = 0, 1, \dots, \tfrac N2 - 1,\qquad \delta f = \frac{1}{N\delta t}$$
\(y_r\)sample number \(r\), unit of the signal \(N\)number of samples; \(N\delta t\) is the record length, s \(\delta f\)frequency resolution: spacing of the bins, Hz \(|Y(f_k)|\)amplitude \(C_k\) of the component at \(f_k\) (use \(1/N\) instead of \(2/N\) at \(k = 0\), which gives the mean)

The fast Fourier transform (FFT) computes the same sum in about \(N\log_2 N\) operations instead of \(N^2\) (for \(N = 1024\): 10 000 instead of a million), best when \(N\) is a power of 2.

You need \(\delta f = 0.1\) Hz and you sample at 1000 Hz. How many samples must you record?

Worked example

Eight samples of a sine, and their spectrum by hand

The signal \(y(t) = 10\sin 2\pi t\) V (1 Hz, amplitude 10 V) is sampled \(N = 8\) times at \(\delta t = 0.125\) s. List the samples and compute the amplitude spectrum by hand with the DFT.

Known: \(N = 8\), \(\delta t = 0.125\) s. Find: \(y_r\) and \(|Y(f_k)|\). Assume: exact samples (no quantization).

  1. \(y_r = 10\sin(2\pi r \times 0.125) = 10\sin(\pi r/4)\): 0, 7.071, 10, 7.071, 0, −7.071, −10, −7.071 V.
  2. Record length \(N\delta t = 1\) s, so \(\delta f = 1\) Hz. Bins \(k = 0, 1, 2, 3\) sit at 0, 1, 2, 3 Hz; the limit \(1/(2\delta t)\) is 4 Hz.
  3. \(k = 0\): \(Y = \frac18\sum y_r = 0\). No DC, as expected.
  4. \(k = 1\): \(e^{-i2\pi r/8} = \cos(\pi r/4) - i\sin(\pi r/4)\). Real part: \(\sum y_r\cos(\pi r/4) = 5 + 0 - 5 + 0 + 5 + 0 - 5 = 0\). Imaginary part: \(\sum y_r\sin(\pi r/4) = 5 + 10 + 5 + 0 + 5 + 10 + 5 = 40\).
  5. \(Y(1\ \text{Hz}) = \frac28(0 - 40i) = -10i\) V, so \(|Y| = 10\) V. Since \(Y = A - iB\): \(A = 0\), \(B = 10\) V, a pure sine of amplitude 10 V. ✓
  6. \(k = 2\): weights \(\sin(\pi r/2) = 0, 1, 0, -1, 0, 1, 0, -1\) give \(7.071 - 7.071 - 7.071 + 7.071 = 0\); the cosine sum is \(-10 + 10 = 0\). \(k = 3\) also gives 0. Orthogonality at work.

What this means: one spike of 10 V at 1 Hz, exactly the input. It came out this clean because the record held a whole number of cycles, so 1 Hz fell exactly on a bin. The next slide shows what happens when it does not.

Two sines in, two spikes out, if the resolution allows

DFT lab

The signal is \(e(t) = C_1\sin 2\pi f_1 t + C_2\sin 2\pi f_2 t\). The Two tones preset is \(e = 5\sin 31.4t + 2\sin 44t\) V: \(31.4/2\pi = 4.997\) Hz and \(44/2\pi = 7.003\) Hz, sampled \(N = 128\) times at \(\delta t = 1/128\) s, so \(\delta f = 1\) Hz. The spikes land on the 5 and 7 Hz bins at 5.00 and 2.01 V.

Leakage: when a frequency falls between bins (the record does not hold a whole number of its cycles), its amplitude spreads into neighbouring bins and the peak reads low.

1. Two tones: read the two peaks. 2. Move f₁ to 5.500 Hz: the peak drops to about 3.3 V and spreads. 3. Set f₁ = 5.000 and f₂ = 5.500 Hz: closer than δf, the tones blur into one hump; choose N = 256 (δf = 0.5 Hz) and they separate. 4. Eight samples preset, then raise f₁ to 7 Hz: a 10 V spike appears at 1 Hz, a false frequency.

Step 4 is aliasing: a component above \(1/(2\delta t)\) masquerades as a lower one. The sample rate \(1/\delta t\) must exceed twice the highest frequency in the signal. Topic 05 treats this in full.

Reading a measured spectrum

What a vibration spectrum tells you, and what resolution it needs

An accelerometer sits on the bearing housing of a 6-blade fan turning at 1470 rev/min. Its cable runs beside 50 Hz mains wiring. (a) Where do you expect spectral peaks? (b) Sampling at 1024 Hz, how many samples show the twice-shaft-speed peak and the mains peak as separate lines?

Known: speed 1470 rev/min, 6 blades, mains 50 Hz, \(1/\delta t = 1024\) Hz. Find: peak frequencies; \(N\). Assume: constant speed during the record.

  1. Shaft frequency \(f_s = 1470/60 = 24.5\) Hz. Residual unbalance pushes once per turn: peak at 1× = 24.5 Hz.
  2. Misalignment or looseness commonly adds a 2× peak: 49 Hz.
  3. Each blade passing a support strut gives a pressure pulse: blade-pass frequency \(6 \times 24.5 = 147\) Hz.
  4. Electrical pickup appears at 50 Hz, only 1 Hz from the 2× peak.
  5. To show two lines 1 Hz apart with an empty bin between them, \(\delta f \le 0.5\) Hz. Record length \(1/\delta f = 2\) s, so \(N = 2 \times 1024 = 2048\) samples (a power of 2, good for the FFT).
  6. Top frequency \(1/(2\delta t) = 512\) Hz > 147 Hz: the blade-pass peak is covered.

What this means: each peak points to a physical source, and a peak that grows over weeks points to a developing fault. Resolution is set by the record length, not by the sample rate.

Fan spectrum (simulated record)DFT amplitude

Choose N = 512 (δf = 2 Hz): 49 and 50 Hz merge into one peak near 50 Hz, and you might blame the mains for a mechanical fault; the 24.5 Hz peak also leaks and reads low. N = 2048: four clean lines with their true amplitudes 2.0, 0.8, 0.5 and 1.2 m/s². Resolution is like the marks on a ruler: with a mark every 2 Hz you cannot tell 49 from 50 Hz.

Check yourself

A data system records 1024 samples at \(\delta t = 1\) ms. What is the DFT frequency resolution?

\(y(t) = t^2\) for \(-\pi < t < \pi\), repeated every \(2\pi\). Which coefficients can be non-zero?

You double the sample rate but keep the same number of samples N. What happens to \(\delta f\)?

Expand: \(4 + 4\sin 10t + \sin^2 10t\). With \(\sin^2\theta = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12\cos 2\theta\): \(y = 4.5 + 4\sin 10t - 0.5\cos 20t\). The spectrum has DC 4.5, a line of amplitude 4 at 10 rad/s (1.59 Hz) and a line of amplitude 0.5 at 20 rad/s (3.18 Hz). Squaring, like rectifying, is a nonlinear operation, and it created a harmonic that was not in the input. Check with Parseval: \(y_\text{rms}^2 = 4.5^2 + (4^2 + 0.5^2)/2 = 28.375\).

IdeaKey relation or rule
Fourier series\(y = A_0 + \sum (A_n\cos n\omega t + B_n\sin n\omega t)\), \(\omega = 2\pi/T\); harmonic \(n\) at \(nf_0\); \(C_n = \sqrt{A_n^2 + B_n^2}\)
Coefficients\(A_0 = \frac1T\int y\,dt\); \(A_n, B_n = \frac2T\int y\,\{\cos, \sin\}\,n\omega t\,dt\) over any one period; they work because of orthogonality
SymmetryEven: cosines only, \(\frac4T\int_0^{T/2}\). Odd: sines only. Square wave: \(B_n = 4a/(n\pi)\), odd \(n\)
Spectrum\(C_n\) against \(nf_0\); \(y_\text{rms}^2 = A_0^2 + \sum C_n^2/2\); jumps give \(1/n\) decay, corners \(1/n^2\)
Transform\(Y(f) = \int y\,e^{-i2\pi ft}dt = A - iB\), \(|Y| = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}\); for signals that never repeat
DFT\(Y(f_k) = \frac2N\sum y_r e^{-i2\pi rk/N}\), \(\delta f = 1/(N\delta t)\), \(k < N/2\); need \(1/\delta t > 2f_\text{max}\)

You should now be able to…

  • explain why splitting a signal into sines predicts instrument response;
  • derive the Fourier coefficients from orthogonality and compute them, using even/odd shortcuts;
  • sketch and interpret an amplitude spectrum, and find the bandwidth a signal needs;
  • compute a small DFT by hand and choose \(N\) and \(\delta t\) for a required \(\delta f\);
  • read a vibration spectrum and spot leakage and merged peaks.
