Signal Types, Mean and RMS

Before you choose an instrument, describe the signal it must follow. Is it steady or changing? Smooth or sampled? Predictable or random? How big is it on average, and how big are its swings?

Where we are

From the instrument to the signal it carries

A measurement system is a chain of stages: a sensor that responds to the measured variable, a signal-conditioning stage that amplifies or filters, and an output stage that displays or records. A static calibration gives two numbers you will use throughout: the static sensitivity (output change per unit input change, the slope of the calibration curve) and the range (the lowest to the highest input the instrument is designed to measure). The lectures on basic measurement concepts treat calibration and its errors in full.

A static calibration assumes the input sits still while you read it. Real inputs often move. This topic gives you the vocabulary and the arithmetic to describe a moving input, so that in Topic 02 you can ask whether an instrument can keep up with it.

Signal: the physical information about a measured variable as it passes from the process to the measurement system, between the stages of the system, or out of it as the reading. Its shape plotted against time is its waveform.

We classify signals, then reduce any record to two numbers that every engineer quotes: the mean and the RMS. We finish with the four numbers that describe a sine wave.

Input / output signal concepts

You cannot choose an instrument until you know the signal

Choosing a tyre gauge needs only one fact about the signal: how big it gets. A car tyre needs a gauge that reads up to about 275 kPa; a racing-bicycle tyre needs one that reads up to about 700 kPa. That is a question of range.

Now try to measure the pressure inside an engine cylinder. It rises and falls once every engine cycle. In a four-stroke engine at 3000 rev/min one cycle takes two revolutions, so the pressure pulse repeats \(3000/2 = 1500\) times per minute, or 25 times per second. A tyre gauge would show a blur, or a wrong steady number, with no warning. Here you must also know how fast the signal changes.

The magnitude of a signal is its size at an instant. Its amplitude is the size of its swings about the average. Its frequency tells how many times per second the pattern repeats. Together they decide the range and the speed the instrument needs.

Photographing a mountain versus a hummingbird. The mountain is a static signal: any shutter speed works. The hummingbird’s wings beat dozens of times a second: the wrong camera gives a blur. You must describe the subject before you pick the camera.

Classification by time and magnitude

Analog, discrete-time and digital signals

An analog signal is continuous in time and in magnitude: it exists at every instant and can take any value in its range, like the pointer of a dial gauge.

A discrete-time signal exists only at separate instants \(t = r\,\delta t\) (\(r = 0, 1, 2, \dots\)). It comes from sampling: reading the analog signal once every sample interval \(\delta t\). Between samples we know nothing, so we must assume something. The simplest assumption, sample and hold, keeps the last value until the next sample, which gives a staircase.

A digital signal is discrete in time and in magnitude. Each sample is rounded to one of a fixed set of levels, a step called quantization. An analog-to-digital (A/D) converter does both jobs and outputs binary numbers.

A digital clock quantizes time: for a whole minute it shows “10:41”, then jumps to “10:42”. A music CD does the same to sound: microphone (analog) → A/D converter → stored numbers (digital) → converter back to an analog voltage for the loudspeaker.

What sampling and quantizing do to a waveform

Sampler and quantizeranalogsamplesdigital, held

The test signal is \(y = \sin 2\pi t + 0.35\sin(6\pi t + 0.6)\): a 1 Hz wave with a smaller 3 Hz ripple on it. The converter covers −1.5 to +1.5 (a full-scale range of 3 units).

Quantization levels and step $$\text{levels} = 2^M,\qquad Q = \frac{\text{full-scale range}}{2^M}$$ $$|\text{rounding error}| \le \frac{Q}{2}$$
\(M\)number of bits of the converter \(Q\)quantization step: the magnitude change between adjacent levels (same unit as the signal)

The orange staircase shows the values the levels stand for, not the bits. With M = 3 there are 8 levels between −1.5 and +1.5, at −1.3125, −0.9375, … , +1.3125 (step Q = 0.375). The converter stores each one as a 3-bit code, 000 for the lowest up to 111 for the highest, so the 0s and 1s live inside the code word, while the level it labels can be negative.

Quick number: an 8-bit converter over 0 to 10 V has 256 levels, so \(Q = 10/256 = 0.039\) V and the reading can be wrong by up to ±0.020 V from rounding alone.

Set M = 8 and slowly raise δt from 0.01 s. Near δt ≈ 0.15 s the 3 Hz ripple disappears from the samples: you sampled too slowly to see it. Then return δt to 0.01 s and drop M to 1 or 2. Now you sample fast, but the magnitude is so coarse that the shape is lost. Time resolution and magnitude resolution are separate limits.

Classification by waveform

Static or dynamic, predictable or random

A train timetable is deterministic: you can say where the train will be at 10:15. The crowd on the platform is nondeterministic: you can say “about 200 people at rush hour, give or take 40”, but never who stands where.

A static signal does not change during the time you care about: a shaft diameter, a battery voltage over an afternoon. A dynamic signal changes with time.

A deterministic signal follows a rule, so its future values can be predicted. A nondeterministic (random) signal has no rule that predicts it; only its statistics, such as its mean and RMS, can be known in advance. Turbulent velocity and data traffic between computers are examples.

A periodic signal repeats exactly every period \(T\). It is simple if it contains one frequency (a single sine) and complex if it is a sum of several. An aperiodic signal is deterministic but never repeats: a step, a ramp, a pulse.

A transient is a dynamic signal that dies away, such as the ringing after a hammer blow. A signal is steady when its character does not change with time; for a random signal this is called stationary: its mean and RMS stay constant even though individual values are unpredictable.

Classification by waveform

Each class has a simple mathematical model

ClassModel
Static\(y(t) = A_0\)
Simple periodic\(y(t) = A_0 + C\sin(\omega t + \phi)\)
Complex periodic\(y(t) = A_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} C_n\sin(n\omega t + \phi_n)\)
Step\(y(t) = A_0\,U(t)\)
Ramp\(y(t) = A_0\,t\) for \(0 < t < t_f\)
Pulse\(y(t) = A_0\,U(t) - A_0\,U(t - t_1)\)
Nondeterministic\(y(t) \approx A_0 + \sum C_n\sin(n\omega t + \phi_n)\), statistically

\(U(t)\) is the unit step function: 0 for \(t < 0\) and 1 for \(t \ge 0\). A pulse of width \(t_1\) is a step up at \(t=0\) minus a step at \(t_1\). \(A_0\) is a constant level, \(C\) an amplitude, \(\omega\) a frequency in rad/s and \(\phi\) a phase angle; these are defined carefully later in this part.

Notice the complex periodic model: a constant plus a sum of sines at whole-number multiples of one frequency. Part B shows that almost any signal can be written this way, which is why the sine is the workhorse of this subject.

Signal analysis · the mean

The mean value: the height of the equal-area rectangle

The sea level in a harbour. Waves slap up and down, but the tide gauge reports one average height. The waves are the AC part; the average level is the DC part.

The problem: a signal that wiggles has no single value, yet we often need one number for “where it sits”.

Mean value of a continuous signal $$\bar y = \frac{1}{t_2 - t_1}\int_{t_1}^{t_2} y(t)\,dt$$
\(y(t)\)the signal (any unit: V, kPa, °C) \(t_1,\ t_2\)start and end of the record (s) \(\bar y\)mean value, same unit as \(y\)

The integral is the area under the curve. Dividing by the width \(t_2 - t_1\) gives the height of a rectangle with the same area. Any constant level that is part of \(y\) passes straight through to \(\bar y\), while the up-and-down swings, if you average over whole cycles, cancel.

The mean is called the DC component or DC offset (from “direct current”). What is left after you subtract it, \(y(t) - \bar y\), is the AC component or fluctuating part.

Quick number: for \(y = 25 + 10\sin 6\pi t\) averaged over one period (\(1/3\) s), the sine contributes zero area, so \(\bar y = 25\).

Signal analysis · root mean square

RMS: the steady value that does the same work

The problem: the mean of a pure sine over a cycle is zero, yet a sinusoidal current clearly heats a resistor. We need a number that measures the size of the fluctuations. Electrical power gives the natural answer.

  1. A current \(I\) through a resistance \(R\) dissipates power \(P = I^2R\) (watts, the rate of energy conversion to heat).
  2. If \(I(t)\) varies, the energy converted from \(t_1\) to \(t_2\) is $$E = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} I(t)^2 R\,dt$$
  3. Ask: which constant current \(I_e\) would convert the same energy in the same time? It would give \(E = I_e^2 R\,(t_2 - t_1)\).
  4. Set the two energies equal; \(R\) cancels: $$I_e = \sqrt{\frac{1}{t_2-t_1}\int_{t_1}^{t_2} I(t)^2\,dt}$$
  5. Read the formula from the inside out: square the signal, take the mean, then the square root. Hence “root mean square”. The same recipe works for any signal.
RMS value of a continuous signal $$y_\text{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{t_2-t_1}\int_{t_1}^{t_2} y(t)^2\,dt}$$
\(y_\text{rms}\)root-mean-square value, same unit as \(y\) \(I_e\)effective (RMS) current, A

Squaring makes every value positive, so positive and negative swings both count and cannot cancel. Big values are weighted more than small ones, just as power grows with the square of current. The final square root brings the unit back to that of \(y\).

A 230 V RMS mains supply heats a kettle exactly as fast as a steady 230 V DC supply would, even though the mains voltage averages zero over each cycle. RMS is the “heating value” of a signal.

When a signal has a large DC offset, its RMS is dominated by that offset and says little about the fluctuations. The RMS of the fluctuations alone is found after subtracting the mean (two slides ahead).

Worked example

Mean and RMS of a sinusoidal current

The current through a resistor is \(I(t) = 10\sin t\) A, with \(t\) in seconds. Find its mean and RMS values over \(0 \le t \le t_f\), first with \(t_f = \pi\) s (half a cycle) and then with \(t_f = 2\pi\) s (one full cycle). Relate the results to the power dissipated.

Known: \(I(t) = 10\sin t\) A, period \(2\pi\) s. Find: \(\bar I\) and \(I_\text{rms}\) for both windows. Assume: \(R\) constant.

  1. Mean: \(\displaystyle \bar I = \frac{1}{t_f}\int_0^{t_f} 10\sin t\,dt = \frac{1}{t_f}\Big[-10\cos t\Big]_0^{t_f} = \frac{10\,(1 - \cos t_f)}{t_f}\).
  2. \(t_f = \pi\): \(\bar I = 10(1+1)/\pi = 20/\pi = 6.37\) A. \(t_f = 2\pi\): \(\bar I = 10(1-1)/2\pi = 0\).
  3. RMS: use \(\sin^2 t = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2t)\): $$I_\text{rms}^2 = \frac{100}{t_f}\int_0^{t_f}\tfrac12(1-\cos 2t)\,dt = \frac{100}{t_f}\Big[\frac t2 - \frac{\sin 2t}{4}\Big]_0^{t_f}$$
  4. For \(t_f = \pi\) and \(t_f = 2\pi\), \(\sin 2t_f = 0\), so \(I_\text{rms}^2 = 100/2 = 50\) and \(I_\text{rms} = \sqrt{50} = 7.07\) A in both cases.
  5. Average power \(= I_\text{rms}^2 R = 50R\) W for either window. Each half-cycle heats the resistor equally; the mean (zero over a full cycle) hides this, the RMS shows it.

For any sine of amplitude \(C\), averaged over whole half-cycles: \(y_\text{rms} = C/\sqrt2 = 0.707\,C\). The mean over a half-cycle is \(2C/\pi = 0.637\,C\).

Orange: \(I(t)\). Dashed lines: mean over \(0\)–\(\pi\) (6.37 A), mean over \(0\)–\(2\pi\) (0 A) and RMS (7.07 A, same for both windows).

Signal analysis · discrete data

For sampled data, integrals become sums

A data-acquisition system stores \(N\) numbers \(y_0, y_1, \dots, y_{N-1}\), where \(y_i = y(t_1 + i\,\delta t)\). The record length is \(N\delta t = t_2 - t_1\).

Replace the integral by a sum: each sample stands for a strip of width \(\delta t\), so \(\int y\,dt \approx \sum y_i\,\delta t\). Dividing by \(t_2 - t_1 = N\delta t\) cancels \(\delta t\):

Mean and RMS of N samples $$\bar y = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1} y_i \qquad y_\text{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1} y_i^2}$$
\(y_i\)the \(i\)-th sample, same unit as the signal \(N\)number of samples (an integer) \(\delta t\)sample interval, s; \(1/\delta t\) is the sample rate in samples per second (Hz)

The sums estimate the true mean and RMS of the analog signal only if the samples are close enough together to follow the waveform and the record spans whole cycles (or very many cycles). Both conditions return in Topic 05 on data acquisition.

Quick number: samples 2, 4, 3, 5 V give \(\bar y = 14/4 = 3.5\) V and \(y_\text{rms} = \sqrt{(4+16+9+25)/4} = \sqrt{13.5} = 3.67\) V.

Worked example

Mean and RMS of a sampled voltage record

A transducer output is sampled ten times, once every 0.1 s. The readings (V) are 31.2, 32.0, 31.5, 30.1, 29.4, 29.6, 30.3, 30.9, 30.4, 29.8. Find the mean, the RMS, and the RMS of the fluctuations.

Known: \(N = 10\), \(\delta t = 0.1\) s. Find: \(\bar y\), \(y_\text{rms}\), \(y'_\text{rms}\). Assume: the record is long enough to be representative.

  1. Sum of readings: \(\sum y_i = 305.2\) V. Mean: \(\bar y = 305.2/10 = 30.52\) V.
  2. Sum of squares: \(\sum y_i^2 = 9321.32\) V². RMS: \(y_\text{rms} = \sqrt{932.132} = 30.53\) V.
  3. The RMS is almost equal to the mean. It is dominated by the 30.5 V offset and tells us nothing about the swings.
  4. Subtract the mean: deviations \(y'_i = y_i - \bar y\) are 0.68, 1.48, 0.98, −0.42, −1.12, −0.92, −0.22, 0.38, −0.12, −0.72 V (they sum to zero, a useful check).
  5. \(\sum y_i'^2 = 6.616\) V², so \(y'_\text{rms} = \sqrt{0.6616} = 0.81\) V.
  6. Check: \(\bar y^2 + y'^2_\text{rms} = 931.47 + 0.66 = 932.13 = y_\text{rms}^2\). ✓

Signal analysis · DC offset

Subtract the mean to see the fluctuations

Top: the raw signal on a 0–35 V scale. Bottom: the same record with its mean removed, on a ±2.5 V scale.

When the AC part is what interests you (vibration, ripple, turbulence), remove the DC part first. Then you can expand the vertical scale and the small swings become visible. The RMS of what remains measures their size.

  1. Split the signal: \(y = \bar y + y'\), where \(y'\) is the fluctuation and its mean is zero, \(\overline{y'} = 0\).
  2. Square and average: \(\overline{y^2} = \overline{(\bar y + y')^2} = \bar y^2 + 2\bar y\,\overline{y'} + \overline{y'^2}\).
  3. The middle term is zero because \(\overline{y'} = 0\). So:
RMS splits into DC and AC parts $$y_\text{rms}^2 = \bar y^2 + y'^2_\text{rms}$$

Squares of the DC and AC parts add, like the two sides of a right triangle. A 30.5 V offset with 0.8 V of ripple has \(\sqrt{30.5^2 + 0.8^2} = 30.51\) V RMS: the ripple barely moves the total, which is why you must look at \(y'_\text{rms}\) on its own.

To measure 1 cm ripples on a lake 30 m deep, you measure from the mean water surface, not from the lake bed. Subtracting the DC offset moves your ruler to the surface.

Average over whole cycles, or the mean misleads you

Averaging windowmeanRMS

The signal is \(y = A_0 + C\sin 6\pi t\) plus optional random noise of RMS \(\sigma\). Its period is \(T = 2\pi/6\pi = 1/3\) s. The averaging time is the window length \(t_2 - t_1\) you integrate over; the mean and RMS depend on it unless it is a whole number of periods or very long.

Long-record RMS of offset + sine + noise $$y_\text{rms}^2 = A_0^2 + \frac{C^2}{2} + \sigma^2$$

Start at the default (A₀ = 25, C = 10, window 0.10 s): the mean reads 31.9, not 25, because 0.1 s is 0.3 of a period and catches mostly the positive half. Now set the window to 0.33 s (one period): mean 25.0, RMS 25.98. Then drag it to 2.00 s (six periods). Finally add noise σ = 3 and watch the RMS approach \(\sqrt{25^2 + 50 + 9} = 26.15\).

For \(y = 25 + 10\sin 6\pi t\), what are the mean and RMS over 0.4 to 0.5 s?

Mean 31.94, RMS 32.09: the same as for 0 to 0.1 s. Over 0–0.1 s the angle \(6\pi t\) runs from 0 to \(0.6\pi\); over 0.4–0.5 s it runs from \(2.4\pi\) to \(3\pi\), which is \(0.4\pi\) to \(\pi\) after removing one whole cycle. Those two pieces of the sine are mirror images about its peak at \(\pi/2\), so they enclose the same area. Both windows are 0.3 of a period long and sit on the positive half, so both overestimate the true mean of 25.

Signal amplitude and frequency

Where sines come from: a mass on a spring

A spring of stiffness \(k = 5000\) N/cm carries a 1 kg mass. What is its natural frequency?

  1. Newton’s second law with the spring force \(F = -ky\) (no damping, no external force): \(m\,\ddot y = -ky\), or \(m\ddot y + ky = 0\).
  2. Try \(y = A\cos\omega t + B\sin\omega t\). Then \(\ddot y = -\omega^2 y\), so \(-m\omega^2 y + ky = 0\), which holds for all \(t\) if \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\).
  3. \(A\) and \(B\) are set by how you start the motion: \(A = y(0)\) and \(B = \dot y(0)/\omega\).
Period, frequency, circular frequency $$T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \frac{1}{f}$$
\(T\)period: time for one complete cycle, s \(f\)frequency: cycles per second, Hz (1 Hz = 1 cycle/s) \(\omega\)circular frequency: rad/s. One cycle is one trip round a circle, \(2\pi\) rad, so \(\omega = 2\pi f\) \(C\)amplitude: the largest displacement from the mean (here from equilibrium)

Quick number: \(y = 8\cos 8t\) has \(\omega = 8\) rad/s, \(f = 8/2\pi = 1.27\) Hz and \(T = 0.785\) s.

Signal amplitude and frequency · phase

Sine plus cosine is one sine, shifted by a phase angle

Rotating arrow (phasor), ω = 4 rad/sy(t)B sin ωtA cos ωt

Set A = 0: the orange wave is a pure sine, φ* = 0. Set B = 0: a pure cosine, which is a sine shifted ahead by a quarter cycle (φ* = π/2 = 1.571 rad). Any mix gives a sine of length \(C = \sqrt{A^2+B^2}\).

The spring-mass solution \(A\cos\omega t + B\sin\omega t\) is really one sine. Expand \(C\sin(\omega t + \phi^*) = C\cos\phi^*\,\sin\omega t + C\sin\phi^*\,\cos\omega t\) and match terms: \(B = C\cos\phi^*\) and \(A = C\sin\phi^*\). Squaring and adding, or dividing, gives:

Amplitude and phase of a sine–cosine pair $$A\cos\omega t + B\sin\omega t = C\sin(\omega t + \phi^*)$$ $$\phantom{A\cos\omega t + B\sin\omega t} = C\cos(\omega t - \phi)$$ $$C = \sqrt{A^2+B^2},\qquad \tan\phi^* = \frac AB$$ $$\tan\phi = \frac BA,\qquad \phi = \frac\pi2 - \phi^*$$

The phase angle \(\phi^*\) says how far (in radians) the wave is shifted along the time axis relative to a plain \(\sin\omega t\). A positive \(\phi^*\) means the wave reaches its peak earlier by \(\phi^*/\omega\) seconds.

A Ferris wheel seen from the side: each seat goes up and down as a sine. Two seats on the same wheel have the same amplitude and frequency but reach the top at different times; that offset is the phase.

Here \(A = 3\), \(B = 5\), \(\omega = 4\) rad/s. \(C = \sqrt{9 + 25} = \sqrt{34} = 5.83\). \(\phi^* = \tan^{-1}(3/5) = 0.540\) rad, so \(y = 5.83\sin(4t + 0.540)\). As a cosine: \(\phi = \tan^{-1}(5/3) = 1.030\) rad, \(y = 5.83\cos(4t - 1.030)\). Period \(T = 2\pi/4 = 1.571\) s, \(f = 0.637\) Hz. These are the simulator’s default settings.

Check yourself

What is the RMS of \(y = 3 + 4\sin\omega t\) over many periods?

Two sampled signals both have mean 0. Signal 1 swings ±20 V, signal 2 swings ±26 V, both sinusoidal and sampled over whole cycles. How do their RMS values compare?

A 10-bit converter covers 0 to 5 V. What is the largest rounding (quantization) error?

\(A = 15\), \(B = 4\). \(C = \sqrt{15^2 + 4^2} = 15.52\). Sine form: \(\phi^* = \tan^{-1}(15/4) = 1.310\) rad, so \(y = 15.52\sin(2\pi t + 1.310)\). Cosine form: \(\phi = \tan^{-1}(4/15) = 0.261\) rad, so \(y = 15.52\cos(2\pi t - 0.261)\). Check: \(1.310 + 0.261 = 1.571 = \pi/2\). ✓
