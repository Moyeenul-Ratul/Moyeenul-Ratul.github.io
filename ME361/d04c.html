Amplifiers, Filters and Grounding

Where we are

Part B gave us a small voltage with an output resistance

A strain-gauge bridge with 5 V excitation gives about 2.5 mV at a strain of \(10^{-3}\). Seen from its output terminals it behaves like a source in series with a resistance \(R_e\) of a few hundred ohms. Part B showed that whatever you connect must have an input impedance at least 100 to 1000 times \(R_e\), or it reads low: that is the loading error.

Three jobs remain before such a signal can be recorded:

  • Make it bigger without loading the source: an amplifier with very high input impedance.
  • Make it cleaner: remove mains hum, high-frequency hiss or a drifting offset with a filter.
  • Keep it clean on the way: grounding, shielding and the right cable.

Part B also showed signals that need special handling: the charge of a piezoelectric crystal, which leaks and depends on cable capacitance.

Think of a faint radio station. The amplifier is the volume knob, the filter is the tuner that rejects other stations, and grounding and shielding are the walls that stop your neighbour’s drill from coming through the speaker.

Amplifiers

The operational amplifier: enormous gain in a small package

An amplifier scales a signal: a linear amplifier gives \(E_o(t)=G\,E_i(t)\), where the gain \(G\) may be positive, negative, or less than 1. In decibels, \(G_{dB}=20\log_{10}|G|\): a gain of 100 is 40 dB. Other amplifiers apply other functions, such as \(E_o=G\log E_i\). Every real amplifier works only over a limited input range and a limited frequency band.

The workhorse is the operational amplifier (op-amp), an integrated circuit with two inputs and one output, powered from a dual supply, typically ±5 V to ±15 V. It amplifies the difference between its inputs:

Op-amp open-loop law$$E_o=A_o\,(E_+-E_-)$$
\(E_+\)non-inverting input: output follows it in phase\(E_-\)inverting input: output moves opposite to it\(A_o\)open-loop gain, \(10^5\) to \(10^6\) at low frequency, falling at high frequency

Typical: input impedance above \(10^7\ \Omega\) (above \(10^{12}\ \Omega\) for field-effect inputs), output impedance below 100 Ω. Two extra pins allow a small trim of the output offset to zero.

Used open loop, an op-amp is useless as an amplifier. With \(A_o=2\times10^5\) and a ±15 V supply that saturates near ±13.5 V, an input difference of \(13.5/(2\times10^5)=68\ \mu\)V drives it to the limit. Its \(A_o\) also varies by large factors between units and with temperature. The next slide shows how feedback turns this unruly gain into a precise one.

Amplifiers · feedback

Negative feedback trades raw gain for a gain you can trust

A driver keeping a car on a lane line does not need a precisely calibrated steering wheel. They watch the error and correct it continuously. An op-amp with feedback is that driver: it watches \(E_+-E_-\) and pushes its output until the error is essentially zero, and the resistors define where “zero error” lies.

  1. The divider feeds back \(E_-=\beta E_o\), with \(\beta=\dfrac{R_1}{R_1+R_2}\).
  2. Open-loop law: \(E_o=A_o(E_i-\beta E_o)\). Collect \(E_o\): \(E_o(1+A_o\beta)=A_oE_i\). $$G=\frac{E_o}{E_i}=\frac{A_o}{1+A_o\beta}\;\xrightarrow{A_o\beta\gg1}\;\frac1\beta=1+\frac{R_2}{R_1}$$
  3. Numbers: \(R_2/R_1=99\), so \(\beta=0.01\). With \(A_o=10^5\), \(G=99.90\). If \(A_o\) halves to \(5\times10^4\), \(G=99.80\): a 50 % change in the op-amp moves the gain by only 0.1 %. The gain is now set by a resistor ratio.
  4. How big is the error voltage? At \(E_o=10\) V, \(E_+-E_-=E_o/A_o=10/10^5=0.1\) mV. Compared with volts, the two inputs are at the same potential.

The two ideal op-amp rules, valid with negative feedback and an unsaturated output: (1) no current flows into either input (input impedance is huge); (2) the output adjusts so that \(E_+=E_-\) (because \(A_o\) is huge). Every circuit on the next slides is solved with these two rules and Kirchhoff’s current law.

Standard circuits · 1

Inverting and non-inverting amplifiers from the two rules

Inverting amplifier

  1. Rule 2: \(E_+=0\), so \(E_-=0\). The inverting node is a virtual ground: at 0 V without being connected to ground.
  2. Current through R₁: \(I=E_i/R_1\). Rule 1: none enters the op-amp, so all of it flows on through R₂.
  3. \(E_o=0-IR_2\), so \(G=-\dfrac{R_2}{R_1}\). The input impedance is just \(R_1\).

\(R_1=10\) kΩ, \(R_2=100\) kΩ gives \(G=-10\). Note that 10 kΩ of input impedance would load a bridge with \(R_e=6.5\) kΩ badly.

Non-inverting amplifier

The circuit of the previous slide. With the rules: \(E_-=E_+=E_i\); the current through R₁ is \(E_i/R_1\) and the same current flows through R₂, so \(E_o=E_i+R_2E_i/R_1\):

Non-inverting gain$$G=1+\frac{R_2}{R_1}=\frac{R_1+R_2}{R_1}$$

The signal enters an op-amp input directly, so the input impedance is the op-amp’s own, multiplied by \(1+A_o\beta\) by the feedback: effectively infinite. The output is in phase with the input. A small resistor equal to \(R_1\parallel R_2\) is often placed in series with the + input so that the tiny bias currents of the two inputs see equal resistances and their offsets cancel; it does not change the gain.

Standard circuits · 2

The voltage follower buffers; the differential amplifier subtracts

Voltage follower (buffer)

The output is wired straight to the inverting input, so \(\beta=1\):

  1. \(E_o=A_o(E_i-E_o)\Rightarrow G=\dfrac{A_o}{1+A_o}\approx1\). With \(A_o=2\times10^5\), \(G=0.999995\).
  2. Input impedance: the op-amp’s own times \(1+A_o\), effectively infinite. Output impedance: the op-amp’s own divided by \(1+A_o\); 75 Ω becomes 0.4 mΩ.
  3. It draws almost nothing from the source and can drive almost any load: the ideal buffer of Part B. It is the cure for the pH electrode and other high-impedance sensors.

Differential amplifier

  1. The + node is a divider: \(E_+=E_2\,R_2/(R_1+R_2)\). Rule 2 sets \(E_-\) equal to it.
  2. Rule 1 at the − node: \((E_1-E_-)/R_1=(E_--E_o)/R_2\). Substitute and simplify: $$E_o=\frac{R_2}{R_1}\,(E_2-E_1)$$
  3. A voltage common to both inputs (the common-mode voltage) cancels. The common-mode rejection ratio \(\text{CMRR}=20\log(G_{diff}/G_{cm})\) in dB measures how well; resistor mismatch limits it.

Weakness: its input impedances are only \(R_1\) and \(R_1+R_2\), unequal and modest, so it loads a bridge. The next fix is the instrumentation amplifier.

Gain, bandwidth and clipping of real op-amp circuits

Op-amp bench · GBW 1 MHz, ±13.5 V swinginputoutputopen-loop gain

Non-inverting with R₁ = 10 kΩ, R₂ = 100 kΩ (gain 11) and 0.1 V in: output 1.1 V. Raise R₂ to 1 MΩ: gain 101, and the closed-loop bandwidth shrinks to about 10 kHz. Push the frequency past it and the gain falls and the output lags. Raise the input to 0.5 V: the output tries for 50 V and flattens at 13.5 V.

The open-loop gain of a general-purpose op-amp falls by a factor of 10 for every factor of 10 in frequency above a few hertz, so gain × bandwidth is constant: the gain–bandwidth product (GBW). A circuit whose feedback divider has \(1/\beta=1+R_2/R_1\) keeps its designed gain only up to about \(f_B=\text{GBW}\,\beta\). Large gains need either a faster op-amp or two stages of moderate gain.

Standard circuits · 3

The instrumentation amplifier: buffered inputs, one gain resistor

An instrumentation amplifier (in-amp) combines two followers-with-gain and a differential amplifier. Derive it with the two rules:

  1. Rule 2 at A₁ and A₂: the two ends of \(R_G\) sit at \(E_1\) and \(E_2\). So \(R_G\) carries \(i=(E_2-E_1)/R_G\).
  2. Rule 1: that same \(i\) flows through both resistors R. The voltage between the two outputs is therefore \(i\,(R+R_G+R)\): \(V_2'-V_1'=(E_2-E_1)\left(1+\dfrac{2R}{R_G}\right)\).
  3. If \(E_1=E_2\) (pure common mode), \(i=0\) and both outputs simply equal the common voltage: the first stage passes common mode at gain 1 while amplifying the difference.
  4. The differential stage subtracts and removes the common mode: $$E_o=\left(1+\frac{2R}{R_G}\right)\frac{R_4}{R_3}\,(E_2-E_1)$$

Both inputs go straight into op-amp + terminals, so each has a very high input impedance and no loading of the bridge. One resistor \(R_G\) sets the gain, and CMRR of 100 dB or more is routine in integrated in-amps.

Worked example

Scaling a strain-gauge bridge to 0–5 V with an in-amp

A quarter bridge has 120 Ω arms, excitation \(E_i=5\) V and an active strain gauge with gauge factor 2.0, so \(\delta R/R=2\,\varepsilon\). Strain ranges from 0 to 2000 µε (\(2\times10^{-3}\)). An in-amp with \(R=25\) kΩ, \(R_3=R_4\) (unity second stage) and CMRR = 100 dB must give 5 V at full scale. Find (a) the full-scale bridge output, (b) the gain and \(R_G\), (c) the error caused by the common-mode voltage.

Known: \(E_i\), arms, gauge factor, strain range, \(R\), CMRR. Find: \(E_o\), \(G\), \(R_G\), common-mode error. Assumptions: ideal in-amp apart from finite CMRR; amplifier draws no current from the bridge.

  1. (a) \(\delta R/R=2.0\times2\times10^{-3}=0.004\). Exact quarter bridge: \(E_o=5\times\dfrac{0.004}{4+0.008}=4.990\) mV.
  2. (b) Gain: \(G=5\ \text{V}/4.990\ \text{mV}=1002\). Choose \(G\approx1000\): \(1+2R/R_G=1000\Rightarrow R_G=50\,000/999=50.05\ \Omega\). The nearest standard 49.9 Ω gives \(G=1003\) and 5.005 V at full scale.
  3. (c) Each bridge output node sits near \(E_i/2=2.5\) V: that is the common-mode voltage. CMRR = 100 dB means the common-mode gain is \(10^5\) times smaller than the difference gain, so the error is \(2.5/10^5=25\ \mu\)V referred to the input.
  4. Relative to the 4.99 mV full-scale signal, 25 µV is 0.5 %. An in-amp with 120 dB CMRR would cut it to 0.05 %.
  5. What this means: the bridge gives mV on top of volts of common mode. Only an amplifier that subtracts very precisely, and loads neither node, can use it. A plain inverting amplifier would have loaded the 120 Ω bridge and amplified the 2.5 V too.

In the in-amp, \(R=25\) kΩ. Which \(R_G\) gives a gain of 101 (second stage unity)?

A data-acquisition converter reading 0–5 V with 12 bits resolves 1.2 mV. Fed the raw 4.99 mV bridge signal it would see only four steps across the whole strain range. After the gain of 1000, the same converter resolves about 0.5 µε.

Special-purpose circuits

Integrate, differentiate, compare, hold, convert charge, send as current

Integrator. The − node is a virtual ground, so \(i=E_i/R\) flows into C, whose voltage obeys \(i=C\,d(0-E_o)/dt\). Hence \(E_o=-\dfrac{1}{RC}\displaystyle\int E_i\,dt\). A constant 1 V with \(R=100\) kΩ, \(C=1\ \mu\)F gives a ramp of −10 V/s, the basis of integrating voltmeters and ramp converters. Integration averages out high-frequency noise.

Differentiator. Swap R and C: \(E_o=-RC\,dE_i/dt\). It amplifies noise in proportion to frequency: a 1 mV ripple at 10 kHz has the same derivative (63 V/s) as a 1 V signal at 10 Hz. A small resistor in series with C limits the high-frequency gain.

Comparator. An op-amp with no feedback: the output sits at \(+E_{sat}\) when \(E_1-E_2\) exceeds a tiny threshold (tens of µV) and at \(-E_{sat}\) when it is below. With \(E_2\) a reference, it answers “is the signal above the reference?”, the basic decision inside every analog-to-digital converter.

Sample-and-hold. A fast switch connects the signal to a capacitor C through a small resistance, so C tracks the input. On command the switch opens and C, read by a follower with tiny input current, holds the value steady while a converter digitises it.

Charge amplifier. An inverting op-amp with a capacitor \(C_r\) as feedback. The crystal’s charge \(q\) is forced onto \(C_r\), so \(E_o\approx-q/C_r\), independent of cable capacitance (strictly \(E_o=-q/(C_r+C_T/A_o)\), with \(C_T\) the total of crystal, cable and feedback capacitance). A charge of 230 pC (100 N on a quartz element with \(d\approx2.3\) pC/N) into \(C_r=1000\) pF gives −0.23 V for any cable length.

4–20 mA current loop. The transmitter converts the signal to a current between 4 mA (zero) and 20 mA (full scale). Wire resistance cannot change a current set by a current source, so the signal survives hundreds of metres. A 250 Ω resistor at the receiver gives 1–5 V, and a reading of 0 mA reveals a broken wire.

Filters

A filter passes one band of frequencies and blocks the rest

The passband is the frequency range a filter lets through; the stopband is the range it blocks; the cutoff frequency \(f_c\) is the boundary, defined as the frequency where the output power is halved, so the magnitude ratio is \(M=1/\sqrt2=0.707\), or −3 dB (\(20\log 0.707=-3.0\)). The transition band lies between them, and its steepness is the roll-off, in dB per decade of frequency.

A low-pass filter keeps slow content and removes fast noise, and it is the anti-aliasing filter in front of every sampled measurement. A high-pass filter removes a DC offset or slow drift. Band-pass combines the two; a notch removes one narrow band.

A real filter cannot be a brick wall. It also shifts the phase of what it passes, and different frequencies are delayed by different amounts, which can distort a waveform. Designs optimise one of three things, and no single design achieves all three:

  1. the flattest possible magnitude in the passband (Butterworth);
  2. a phase that is linear in frequency, so every component is delayed by the same time and shapes are preserved (Bessel);
  3. the steepest possible roll-off (other families, at the cost of ripple).

Passive filters use only R, L and C; active filters add op-amps, which provide gain, avoid inductors and isolate the load.

A filter is a doorman checking the frequency of each guest. A first-order doorman is lenient near the line and lets many slightly-too-fast guests in. A high-order doorman checks every ID and turns away almost everyone above the line.

First-order filters

The RC low-pass filter is the first-order system of Topic 02

  1. Low-pass, no load on the output. The capacitor current is \(i=C\,dE_o/dt\) and the same current flows through R, so Kirchhoff’s voltage law gives \(E_i=iR+E_o\): $$RC\,\frac{dE_o}{dt}+E_o=E_i$$
  2. This is the first-order system of Topic 02 with time constant \(\tau=RC\). For a sine input at \(\omega=2\pi f\) its magnitude ratio and phase are $$M(f)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\omega\tau)^2}},\qquad\phi=-\tan^{-1}(\omega\tau)$$
  3. Set \(M=0.707\): \(\omega\tau=1\), so \(f_c=\dfrac{1}{2\pi RC}\) and \(M=1/\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}\). Far above \(f_c\), \(M\approx f_c/f\): ten times the frequency, one tenth the amplitude, a roll-off of 20 dB/decade.
  4. The high-pass circuit takes its output across R and gives \(M=1/\sqrt{1+(f_c/f)^2}\) with the same \(f_c\).

Design a one-stage RC low-pass filter with \(f_c=100\) Hz using \(R=50\ \Omega\). Then, with the nearest stock capacitor, find the cutoff, the dynamic error \(\delta=M-1\) and the attenuation at 192 Hz.

  1. \(\tau=RC=\dfrac{1}{2\pi(100)}=1.59\) ms, so \(C=1.59\times10^{-3}/50=31.8\ \mu\)F.
  2. Stock value 33 µF: \(f_c=\dfrac{1}{2\pi(50)(33\times10^{-6})}=96.5\) Hz.
  3. At 192 Hz, \(f/f_c=1.99\): \(M=1/\sqrt{1+1.99^2}=0.449\), so \(\delta=-0.551\): 55 % of the 192 Hz content is removed.
  4. Attenuation \(=10\log[1+(f/f_c)^2]=10\log(4.96)=7.0\) dB; the phase lag is 63°.
  5. What this means: one octave above cutoff a first-order filter still passes 45 % of the amplitude. For sharp separation you need more stages.

Higher-order filters

Cascade reactive elements for a flatter passband and a steeper roll-off

Adding capacitors and inductors in a ladder (each one a stage) raises the filter’s order \(k\). The Butterworth design chooses the element values so the passband is as flat as possible. Its magnitude ratio is

k-stage Butterworth low-pass $$M(f)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^{2k}}}$$ $$A=10\log_{10}\!\left[1+(f/f_c)^{2k}\right]\ \text{dB}$$

\(k=1\) is the RC filter. Every \(k\) gives −3 dB at \(f_c\); well above it the roll-off is \(20k\) dB/decade. For high-pass, replace \(f/f_c\) by \(f_c/f\).

Bessel filters choose element values for a nearly linear phase instead: all passband frequencies are delayed by the same time, so a step or a pulse keeps its shape without ringing. The price is a softer knee near \(f_c\). Active version: the inverting amplifier with a capacitor \(C_2\) across \(R_2\) is a first-order low-pass with \(f_c=1/(2\pi R_2C_2)\) and gain \(-R_2/R_1\); for 100 Hz with \(R_2=100\) kΩ, \(C_2=15.9\) nF.

High-pass at 2 Hz removes nothing useful here; push it to 20 Hz and the 5 Hz signal itself disappears.

Worked example

How many stages for 60 dB at ten times the cutoff?

A Butterworth low-pass filter must have \(f_c=3\) kHz and attenuate content at 30 kHz by at least 60 dB. Source and load resistances are both 50 Ω. Find the number of stages and the element values of an LC ladder.

Known: \(f_c\), \(f=30\) kHz, \(A\ge60\) dB, \(R_s=R_L=50\ \Omega\). Find: \(k\), \(L\), \(C\). Assumptions: ideal L and C; ladder as drawn on the previous slides.

  1. Normalised frequency: \(f/f_c=30\,000/3000=10\).
  2. Solve the attenuation formula for \(k\): \(10^{A/10}=1+10^{2k}\Rightarrow k=\dfrac{\log_{10}(10^{6}-1)}{2\log_{10}10}=3.00\). Check: \(k=2\) gives 40.0 dB (too little); \(k=3\) gives 60.0 dB. So k = 3.
  3. Table row \(k=3\): \(C_1=C_3=1.000\) F, \(L_2=2.000\) H (normalised).
  4. Scale: \(L=\dfrac{L_2R}{2\pi f_c}=\dfrac{2(50)}{2\pi(3000)}=5.31\) mH.
  5. \(C=\dfrac{C_1}{2\pi f_cR}=\dfrac{1}{2\pi(3000)(50)}=1.06\ \mu\)F for both \(C_1\) and \(C_3\).
  6. What this means: a 1 kHz signal component passes with \(M=1/\sqrt{1+(1/3)^6}=0.9993\), a loss of only 0.07 %, while 30 kHz is cut to one-thousandth. Three reactive parts buy a 1000:1 separation over one decade.

Grounds, shielding and connecting wires

One ground point, shield at one end, twist the pair

Two surveyors measure heights from “ground level”, one standing on the street and one in the basement. Each is right about his own zero, but subtract their readings and you get the depth of the basement, not the height of anything you meant to measure.

Ground. A ground is a return path to earth, but it is not an absolute zero: building wiring carries current and picks up voltage, so two ground points differ. That difference is a common-mode voltage. A ground loop forms when a circuit is grounded at two points: the difference drives a current through the signal return wire, adding hum or an offset to the reading. Rule: ground the whole system at one point; let the other end float. A third-prong mains ground on an instrument can create the second point unless its supply is isolated.

Shields. Long wires act as antennas. Mains wiring couples to them through stray capacitance: 1 pF to 230 V at 50 Hz has a reactance of 3.2 GΩ and injects about 72 nA, which is 72 mV across a 1 MΩ input but only 72 µV across 1 kΩ. A shield (foil or braid connected to ground at one end) intercepts this electric field. Low source impedance helps too.

Twisted pairs. A changing magnetic field (motors, transformers) induces voltages in the loop formed by the two wires. Twisting reverses the loop orientation every half twist, so successive induced voltages cancel; more twists per metre, better cancellation.

Wires: plain single or flat cable only for signals of a volt or more; shielded twisted pair for low-level (below about 100 mV) signals; coaxial cable for low-level high-frequency signals; optical fibre for long runs in electrically noisy plant.
