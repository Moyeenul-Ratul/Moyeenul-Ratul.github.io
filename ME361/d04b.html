Bridges, Loading and Impedance

A strain gauge changes by a fraction of an ohm, an RTD by a few ohms. The Wheatstone bridge turns those tiny changes into a voltage you can read. Then every meter you connect borrows a little of that signal. This part shows how to read small resistance changes and how to keep the act of reading from spoiling them.

Where we are

Parts A to C left us with small resistance changes and meters that disturb

Parts A and B introduced the transducers. The resistive ones (strain gauge, RTD, potentiometer) are the most common, and their change is small: a 120 Ω strain gauge at a strain of \(10^{-3}\) changes by 0.24 Ω, which is 0.2 % of its value.

Part C showed two ways of using a meter. In deflection mode the meter’s pointer angle is the reading, and the meter must take some energy from the circuit to move. In null mode you adjust a known quantity until a detector reads zero; at zero current the detector takes nothing. A voltmeter with 40 kΩ resistance on a source with 10 kΩ internal resistance read 0.800 V instead of 1.000 V. That 20 % shortfall was the first example of a loading error.

This part answers two questions. How do you read a tiny \(\Delta R\) precisely? And how do you predict, and keep small, the error that every connected instrument causes?

A voltage divider is two resistors in series across a supply \(E_i\). The same current \(I=E_i/(R_a+R_b)\) flows through both, so the voltage across \(R_a\) is \(I R_a\): $$E_a=E_i\,\frac{R_a}{R_a+R_b}$$ The output is a fixed fraction of the supply, set only by the resistance ratio.

Weighing a ship’s captain by weighing the ship with and without him is hopeless: his 80 kg vanishes in the scale’s uncertainty. You want an instrument that compares the captain with a known weight directly. The bridge does that for resistance: it cancels the large common part and shows only the difference.

Wheatstone bridge · the idea

A bridge is two voltage dividers, read against each other

Call the bottom rail node A (0 V) and the top rail node D (+\(E_i\)). Each vertical branch is a voltage divider, so the two middle nodes sit at

Node voltages of the two dividers $$E_B=E_i\frac{R_1}{R_1+R_2},\qquad E_C=E_i\frac{R_3}{R_3+R_4}$$

A voltmeter between B and C reads the difference \(E_o=E_B-E_C\). If all four resistors are equal, both nodes sit at \(E_i/2\) and \(E_o=0\): the large common voltage cancels exactly. When R₁ changes slightly, only B moves, and the meter sees the small change on a zero background. You can now use a sensitive low-range meter instead of trying to spot 0.2 % on a full-scale reading.

Reading a small change as the difference of two nearly equal voltages is the whole trick. The reference divider subtracts the part of the signal you do not care about.

Wheatstone bridge · null method

At balance no current crosses the bridge, and the supply drops out

The bridge is balanced when the detector current \(I_g=0\). Find the condition:

  1. With \(I_g=0\), B and C are at the same potential, so the drops from A are equal: \(I_1R_1=I_3R_3\). The drops to D are equal too: \(I_2R_2=I_4R_4\).
  2. No current leaves through G, so each arm pair carries one current: \(I_1=I_2\) and \(I_3=I_4\).
  3. Divide the second equation by the first and the currents cancel: $$\frac{R_2}{R_1}=\frac{R_4}{R_3}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad R_1=R_2\,\frac{R_3}{R_4}$$

In the null method, R₂ is a calibrated adjustable resistor. When the sensor changes, you turn R₂ until G reads zero and read R₁ from the dial. Three advantages: \(E_i\) is absent from the result, so supply drift does not matter; G only has to say “zero or not”; and at balance no current is drawn, so there is no loading. The cost is speed: a hand or a servo must rebalance for every reading.

An RTD of 100 Ω at 0 °C sits in R₁, with \(R_3=R_4=500\ \Omega\). What R₂ balances the bridge at 0 °C, and at 50 °C if \(R=R_0[1+0.00395\,T]\)?

At 0 °C: \(R_2=R_1R_4/R_3=100\ \Omega\). At 50 °C, \(R_1=100(1+0.1975)=119.75\ \Omega\), so \(R_2=119.75\ \Omega\). With equal ratio arms the dial reads the sensor resistance directly.

Wheatstone bridge · deflection method

Deflection mode: let the bridge go out of balance and read the voltage

Rebalancing is slow and cannot follow a vibrating strain or a fluctuating temperature. In the deflection method you balance the bridge once at a reference condition, then leave it alone and read the out-of-balance voltage \(E_o\) with a voltmeter of very high resistance. The output follows the sensor as fast as the meter can respond.

Two identical see-saws side by side, both level. A child on the left one gets a little heavier and its middle dips. \(E_o\) is the height difference between the two middles: zero when both are level, and growing with the extra weight.

Deflection mode needs what the null method did not: a known, stable \(E_i\) (the output is proportional to it) and an accurately calibrated voltmeter.

  1. Subtract the two divider voltages from the previous slide: $$E_o=E_i\left(\frac{R_1}{R_1+R_2}-\frac{R_3}{R_3+R_4}\right)$$ This is exact for any four resistors, provided the meter draws no current.
  2. Most bridges start with all four arms equal to \(R\), so \(E_o=0\). Now let the sensor change: \(R_1=R+\delta R\).
  3. The reference divider still gives \(\tfrac12\). The sensor divider gives \(\dfrac{R+\delta R}{2R+\delta R}\). Subtract and put over a common denominator: $$\frac{\delta E_o}{E_i}=\frac{2(R+\delta R)-(2R+\delta R)}{2(2R+\delta R)}=\frac{\delta R}{4R+2\,\delta R}$$
  4. Divide top and bottom by \(R\): $$\frac{\delta E_o}{E_i}=\frac{\delta R/R}{4+2\,(\delta R/R)}\;\approx\;\frac14\,\frac{\delta R}{R}\quad(\delta R\ll R)$$
  5. The \(2\,\delta R/R\) term is the bridge’s nonlinearity. For a strain gauge (\(\delta R/R=0.002\)) it changes the result by 0.1 %, negligible. For an RTD that changes by 25 %, it matters a lot.

Wheatstone bridge · sensitivity

More active arms, more output: quarter, half and full bridges

The bridge sensitivity is the output change per unit change of sensor resistance near balance. Differentiating the sensor divider gives \(dE_o/dR_1=E_iR_2/(R_1+R_2)^2\), which for equal arms is \(E_i/(4R)\) volts per ohm.

Each arm pulls \(E_o\) in a definite direction: raising R₁ lifts B, raising R₂ lowers B, raising R₃ lifts C (so \(E_o\) falls), raising R₄ lowers C. For small changes the effects add:

Small changes in all four arms $$\frac{\delta E_o}{E_i}\approx\frac14\left(\frac{\delta R_1}{R}-\frac{\delta R_2}{R}-\frac{\delta R_3}{R}+\frac{\delta R_4}{R}\right)$$

Rule: changes in adjacent arms (sharing a node, e.g. R₁ and R₂) subtract; changes in opposite arms (R₁ and R₄) add.

A dummy strain gauge, unstrained but at the same temperature, is used to cancel temperature drift of the active gauge in R₁. Where should it go?

ArrangementActive arms\(\delta E_o/E_i\)
Quarter bridgeR₁: \(+\delta R\)\(\dfrac{\delta R/R}{4+2\delta R/R}\) (slightly nonlinear)
Half bridgeR₁: \(+\delta R\), R₂: \(-\delta R\)\(\tfrac12\,\delta R/R\) exactly
Full bridgeR₁, R₄: \(+\delta R\); R₂, R₃: \(-\delta R\)\(\delta R/R\) exactly

Why the half bridge is exactly linear: \(R_1+R_2=2R\) stays constant, so \(E_B=E_i(R+\delta R)/2R\) is a straight line in \(\delta R\). A beam in bending gives this naturally, with one gauge in tension on top and one in compression underneath.

Raising \(E_i\) raises the output in proportion, but each arm then carries current \(I=E_i/2R\) and dissipates \(I^2R=E_i^2/4R\). Doubling \(E_i\) doubles the signal and quadruples the self-heating of the sensor. The excitation is a compromise between sensitivity and heating error.

Worked example

Reading a temperature from an RTD deflection bridge

All four arms of a bridge are 100 Ω at 0 °C. R₁ is a resistance temperature sensor with \(R=R_0[1+\alpha(T-T_0)]\), \(R_0=100\ \Omega\) at \(T_0=0\ ^\circ\)C and \(\alpha=0.00395\ ^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\). The supply is \(E_i=10\) V. The bridge output reads \(E_o=0.569\) V. Find the sensor temperature, the current through the sensor and the power it dissipates.

Known: \(R=100\ \Omega\), \(E_i=10\) V, \(E_o=0.569\) V, \(\alpha\). Find: \(T_1\), \(I_1\), \(P_1\). Assumptions: voltmeter draws no current; supply has negligible internal resistance; only R₁ changes.

  1. Use the exact quarter-bridge result with \(\delta E_o/E_i=0.0569\): \(0.0569=\dfrac{\delta R}{400+2\,\delta R}\).
  2. Solve: \(\delta R\,(1-2\times0.0569)=0.0569\times400\), so \(\delta R=\dfrac{22.76}{0.8862}=25.68\ \Omega\) and \(R_1=125.68\ \Omega\).
  3. Temperature: \(T_1=\dfrac{\delta R}{R_0\alpha}=\dfrac{25.68}{100\times0.00395}=65.0\ ^\circ\)C.
  4. Had you used the linear rule \(\delta R\approx4R\,E_o/E_i=22.76\ \Omega\), you would report 57.6 °C, about 7 °C low.
  5. Sensor current (the meter takes none): \(I_1=\dfrac{E_i}{R_1+R_2}=\dfrac{10}{225.68}=44.3\) mA, down from 50 mA at balance.
  6. Power: \(P_1=I_1^2R_1=(0.0443)^2(125.68)=0.247\) W.
  7. What this means: a quarter watt warms a small sensor noticeably above the fluid it measures, so the reading is biased high. That is a loading error of the thermal kind. A lower \(E_i\) would cut it by the square of the ratio.

You halve the excitation to 5 V. What happens to the output for the same temperature, and to the sensor’s self-heating?

Put \(T=65\ ^\circ\)C back in: \(R_1=125.675\ \Omega\) and \(E_o=10\,(125.675/225.675-0.5)=0.5688\) V, which rounds to the 0.569 V reading.

The bridge seen from its terminals

Seen from the meter, a bridge is one voltage source behind one resistance

Thévenin’s theorem: any network of linear resistors and sources, seen from two terminals, behaves like a single source \(E_{th}\) in series with a single resistance \(R_{th}\). \(E_{th}\) is the open-circuit voltage at the terminals; \(R_{th}\) is the resistance you would measure looking into them with every ideal voltage source replaced by a short circuit.

  1. Open-circuit voltage between B and C: the deflection equation, call it \(E_o'\).
  2. Short the supply, so A and D become one node. From B you see R₁ and R₂ in parallel to that node, and from it R₃ and R₄ in parallel to C: $$R_e=\frac{R_1R_2}{R_1+R_2}+\frac{R_3R_4}{R_3+R_4}$$
  3. A voltmeter of resistance \(R_m\) forms a divider with \(R_e\): \(E_m=E_o'\dfrac{R_m}{R_m+R_e}\). It reads low unless \(R_m\gg R_e\).
  4. A current detector of resistance \(R_g\) passes \(I_g=E_o'/(R_e+R_g)\). For equal arms, \(R_e=R\) and \(E_o'\approx E_i\,\delta R/4R\): $$I_g\approx\frac{E_i\,(\delta R/R)}{4\,(R+R_g)}$$

The supply sees the bridge as \(R_B=(R_1+R_2)\parallel(R_3+R_4)\), which is \(R\) for equal arms. A supply of open-circuit voltage \(E_s\) and internal resistance \(R_s\) then delivers only \(E_i=E_s R_B/(R_s+R_B)\). With 100 Ω arms and \(R_s=1\ \Omega\), \(E_i=0.990\,E_s\): a 1 % sensitivity error unless you measure \(E_i\) at the bridge terminals.

One line of arithmetic (\(R_e\)) replaces three simultaneous Kirchhoff equations, and it tells you at once how large the meter resistance must be.

How good is a bridge reading?

Bridge uncertainty: detector resolution and arm tolerances

Null method

1. Detector resolution. A galvanometer cannot show a current smaller than its resolution \(I_{g,min}\). Any \(\delta R\) that produces less than that goes unseen. Invert the detector-current formula of the previous slide for an equal-arm bridge:

Resolution limit of a null bridge (equal arms) $$\frac{u_R}{R}=\frac{4\,I_{g,min}\,(R+R_g)}{E_i}$$

Numbers: \(R=100\ \Omega\), \(R_g=50\ \Omega\), \(I_{g,min}=0.1\ \mu\)A, \(E_i=2\) V give \(u_R/R=3.0\times10^{-5}\), so \(u_R=0.0030\ \Omega\), or 0.008 °C for the RTD of the last example. A higher \(E_i\) shrinks it, at the price of \(E_i^2/4R\) of self-heating (0.01 W at 2 V).

2. Arm tolerances. The result is \(R_1=R_2R_3/R_4\), a product and quotient. The uncertainty-analysis lectures show that relative uncertainties of such a result combine in quadrature:

Propagated uncertainty of the balance result $$\frac{u_{R_1}}{R_1}=\sqrt{\Big(\frac{u_{R_2}}{R_2}\Big)^2+\Big(\frac{u_{R_3}}{R_3}\Big)^2+\Big(\frac{u_{R_4}}{R_4}\Big)^2}$$

With 0.1 % resistors: \(\sqrt3\times0.1=0.17\ \%\), i.e. 0.17 Ω or about 0.4 °C, nearly sixty times the detector limit.

Deflection method

Here \(\delta R/R\approx4E_o/E_i\), so the relative uncertainties of the output reading and of the excitation both pass straight through:

Deflection bridge, small changes $$\frac{u_{\delta R}}{\delta R}\approx\sqrt{\Big(\frac{u_{E_o}}{E_o}\Big)^2+\Big(\frac{u_{E_i}}{E_i}\Big)^2}$$

Add the nonlinearity if you use the \(\tfrac14\) rule instead of the exact equation, and the meter loading of slide 9 if \(R_m\) is not much larger than \(R_e\).

In a null bridge the accuracy is set by the calibrated arms, not by the detector. In a deflection bridge it is set by the voltmeter and the excitation.

A null bridge uses arms known to ±0.05 % and a detector that resolves \(2\times10^{-6}\) of R. Which improvement helps most?

Loading errors

A meter across a divider becomes part of the divider

Loading error: the difference between the value of the measurand and the indicated value that is caused by the act of measuring. It can be thermal (a cold thermometer cools the liquid it is dipped in), mechanical (a heavy accelerometer changes the vibration of a light panel) or electrical. Null methods make it negligible; deflection methods always take some energy and need checking.

Take a potentiometer of total resistance \(R_T\) with supply \(E_i\). The wiper splits it into \(R_1=xR_T\) (across the output) and \(R_2=(1-x)R_T\). Ideally \(E_o/E_i=x\). Now connect a meter of resistance \(R_m\) across \(R_1\):

  1. \(R_1\) and \(R_m\) in parallel: \(R_L=\dfrac{R_1R_m}{R_1+R_m}\).
  2. The divider is now \(R_2\) over \(R_L\): \(\dfrac{E_o}{E_i}=\dfrac{R_L}{R_2+R_L}=\dfrac{1}{1+(R_2/R_1)(1+R_1/R_m)}\).
  3. In terms of \(x\): \(\dfrac{E_o}{E_i}=\dfrac{x}{1+x(1-x)\,R_T/R_m}\). As \(R_m\to\infty\) it returns to \(x\).
  4. Loading error \(e_I=E_o-xE_i\approx-E_i\,x^2(1-x)\,R_T/R_m\) for \(R_m\gg R_T\). It is zero at both ends and largest at \(x=2/3\), where it is \(-0.148\,E_iR_T/R_m\).

\(R_T=10\) kΩ, \(E_i=10\) V, wiper at the middle (true 5.000 V). Meter of 10 kΩ: 4.000 V (−20 %). 100 kΩ: 4.878 V (−2.4 %). 1 MΩ: 4.988 V (−0.25 %). A meter needs to be about 100 times the circuit resistance for errors near 1 % or below.

Loading between instruments

Every stage is a source with an output impedance driving a load

The output impedance \(Z_1\) of a device is its Thévenin series impedance: how much its output voltage sags per ampere drawn. The input impedance \(Z_m\) of the next device is the impedance it presents across its input terminals. Impedance is resistance generalised to AC (it also includes the effect of capacitance and inductance); for DC signals read it as resistance.

Checking tyre pressure lets a little air out. A good gauge has a tiny volume so the puff is negligible. A high input impedance is the electrical version of that small gauge volume.

Voltage sensed (a). The loop current is \(I=E_1/(Z_1+Z_m)\), so the voltage across \(Z_m\) is

Voltage passed to the next stage $$E_m=IZ_m=\frac{E_1}{1+Z_1/Z_m}$$ $$e_I=E_m-E_1=E_1\Big(\frac{1}{1+Z_1/Z_m}-1\Big)$$

Want \(Z_m\gg Z_1\). \(Z_m=100\,Z_1\) gives \(-0.99\ \%\); \(Z_m=1000\,Z_1\) gives \(-0.1\ \%\).

Current sensed (b). Without the meter the loop current would be \(I'=E_1/Z_1\). With it, \(I_m=E_1/(Z_1+Z_m)\):

Current loading error $$e_I=I_m-I'=-E_1\frac{Z_m}{Z_1^2+Z_1Z_m}$$

Want \(Z_m\ll Z_1\). A 5 Ω ammeter in a 10 V, 1 kΩ loop reads 9.950 mA instead of 10.00 mA (−0.5 %).

Power transferred. \(P=E_1^2Z_m/(Z_1+Z_m)^2\) is largest when \(Z_m=Z_1\), giving \(E_1^2/4Z_1\).

The three curves on the right depend only on the ratio \(Z_m/Z_1\). The voltage curve rises toward 1 as the meter impedance grows; the current curve does the opposite; the power curve peaks at a ratio of exactly 1.

In voltage mode set Z₁ = 1 kΩ and find the smallest Zₘ that keeps the error under 1 % (about 99 kΩ). Raise Z₁ to 1 MΩ, like a pH electrode or a piezoelectric film: now even a 10 MΩ meter reads 9 % low. Switch to current mode and see that the same meter would be excellent if it were tiny. In power mode the bar is full only when Zₘ = Z₁.

Impedance matching means choosing the input impedance of each stage relative to the output impedance of the one before it, for the quantity that carries the signal: \(Z_m\gg Z_1\) for voltage, \(Z_m\ll Z_1\) for current, \(Z_m=Z_1\) for maximum power (loudspeakers, radio-frequency lines, and filters designed for equal source and load resistances).

Worked example

What does a 200 kΩ voltmeter read on this bridge?

A bridge has \(R_1=R_2=5000\ \Omega\) and \(R_3=R_4=8000\ \Omega\), supply \(E_i=50\) V of negligible internal resistance. The arms change by \(\delta R_1=+40\), \(\delta R_2=-40\), \(\delta R_3=+40\), \(\delta R_4=-40\ \Omega\). (a) Find the open-circuit output. (b) Find the reading of a voltmeter with \(R_m=200\) kΩ and the loading error. (c) What \(R_m\) keeps the error under 1 %?

Known: arm values and changes, \(E_i\), \(R_m\). Find: \(E_o'\), \(E_m\), \(e_I\), \(R_{m,min}\). Assumptions: linear resistors; ideal supply.

  1. (a) New arms: 5040, 4960, 8040, 7960 Ω. Deflection equation: \(E_o'=50\left(\dfrac{5040}{10\,000}-\dfrac{8040}{16\,000}\right)=50\,(0.50400-0.50250)=0.0750\) V.
  2. (b) Bridge output resistance: \(R_e=\dfrac{5040\times4960}{10\,000}+\dfrac{8040\times7960}{16\,000}=2499.8+3999.9=6499.7\ \Omega\approx6.50\) kΩ.
  3. Meter reading: \(E_m=E_o'\dfrac{R_m}{R_m+R_e}=0.0750\times\dfrac{200\,000}{206\,500}=0.0750\times0.9685=0.0726\) V.
  4. Loading error: \(e_I=0.0726-0.0750=-0.0024\) V, i.e. \(-3.15\ \%\). The meter reads low.
  5. (c) Need \(\dfrac{R_m}{R_m+R_e}\ge0.99\), so \(R_m\ge99R_e=99\times6500\approx644\) kΩ.
  6. What this means: a 10 MΩ digital voltmeter would load this bridge by only 0.065 %. An old 20 kΩ/V analog meter on its 1 V range (20 kΩ) would read about 25 % low.

Solving the three Kirchhoff loop equations of the bridge with the 200 kΩ meter as the detector gives \(E_m=0.07264\) V, identical to the Thévenin shortcut.

Designing a measurement chain

Walk the chain stage by stage and compare impedances

A measurement system is a chain of stages, and each joint is a source driving a load. The loading error of the whole chain is set by the ratio \(Z_{in}/Z_{out}\) at every joint. Typical values make the pattern clear:

StageTypical impedance
Strain-gauge or RTD bridge output100 Ω to a few kΩ
Thermocouple (with leads)a few Ω to a few hundred Ω
Piezoelectric sensor, pH electrode\(10^{8}\) to \(10^{12}\ \Omega\)
Digital voltmeter inputabout 10 MΩ
Op-amp input (bipolar / field-effect)\(>10^{7}\) / \(>10^{12}\ \Omega\)
Op-amp output\(<100\ \Omega\), far less with feedback

Read the table across: a 10 MΩ voltmeter is excellent on a bridge (ratio \(10^4\) or more) and useless on a pH electrode (ratio below 1).

When the next stage’s input impedance is too low, insert a buffer: a stage with very high input impedance, very low output impedance and a gain of 1. It draws almost nothing from the sensor and can drive almost anything. The op-amp voltage follower in Part E is exactly this.

A buffer is a relay runner. The weak first runner only has to hand the baton over a short distance to a fresh runner who then carries it the rest of the way, instead of dragging it all the way to the finish against the crowd.
