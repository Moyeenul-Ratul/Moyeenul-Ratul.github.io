Resistive, Capacitive and Inductive Transducers

A sliding contact, a moving plate and an iron core: three simple mechanical changes that turn a displacement into a change of resistance, capacitance or magnetic coupling. This part builds each one, derives its output, and shows where it bends away from a straight line.

Where we are

Every instrument starts with a piece of physics

Topics 01 and 02 treated an instrument as a black box with a sensitivity and a dynamic response. Now we open the box. Almost every electrical instrument is a chain of three stages: a sensor that responds to the quantity, signal conditioning that makes the response larger and cleaner, and an output stage that shows or stores it.

The measurand is the quantity you want (a position, a force, a light level). A transducer is a device that converts the measurand into an equivalent electrical signal. A passive (modulating) transducer only changes an electrical property, such as R, C or L, so you must supply an excitation voltage to read it. An active (self-generating) transducer produces its own voltage or charge from the measurand’s energy.

Parts A and B of this topic go through the main transducer families. Part A covers the three passive ones, which mostly measure displacement. Part B covers the self-generating, optical, magnetic and digital ones. Topic 04 then covers the instruments and circuits that read them.

A transducer is an interpreter at a meeting. The measurand speaks “millimetres” or “newtons”; the rest of the instrument only understands volts. A passive transducer is an interpreter who needs to be paid (an excitation supply) before saying anything; an active one works for free, powered by the speaker’s own words.

Resistive transducers · 1

The potentiometer turns position into a fraction of a voltage

A resistance potentiometer is a resistive track with a sliding contact, the wiper, fixed to the moving object. The track is either fine wire wound on a former or a continuous film of conductive plastic or carbon. It may be straight (linear displacement) or curved (angle).

Put a steady voltage \(E_i\) across the whole track of resistance \(R_p\). A uniform track drops voltage evenly along its length, so the wiper at fraction \(x\) of the travel sees

Unloaded potentiometer$$\frac{E_o}{E_i}=\frac{R_x}{R_p}=x=\frac{s}{L}\quad\text{or}\quad\frac{\theta}{\theta_{max}}$$
\(s,\ L\)wiper position and full travel, m\(\theta\)angle turned, of a maximum \(\theta_{max}\)\(R_x\)track resistance between one end and the wiper, Ω

Resolution. On a wire-wound track the wiper steps from one turn to the next, so the output moves in steps of \(E_i/N\) for N turns. 500 turns give steps of 0.2 % of full scale. Film tracks are continuous, so they have no such steps.

Limits: the wiper rubs, so it wears, adds friction and produces contact noise, and its mass limits general-purpose units to slow motion (a few hertz). The excitation is limited by heating, \(P=E_i^2/R_p\). Typical uses: position feedback, throttle and valve position, float-type level gauges, and pressure or force once a bellows or spring turns them into a displacement.

A potentiometer is a ruler that answers in volts. Walk a fraction \(x\) of the way down a hill of height \(E_i\) on an even slope and your height has dropped by \(xE_i\). The wiper reports its height on that electrical hill.

Resistive transducers · loading

Reading the wiper bends the straight line

The straight line \(E_o=xE_i\) holds only if the meter across the output draws no current. A real meter of resistance \(R_m\) sits in parallel with the lower part of the track, \(xR_p\):

  1. Lower part in parallel with the meter: \(R_{low}=\dfrac{xR_p\,R_m}{xR_p+R_m}\). Upper part: \((1-x)R_p\).
  2. Divider: \(\dfrac{E_o}{E_i}=\dfrac{R_{low}}{R_{low}+(1-x)R_p}\).
  3. Divide top and bottom by \(R_{low}\) and simplify: $$\frac{E_o}{E_i}=\frac{x}{1+x(1-x)\,R_p/R_m}$$
  4. Error as a fraction of the ideal reading: \(\dfrac{E_o-xE_i}{xE_i}\approx-x(1-x)\dfrac{R_p}{R_m}\) when \(R_m\gg R_p\). Expressed as a fraction of full scale, \(-x^2(1-x)R_p/R_m\), which peaks at \(x=2/3\).

At \(x=0\) the output is zero anyway and at \(x=1\) the meter sits straight across the supply, so the error vanishes at both ends. In between, the meter steals current through the upper part of the track and drags the wiper voltage down.

Potentiometer loadingideal xloadederror

Start at \(R_m/R_p\approx10\) and watch the orange curve sag below the line, worst near \(x=0.67\). Raise the ratio to 100: the sag shrinks tenfold. Drop it to 1 and the potentiometer becomes useless as a linear sensor.

Worked example

How much does a 10 kΩ meter disturb a 150 Ω potentiometer?

A displacement potentiometer has a total resistance of 150 Ω and is supplied with 100 V. Its wiper voltage is read with a meter of internal resistance 10 000 Ω. Find the loading error and the voltage actually read when the wiper is at 25 % and at 75 % of its travel.

Known: \(R_p=150\ \Omega\), \(E_i=100\) V, \(R_m=10\,000\ \Omega\), \(x=0.25\) and \(0.75\). Find: error, indicated \(E_o\). Assumptions: uniform track; meter is a pure resistance.

  1. Resistance ratio: \(R_p/R_m=150/10\,000=0.015\).
  2. At \(x=0.25\): \(x(1-x)R_p/R_m=0.25\times0.75\times0.015=0.002813\). Loaded output \(E_o=100\times0.25/1.002813=24.93\) V instead of 25.00 V.
  3. Error relative to the ideal reading: \(24.930/25.000-1=-0.28\ \%\).
  4. At \(x=0.75\): \(x(1-x)\) is again \(0.1875\), so the relative error is the same, −0.28 %. \(E_o=75/1.002813=74.79\) V instead of 75.00 V.
  5. What this means: even a meter only 67 times the track resistance costs less than 0.3 %. The error is a deviation from the straight line, so a calibration done with the same meter attached would remove it.

The same potentiometer is read with a 1 MΩ digital meter. The relative error at mid-travel becomes about…

A low-resistance track makes loading small but heats up: 100 V across 150 Ω dissipates 67 W, far beyond a small potentiometer’s rating of a watt or two. Choosing \(R_p\) is a compromise between loading (want small \(R_p\)) and self-heating (want large \(R_p\)).

Resistive transducers · 2

Stretch it or warm it and R changes too

A conductor of resistivity \(\rho\), length \(L\) and area \(A\) has \(R=\rho L/A\). Taking logarithms and differentiating,

Fractional change of resistance$$\frac{dR}{R}=\frac{d\rho}{\rho}+\frac{dL}{L}-\frac{dA}{A}=\frac{d\rho}{\rho}+(1+2\nu)\,\varepsilon$$

since a wire stretched by strain \(\varepsilon=dL/L\) thins by \(dA/A=-2\nu\varepsilon\) (\(\nu\), Poisson’s ratio). A strain gauge packs this into one number, the gauge factor \(F=(dR/R)/\varepsilon\approx2\): a 120 Ω gauge at \(\varepsilon=1000\times10^{-6}\) changes by only 0.24 Ω. A resistance thermometer (RTD) uses \(\rho(T)\): platinum follows \(R=R_0[1+\alpha(T-T_0)]\), so 100 Ω at 0 °C with \(\alpha=0.00385\ ^\circ\)C⁻¹ reads 119.25 Ω at 50 °C. A thermistor is a semiconductor whose R falls steeply and nonlinearly with T. The lectures on temperature and strain measurement treat these in depth.

The simplest way to read any of them is the ballast circuit: the sensor R in series with a fixed resistor \(R_i\) across \(E_i\), reading the voltage across the sensor, \(E=E_iR/(R+R_i)\).

  1. Sensitivity to the sensor’s resistance: \(S=\dfrac{dE}{dR}=\dfrac{E_iR_i}{(R+R_i)^2}\).
  2. Which \(R_i\) gives the largest S? \(\dfrac{dS}{dR_i}=E_i\dfrac{R-R_i}{(R+R_i)^3}=0\Rightarrow R_i=R\).
  3. Worked numbers. A 150 Ω transducer, 100 V supply, best sensitivity wanted at mid-range (\(R=75\ \Omega\)): choose \(R_i=75\ \Omega\). Then at mid-range \(S=100\times75/150^2=0.333\) V/Ω.
  4. At 25 % (\(R=37.5\ \Omega\)): \(S=7500/112.5^2=0.593\) V/Ω. At 75 % (\(R=112.5\ \Omega\)): \(S=7500/187.5^2=0.213\) V/Ω.
  5. What this means: the ballast output is nonlinear (sensitivity falls by almost 3:1 over the range), and the full supply voltage sits under the small change. That is why precise work uses a bridge (topic 04, part B), which subtracts the large steady part.

Inductive transducers · 1

Move iron near a coil and its inductance changes

A current \(i\) in a coil of N turns drives a magnetic flux \(\Phi=Ni/\mathcal{R}\) around a magnetic path. \(\mathcal{R}\), the reluctance, plays the role of resistance for flux: \(\mathcal{R}=l/(\mu A)\) for a path of length \(l\), area \(A\) and permeability \(\mu\). Flux linkage per ampere is the inductance:

Inductance of a coil on a magnetic path$$L=\frac{N\Phi}{i}=\frac{N^2}{\mathcal{R}}$$

Iron has a permeability thousands of times that of air, so an air gap \(g\) dominates the path: \(\mathcal{R}\approx2g/(\mu_0A)\) for the two gaps in the drawing, and \(L\approx\mu_0N^2A/(2g)\). Closing the gap raises L as \(1/g\), the same curved law as a capacitor’s gap (later in this part). A variable-reluctance sensor reads that change of L in an AC bridge.

With a permanent magnet in the path the coil needs no supply: steel gear teeth sweeping past change the flux, and Faraday’s law \(e=-N\,d\Phi/dt\) gives one voltage pulse per tooth. Counting pulses per second gives shaft speed (a variable-reluctance pickup).

An eddy-current proximity probe works on any conducting target, magnetic or not. Its coil carries a high-frequency current; the field induces circulating (eddy) currents in the nearby metal, whose own field opposes the coil’s, lowering its effective inductance and raising its losses. The closer the target, the stronger the effect. Electronics turn this into a voltage, a common industrial calibration being 7.87 V/mm (200 mV per thousandth of an inch). Non-contact and unaffected by oil, these probes watch shaft vibration and position in turbines and compressors.

Inductive transducers · 2

The LVDT: one primary, two opposed secondaries, a sliding core

Two equal buckets under one tap, connected so you weigh their difference. With the tap (the core) centred the difference is zero. Shift the tap and one bucket gains what the other loses, so the difference grows twice as fast as either change alone, and its sign tells you which way you moved.

The linear variable differential transformer (LVDT) is a transformer whose coupling depends on core position. Derive its output:

  1. The primary, resistance \(R_p\) and inductance \(L_p\), carries \(I_p=E_i/(R_p+j\omega L_p)\) from an AC excitation of angular frequency \(\omega\).
  2. Each secondary picks up \(e_1=M_1\,dI_p/dt\) and \(e_2=M_2\,dI_p/dt\), with \(M_1,M_2\) the mutual inductances (volts induced per ampere-per-second).
  3. Wired in opposition, \(E_o=e_1-e_2=(M_1-M_2)\,dI_p/dt\), so the amplitude is \(\lvert E_o\rvert=\omega\lvert M_1-M_2\rvert\lvert I_p\rvert\).
  4. By symmetry \(M_1=M_2\) at the centre. For small x, one rises and the other falls by the same amount, \(M_1-M_2\approx2k\,x\): $$\lvert E_o\rvert\approx2k\,\omega\lvert I_p\rvert\,\lvert x\rvert$$
  5. The sign of \(M_1-M_2\) flips as the core crosses the centre, so the output is in phase with the excitation on one side and 180° out of phase on the other.

A phase-sensitive demodulator compares the output with the excitation and gives a DC voltage whose sign follows the side. At the centre a small null voltage remains, from unequal coils, stray capacitance and harmonics of the excitation.

Worked example · LVDT in practice

From a V-graph to a displacement

Ranges from about ±0.1 mm to ±75 mm; nonlinearity about 0.5 % of range; output 16 to 160 mV per mm of travel per volt of excitation, depending on excitation frequency; low output resistance. The excitation frequency should be at least ten times the highest frequency of core motion.

The LVDT whose V-graph is drawn gives 1.00 V rms at ±1.52 mm. (a) What is its sensitivity? (b) The demodulator shows 0.40 V in phase with the excitation, then later 0.40 V in opposite phase. Where is the core each time? (c) What displacement is hidden in the 3 mV null? (d) It is excited at 2.5 kHz. Can it follow a 1 kHz vibration?

  1. (a) Slope of the linear part: \(K=1.00\ \text{V}/1.52\ \text{mm}=0.656\) V/mm.
  2. (b) \(x=0.40/0.656=0.61\) mm. In phase means the side marked “in phase”: \(x=+0.61\) mm; opposite phase: \(x=-0.61\) mm.
  3. (c) \(3\ \text{mV}/0.656\ \text{V/mm}=0.0046\) mm \(=4.6\ \mu\)m. Near the centre, displacements smaller than about 5 µm cannot be read from the magnitude.
  4. (d) No. The rule “excitation at least ten times the motion frequency” allows motion up to about 250 Hz. Each excitation cycle is one look at the core, and a demodulator needs several looks per motion cycle.
  5. What this means: an LVDT is frictionless (the core never touches the coils), has no wiper steps, and is electrically isolated from the moving part, which is why it is the standard laboratory displacement transducer. Its price is AC excitation and demodulating electronics.

Capacitive transducers · 1

Three handles on one formula: gap, area and dielectric

Put charge \(+Q\) and \(-Q\) on two parallel plates of overlap area \(A\), a distance \(d\) apart. The field between them is uniform, \(E=Q/(\varepsilon A)\), so the voltage is \(V=Ed=Qd/(\varepsilon A)\). The ratio \(Q/V\) is the capacitance:

Parallel-plate capacitance$$C=\frac{\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0A}{d}$$
\(\varepsilon_0\)permittivity of vacuum, \(8.854\times10^{-12}\) F/m\(\varepsilon_r\)dielectric constant: 1.0006 for air, about 3 for plastics\(A,\ d\)overlap area (m²) and gap (m)

In laboratory units: \(C=0.0885\,\varepsilon_rA/d\) pF with A in cm² and d in cm. Plates of 10 mm diameter with a 0.1 mm air gap give only 6.95 pF.

Differentiate with respect to each handle to get the sensitivity:

Sensitivities$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial d}=-\frac{C}{d},\qquad\frac{\partial C}{\partial A}=\frac{C}{A},\qquad\frac{\partial C}{\partial\varepsilon_r}=\frac{C}{\varepsilon_r}$$

Area and dielectric enter C linearly, so those sensors are linear. The gap is in the denominator, so a gap sensor follows \(1/d\): closing the gap by 10 % raises C by 11.1 %, and by 20 % raises it by 25 %. Small gaps give high sensitivity but a curved calibration.

Level gauge. For two coaxial tubes of radii \(a\) and \(b\) and length \(L\), with liquid of dielectric constant \(\varepsilon_r\) up to height \(h\), the wet and dry lengths are capacitors in parallel: \(C=\dfrac{2\pi\varepsilon_0[\varepsilon_rh+(L-h)]}{\ln(b/a)}\), linear in h. With \(b/a=2\) that is 80.3 pF per metre of air, and twice that per metre of a liquid with \(\varepsilon_r=2\) such as kerosene.

A capacitor is a sponge for charge squeezed between two walls. Bigger walls (area) or a more absorbent filling (dielectric) hold more, in proportion. Pushing the walls closer also holds more, but the gain runs away as the gap closes, like a spring that stiffens as you compress it.

Capacitive transducers · 2

Two gaps that change in opposite directions cancel the curvature

Put a movable plate midway between two fixed plates, each gap \(d\). Move the middle plate by \(x\) toward plate 1: \(C_1=\varepsilon A/(d-x)\) and \(C_2=\varepsilon A/(d+x)\). Both are curved in x, but their difference ratio is not.

  1. Connect \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) in series across an AC supply \(E_i\), and compare their middle node with a fixed half-supply reference (an AC bridge). Capacitive reactance is \(1/(\omega C)\), so the voltage across \(C_2\) is \(E_i\,\dfrac{1/C_2}{1/C_1+1/C_2}=E_i\dfrac{C_1}{C_1+C_2}\).
  2. Output: \(E_o=E_i\left(\dfrac{C_1}{C_1+C_2}-\dfrac12\right)=\dfrac{E_i}{2}\,\dfrac{C_1-C_2}{C_1+C_2}\).
  3. Substitute: \(C_1-C_2=\dfrac{2\varepsilon Ax}{d^2-x^2}\) and \(C_1+C_2=\dfrac{2\varepsilon Ad}{d^2-x^2}\). The common factor cancels: $$E_o=\frac{E_i}{2}\,\frac{x}{d}$$
  4. Exactly linear in x, independent of \(\varepsilon\) and A, and twice as sensitive as one gap at small x. The same push-pull idea is used in MEMS accelerometers (Part B).

Capacitive sensors have very high output impedance, \(Z=1/(2\pi fC)\). A 22.5 pF sensor excited at 10 kHz has \(Z\approx707\) kΩ, so any meter or cable connected to it loads it heavily. A metre of ordinary cable adds around 100 pF in parallel, several times the sensor itself, and that cable capacitance changes when the cable flexes or warms. Practical sensors keep the electronics close, use driven guard electrodes, and run at high excitation frequency.

The plot shows the sensor’s normalised output against normalised displacement \(u\). For the single gap the output is \(\Delta C/C_0=u/(1-u)\), with \(u\) the fraction by which the gap closes. For sliding plates it is \(\Delta A/A_0=u\). For the differential pair in its bridge it is \(E_o/(E_i/2)=u\).

Pick “single gap” and move u to +0.3: the output is 0.43, 43 % above the straight line. Move to −0.3 (gap opening): the error changes sign. Switch to the differential pair: the curve lies on the line at every u. Finally pick “area”: linear too, but the sensitivity per unit displacement is set by plate width, not by a tiny gap, so it is usually lower.

Use a single gap for small motions where the high sensitivity matters (microphones, proximity probes), area change for longer linear or angular travel, and a differential pair when you need both sensitivity and linearity.

Worked example

Sensitivity, and how well must C be measured?

A capacitive displacement transducer has two square plates of area 645.2 mm² (1 in²) separated by an air gap of 0.254 mm (0.01 in); for air \(\varepsilon_r=1.0006\). (a) Find its capacitance and displacement sensitivity. (b) The gap must be inferred within ±0.00254 mm (±0.0001 in), and the plate area is only known within ±3.23 mm² (±0.005 in²). How accurately must C be measured?

Assumptions: parallel-plate formula (gap much smaller than plate width, so fringing is negligible); uncertainties independent, combined by root-sum-square.

  1. (a) \(C=\dfrac{\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0A}{d}=\dfrac{1.0006\times8.854\times10^{-12}\times6.452\times10^{-4}}{2.54\times10^{-4}}=22.50\ \text{pF}\).
  2. Sensitivity \(S=\partial C/\partial d=-C/d=-22.50/0.254=-88.6\) pF/mm, about −2250 pF per inch. A 1 µm change of gap changes C by 0.089 pF.
  3. (b) The gap is computed from the measurements as \(d=\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0A/C\), a product of powers. Its relative uncertainty combines the relative uncertainties of A and C: \(\left(\dfrac{w_d}{d}\right)^2=\left(\dfrac{w_A}{A}\right)^2+\left(\dfrac{w_C}{C}\right)^2\).
  4. Allowed \(w_d/d=0.00254/0.254=0.010\); known \(w_A/A=3.23/645.2=0.0050\). So \(w_C/C=\sqrt{0.010^2-0.005^2}=0.00866\).
  5. \(w_C=0.00866\times22.50=\pm0.195\) pF.
  6. What this means: you must measure about 22.5 pF to within 0.2 pF, which is less than the capacitance of a few centimetres of cable. The electronics must sit at the sensor or the cable must be guarded.

For a result \(d=kA^aC^b\), each relative uncertainty enters multiplied by its exponent: \((w_d/d)^2=(a\,w_A/A)^2+(b\,w_C/C)^2\). Here \(a=1\) and \(b=-1\), so both enter with weight 1. The tighter the area tolerance, the more of the uncertainty budget is left for the capacitance measurement.

Quick check: if the plate area were known exactly, how well would C need to be measured?

Then \(w_C/C=w_d/d=1.0\ \%\), so \(w_C=\pm0.225\) pF. Halving the area uncertainty from 0.5 % to zero only relaxes the capacitance requirement from 0.87 % to 1.0 %, because uncertainties add in quadrature.

An uncertainty budget is a shared bill. The total you may spend is fixed (1 % on the gap). Whatever the area already costs is paid first, in quadrature, and the capacitance measurement gets what is left.
